## 1. Band-plan customs & sideband conventions

*The invisible zoning map every HF operator carries in their head — custom, not law, and load-bearing.*

- FACT — The voice sideband convention: **LSB below 10 MHz (160/75/40 m), USB at 10 MHz and above** — 20, 17, 15, 12, 10 m, and VHF/UHF SSB too. The ARRL/IARU ethics manual states it flatly: "SSB transmissions below 10 MHz are done on LSB, above 10 MHz on USB." It is commonly accepted practice, not regulation or physics (pool G2A01, G2A02, G2A03, G2A04, G2A09). `[pool-supported: G2A01, G2A02, G2A03, G2A04, G2A09]`
- FACT — The one band where sideband IS law: **60 m is USB-only by FCC rule**, max 2.8 kHz bandwidth, on channelized assignments (5330.5, 5346.5, 5371.5, 5403.5 kHz plus the 5351.5–5366.5 kHz segment) — the ARRL band plan lists "USB phone" for each (pool G1C03; arrl.org/band-plan). Neat answer to the sharp student who asks "so what's used on 60 m, which is below 10 MHz?" `[pool-supported: G2A09]` `[practice]`
- FACT — HF voice is single sideband, period (pool G2A05): one sideband, carrier and other sideband suppressed (G2A07), which buys less bandwidth and better power efficiency than other analog voice modes (G2A06) — and makes SSB the narrowest phone emission (G8A07). The crowded-band payoff: a 20 m phone segment holds roughly five times as many SSB stations as it would AM. `[pool-supported: G2A05, G2A06, G2A07, G8A07]`
- FACT — Your dial reads the suppressed-carrier frequency; speech energy spreads ~3 kHz to one side — above the dial on USB, below it on LSB. The ethics manual's band-edge rule of thumb: no LSB with the dial below 3.603 MHz on 80 m, no USB with the dial above 14.347 MHz on 20 m. Same reason the pool's minimum SSB separation is 2–3 kHz (G2B05), while CW neighbors can live 150–500 Hz apart (G2B04). (Ethics doc §II.8.1) `[pool-supported: G2B04, G2B05]` `[practice]`
- FACT — Where the modes live on 20 m, per the ARRL band plan: **14.070–14.095 RTTY, 14.095–14.0995 packet, 14.100 NCDXF beacons, 14.1005–14.112 packet, 14.230 SSTV, 14.286 AM calling**. "Most digital operations on 20 m are in 14.070–14.100 MHz" is pool G2E08 verbatim. (arrl.org/band-plan) `[pool-supported: G2E08]`
- FACT — The NCDXF/IARU international beacon network operates on **14.100, 18.110, 21.150, 24.930, and 28.200 MHz** (arrl.org/considerate-operator). Tuning across the five beacons is the oldest "is the band open, and to where?" check in HF — supports the propagation-check material r3 teaches for G3B. `[practice]`
- FACT — AM isn't dead on HF; it has calling frequencies by custom: **3.885, 7.290, and 14.286 MHz** (arrl.org/band-plan). A new General tuning 3.885 kHz on a winter evening hears full-carrier double-sideband AM — a live demo for the G8A modulation-envelope material (G8A05, G8A11), and a station to compare against SSB's efficiency (G2A06). `[practice]` `[pool-supported: G8A05, G8A11]`
- FACT — 6 m DX window: **50.100–50.125 MHz** (arrl.org/band-plan: "50.10–50.125 DX window"), where by band-plan custom US-48 stations work only stations OUTSIDE the 48 contiguous states (pool G2B08). Voluntary, not FCC law — same gentleman's-agreement mechanics as the HF DX windows in §3. `[pool-supported: G2B08]`
- FACT — Nobody owns a frequency. The ARRL Considerate Operator page quotes 47 CFR 97.101(b) verbatim — "No frequency will be assigned for the exclusive use of any station" — and adds: "No one 'owns' a frequency." That is pool G2B01 in the wild: nets, contests, and ragchews all yield by cooperation (G2B03) and the voluntary band plan (G2B07), not by seniority. `[pool-supported: G2B01, G2B03, G2B07]`

## 2. Everyday HF QSO craft

*What a polite, effective phone contact actually sounds like.*

- FACT — The CQ pattern the pool tests (G2D05): "CQ" a few times, "this is," your call a few times, pause, listen, repeat. Ethics-manual coaching that makes it sound like an operator: several short CQs beat one long one, give the call two to four times with phonetics once or twice (G2D07), and never end a CQ with "over" or "QRZ" — over to whom? Nobody was calling you yet. (Ethics doc §II.8.1) `[pool-supported: G2D05, G2D07]` `[practice]`
- Answering a CQ: the other station's call once at most, then yours — in busy conditions many skip the caller's call entirely ("he knows his own call"). Breaking into a QSO in progress: your call sign once, dropped into the pause between transmissions (pool G2A08). `[pool-supported: G2A08]` `[practice]`
- FACT — Phone reports are **RS — readability 1–5, strength 1–9**; CW adds **T (tone) 1–9**, and a "C" suffix means a chirpy, unstable signal (pool G2C07). Reports go FIRST in an exchange so each station can adapt to conditions (pool G2D11). Outside contests an honest report is normal and useful: "you're 5 by 3 but solid copy through the QSB." `[pool-supported: G2C07, G2D11]`
- FACT — "CQ DX" from the contiguous 48 invites answers from stations OUTSIDE the lower 48 (pool G2A11). Courtesy corollary from the ethics manual: if a local answers your CQ DX anyway, give him the quick contact — you may be a new one for him too. `[pool-supported: G2A11]` `[practice]`
- The pre-CQ ritual: listen, then ask — "Is this frequency in use? This is W1ZZZ" on phone, "QRL?" plus your call on CW (pool G2B06, G2C04). The skip-zone reason asking matters: a station a few hundred km away can be inaudible to you yet mid-QSO on that frequency — his contact can hear you and answer. `[pool-supported: G2B06, G2C04]` `[practice]`
- VOX = hands-free T/R switching by voice (pool G2A10): loved by net controls and contesters for pace; PTT is the safer default in a noisy shack — the dog barking at the wrong moment keys the transmitter. `[pool-supported: G2A10]` `[practice]`
- FACT — ALC in one breath: set mic gain / transmit audio so ALC just begins to work on peaks (pool G2A12). Past that point you are flat-topping the envelope — distortion from excessive drive or speech levels (G8A10) — splattering into excessive bandwidth (G8A08), and on someone's waterfall your signal sprouts the vertical side-lines of overmodulation (G8C13). One knob links four questions across two subelements. `[pool-supported: G2A12, G8A08, G8A10, G8C13]`
- Ragchew vs rubber-stamp: the stereotype HF QSO is call signs, report, name, QTH, rig/antenna, weather, 73; long ragchews live mostly in 75/40 m evening nets and slower hours, while DX and contest contacts are minimal by custom. The station who called CQ sets the tone — if he wants short, keep it short. (Ethics doc §II.8.4) `[practice]`
- Phonetics discipline: the NATO alphabet (G2D07) is the DX standard; cute alternates ("Kilowatt," "Radio," "Golf-three-Zanzibar") confuse non-English speakers exactly when you need clarity — the ethics manual explicitly warns against fantasy phonetics and against mixing word sets mid-call. `[pool-supported: G2D07]` `[practice]`

## 3. DXing

*Pileups, split, DXpeditions, and the twilight bonus.*

- FACT — Split operation: when a rare-DX station announces "listening 5 to 10 up," he transmits on his frequency and listens 5–10 kHz higher. You call on his listening frequency — never on his transmit frequency, and you say your own call, not his. The ethics manual's caution: before working split yourself, check that BOTH frequencies are clear. (Ethics doc §II.8.1; split pileups §III.1.6–III.1.7) `[practice]`
- Pileup manners (Ethics doc §III.1): call exactly as the DX finishes the previous contact, full call once, then listen. The DX often comes back to a partial — "the station ending Alpha-Bravo, again?" — and only matching stations call again. Never call over an ongoing QSO; never tail-end into a split pileup on the DX's own frequency. `[practice]`
- FACT — Long path = point the beam **180 degrees from the short-path heading** (pool G2D06). When it's worth doing: gray-line windows — enhanced low-band propagation along Earth's day/night terminator, roughly ±30 minutes around sunrise/sunset on 160/80 m and up to about an hour on 40 m (QST, Nov 1992, "An introduction to gray-line DXing"; Monitoring Times, Dec 1991). Both ends of the path ride the terminator at once — your sunrise is their sunset. `[pool-supported: G2D06]` `[practice]`
- The DXer's map: an azimuthal projection centered on your own QTH shows true bearings and distances (pool G2D04) — because great-circle intuition fails beginners: from the US Midwest, Europe is northeast and Japan is northwest over the pole, not "east across the Atlantic / west across the Pacific." `[pool-supported: G2D04]` `[practice]`
- FACT — DXpeditions publish operating plans (bands, modes, split ranges) on their own sites and get spotted on DX clusters. Modern FT8 DXpeditions run **Fox/Hound**: the DX is the "fox" working many "hounds" at once; WSJT-X 2.7's SuperFox mode transmits to as many as 9 hounds simultaneously with about +10 dB system gain over the original 5-slot Fox mode (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide, "New in Version 2.7"). Hounds need current WSJT-X with the hound role enabled. `[practice]`
- DX clusters/spotting: operators post "spots" — call, frequency, time, comment — to telnet/web networks; the modern answer to "where's the DX today?" Ethics-manual rule: spot the DX, not yourself; self-spotting is frowned on outside the contests that permit it. (Ethics doc §III.6) `[practice]`
- DX windows inside band plans are stay-clear segments where locals keep quiet so weak DX can be heard: the 6 m window of G2B08, plus classics like 3.790–3.800 MHz on 80 m in the ARRL band plan. Same voluntary machinery everywhere: custom, enforced socially. `[pool-supported: G2B08]` `[practice]`
- QRP = low-power transmit operation (pool G2D10); by long-standing club and award custom that's ~5 W (10 W for SSB in some award rules) — a custom, not a regulation (see Watch item 9). Sweepstakes literally has a QRP entry class (local: rules.ts ss entry). CW custom: appending "/QRP" to a CQ is normal; on phone, say "QRP" as a word, not as a phonetic suffix. `[pool-supported: G2D10]` `[practice]`
- Volunteer Monitor color (pool G2D01–G2D03): the VM program is a formal FCC–ARRL agreement — trained, vetted volunteers who monitor for rules violations so the service self-regulates (G2D02); it replaced the old Official Observer program in 2019 (arrl.org/volunteer-monitor-program; ARRL news, Sept 2019). A VM notice is coaching, not a pink ticket. `[pool-supported: G2D01, G2D02]` `[practice]`

## 4. Contests & operating events

*Weekend-long crowd scenes with their own grammar.*

- FACT — A contest exchange is the minimum that scores: call sign, signal report, plus whatever the sponsor defines — serial number, state/province, grid square, ITU zone. Contest rules never override FCC identification rules: normal Part 97 ID applies mid-contest (pool G2D09). `[pool-supported: G2D09]`
- FACT — Real ARRL exchange formats (local: `~/arrl-calendar/src/data/rules.ts`, each entry carrying its official arrl.org rules URL): ARRL DX — W/VE send RST + state/province, DX sends RST + transmitter power; November Sweepstakes — serial number, precedence code, your call, "check" (2-digit year first licensed), ARRL/RAC section; Field Day — transmitter class + section ("3A Ohio"); IARU HF — RST + ITU zone; RTTY Roundup — W/VE RST + state/province, DX RST + serial; International Digital — 4-character grid square. `[practice]`
- The default contest report is "59" on phone / "599" on CW, sent regardless of actual conditions — the ethics manual's worked contest examples run exactly so ("G3XXX 59001"). The pool's reason for exchanging reports (adapt to conditions, G2D11) is ragchew practice; in contests the report is ritual. See Watch item 5. `[pool-supported: G2D11]` `[practice]`
- FACT — The rhythm of the year (arrl.org/contest-calendar; rules.ts): January RTTY Roundup + VHF; February/March ARRL DX (CW, then phone); June International Digital + Field Day (4th full weekend); July IARU HF; November Sweepstakes CW/phone; December 160 m + 10 m. The ethics manual counts 200+ contests a year worldwide, about 20 majors. `[practice]`
- FACT — **No contests on the WARC bands (30/17/12 m)** — a long-standing truce because those bands are narrow; contesting would render them unusable for everyone else (Ethics doc §II.8.6). Practical upshot for a new General: 17 m stays civil on the biggest weekends. `[practice]`
- State QSO parties: in-state stations send their county, out-of-state stations send state/province (K8ZT Contest University deck; stateqsoparty.com runs the year-long State QSO Party Challenge scored via 3830scores.com). Beginner-friendly: slower speeds, forgiving ops, and one log can score several simultaneous parties — 7QP, Indiana, Delaware, and New England share a weekend and accept a single Cabrillo file (ARRL news, Apr 2021). `[practice]`
- Contest voice craft (Ethics doc §II.8.6): end your CQ with the word "contest" ("W1ZZZ contest"), answer calls with the exchange only — no "please copy," no pleasantries — and close each contact "thanks W1ZZZ contest," which signs off, IDs, and re-calls CQ in three words. "Running" = holding a frequency calling CQ; "search and pounce" = tuning for the stations you need. `[practice]`
- FACT — CW contest speed trick: **cut numbers** — letters standing in for number digits (N for 9, T for 0), so "599" goes "5NN" and a serial "101" may arrive "A TT"-style — documented in the ethics manual (§II.9.22, "Abbreviated numbers (cut numbers) used in contests"). A new General hearing "5NN" in Sweepstakes knows it's shorthand, not a mystery prosign. `[practice]`
- FACT — W1AW, the ARRL headquarters station, sends daily code practice from 5 to 35 WPM plus CW/digital/voice bulletins (local: rules.ts w1aw entry) — the free on-air CW school behind the G2C material. Straight Key Night runs every January 1, non-competitive, send "SKN" in your report (rules.ts skn entry). `[practice]` `[pool-supported: G2C02]`

## 5. Digital-modes practice

*Where the waterfall replaces the ear.*

- FACT — **FT8 watering holes, all USB:** 160 m 1.840, 80 m 3.573, 40 m 7.074, 30 m 10.136, 20 m 14.074, 17 m 18.100, 15 m 21.074, 12 m 24.915, 10 m 28.074 MHz (ARRL On The Air magazine, "Discover the Excitement of FT8," Table 1 — the pool's G2E15 pins 14.074–14.077). 6 m 50.313 MHz (50.323 for intercontinental DX); 60 m 5.357 MHz (OnAllBands/DX Engineering FT8 table). `[pool-supported: G2E15]`
- FACT — The opposite-conventions pair the pool tests twice: **AFSK RTTY → LSB** (G2E01) versus **JT65/JT9/FT4/FT8 → USB** (G2E05) — USB on EVERY band, including 80/40 m where voice is LSB. The WSJT-X manual is explicit: "WSJT-X uses upper sideband mode for both transmitting and receiving"; ARRL's OTA article instructs "switch your radio to upper sideband (USB)" for FT8. See Watch item 1. `[pool-supported: G2E01, G2E05]`
- FACT — FT8 clock discipline: computer time accurate to ~1 second (pool G2E07). ARRL's OTA article says within 2 seconds, "the closer the better," and recommends an always-running sync tool because built-in OS time sync drifts. Signature symptom of a bad clock: waterfall full of signals, zero decodes. `[pool-supported: G2E07]`
- FACT — The six-message FT8 script, verbatim from ARRL's OTA article: `CQ WB8IMY FN31` → `WB8IMY N1NAS EN72` → `N1NAS WB8IMY -11` → `WB8IMY N1NAS R-15` → `N1NAS WB8IMY RRR` → `WB8IMY N1NAS 73`. Reports are SNR in dB in a 2.5 kHz bandwidth (pool G8C15), so "-11" is a fine contact — decoding deep in the noise is the mode's reason to exist (G8C07). A contact can finish in under 90 seconds. `[pool-supported: G8C07, G8C15]`
- FACT — Answering an FT8 CQ: find a clear audio offset in the ALTERNATE time slot (pool G2E04) — dozens of stations share one dial frequency at different audio pitches across the waterfall, alternating 15-second transmit/receive slots. Calling on the caller's own offset in his slot just collides with him. `[pool-supported: G2E04]`
- FACT — Why WSJT-X wants Split Operation set to "Rig" or "Fake It": it keeps transmit audio in the 1500–2000 Hz range so audio harmonics can't pass the transmitter's sideband filter — a measurably cleaner signal (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §4.2). `[practice]`
- FACT — FT8 is 8-tone FSK (pool G8A09) on 15-second T/R sequences; its siblings pace differently: FT4 at 7.5 s built for contesting, JT65/JT9 at 1-minute sequences (a minimal QSO takes 4–6 minutes) (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1). `[pool-supported: G8A09]` `[practice]`
- FACT — RTTY on HF: most common shift **170 Hz** (pool G2E06; 850 Hz was the old commercial standard), 45.45-baud Baudot — a 5-bit code plus start and stop bits (G8C04) — its two tones named mark and space (G8C11). Properly tuned but won't decode? Suspect reversed mark/space, wrong baud rate, or wrong sideband (G2E14). `[pool-supported: G2E06, G8C04, G8C11, G2E14]`
- FACT — PSK31 hangs out at **14.070 MHz** on 20 m, and near the bottom of each band's data segment (Ethics doc §II.10.2 names 14.070; galanto.com PSK31 frequency table). PSK31's Varicode gives common letters short codes, so UPPERCASE slows you down (pool G8C12, G8C08): PSK31 old hands really do type in lowercase, and it isn't laziness. On the same watering holes you'll meet QPSK31, the 4-phase variant that adds error correction in about the same bandwidth and is sideband-sensitive — tune it with the wrong sideband selected and it won't decode (pool G8A06, "all these choices are correct"). `[pool-supported: G8C08, G8C12, G8A06]` `[practice]`
- FACT — WSPR (pool G8C02): a beacon mode sending call sign, grid, and power in 2-minute sequences, decodable to −31 dB SNR in 2500 Hz; reception reports upload to WSPRnet, which maps who heard whom (WSJT-X 2.7 User Guide §1). One evening of WSPR is the zero-effort way to compare two antennas. `[pool-supported: G8C02]` `[practice]`
- FACT — SSTV calling frequencies: **3.845, 7.171, 14.230, 21.340, 28.680 MHz** (ARRL band plan). Space bonus: the ISS downlinks SSTV on **437.550 MHz in Robot36 mode**, about two minutes between images (ariss.org "Contact the ISS," verified 2026-07-24; matches the Technician canon pin of 2026-07-23). Time-sensitive — Watch item 7. `[practice]`
- FACT — Waterfall literacy (pool G8C14): frequency horizontal, time vertical, strength as brightness; a clean data signal is a tidy rectangle, an overdriven one grows the vertical side-lines of overmodulation (G8C13). Digital operators read a whole 3 kHz window at a glance the way SSB operators scan by ear. `[pool-supported: G8C13, G8C14]`
- Duty-cycle reality (pool G8B08): FT8 and RTTY key the transmitter at ~100% for whole sequences — "some modes have high duty cycles that could exceed the transmitter's average power rating." Field practice: run digital at reduced power (many use 20–50 W, not 100) to protect the finals; weak-signal modes don't need brute force anyway. `[pool-supported: G8B08]` `[practice]`
- Filter discipline (pool G8B09): match receiver bandwidth to the mode — ~2.7–3 kHz for SSB, ~500 Hz for CW and RTTY, wide open (3–5 kHz) for the FT8 waterfall. Best signal-to-noise comes from rejecting everything that isn't the signal — and on the transmit side, higher symbol rates need wider bandwidth (G8B10), which is why 45-baud RTTY fits in a CW-width channel. `[pool-supported: G8B09, G8B10]` `[practice]`

## 6. Nets & emcomm on HF

*Directed nets that span oceans instead of a repeater's footprint.*

- FACT — **Maritime Mobile Service Network: 14.300 MHz USB, daily 12:00 PM–10:00 PM Eastern**, on the air since 1968, handling third-party traffic for maritime mobiles and relaying high-seas and tropical weather warnings; formally recognized by DHS, the US Coast Guard, and the National Weather Service (mmsn.org/about-us). 14.300 is also an IARU Global Emergency Center of Activity (GECOA) frequency — the worldwide list: 3.750, 3.985, 7.060, 7.240, 14.300, 18.160, 21.360 MHz. `[practice]`
- FACT — **Hurricane Watch Net: 14.325 MHz USB by day, 7.268 MHz LSB by night**, activating when a hurricane is within ~300 statute miles of populated landmass or at National Hurricane Center request, working alongside WX4NHC, the amateur station at the NHC; when HWN isn't active, storm information moves on 14.300 (hwn.org activation plans). `[practice]`
- Net courtesy is load-bearing, and even these nets don't OWN the frequency (pool G2B01): HWN's own page says that before activating, if the frequency is in use, "we always ask permission to use them." Backup-frequency discipline — have an alternate ready for interference or poor conditions (G2B10) — is standard net-management practice. `[pool-supported: G2B01, G2B10]`
- NTS traffic nets run 365 days a year at local → section → region → area levels, passing radiograms (arrl.org/nts). Same directed-net vocabulary a Technician learns on VHF, but on HF the "local" net can cover a time zone. `[practice]`
- ARES = ARRL volunteers — trained, equipped (go-kits), and working under MOUs with served agencies from public-service events up to multi-week disaster deployments (arrl.org/ares). RACES is the FCC Part 97 civil-defense service: the control operator must hold an FCC amateur license (pool G2B09), and routine drills are limited to 1 hour per week without special authorization (G2B11). `[pool-supported: G2B09, G2B11]`
- Distress priority: a station in distress breaking into your QSO gets acknowledged — find out what help is needed (pool G2B02) — and emergencies are the one exception to "no station has priority" (G2B01). On 14.300/14.325 that means: stop, listen, relay if asked. `[pool-supported: G2B01, G2B02]`
- FACT — **Winlink on HF**: radio email through a worldwide network of gateway stations — a Winlink "Remote Message Server" is a gateway (pool G2E13) — reached by transmitting a connect message on the gateway's published frequency (G2E10), over VARA (G2E02) or PACTOR links. PACTOR is strictly two-station ARQ (G2E09): NAK requests retransmission (G8C05), too many failed retries drop the connection (G8C06), and interference shows up as retries, timeouts, and failed connects (G2E03). Under the hood it's packet radio: the frame's header carries the routing and handling information (G8C03), and forward error correction sends redundant information with the data so the receiver can fix errors without a repeat (G8C10). Emcomm scale: a FEMA-sponsored exercise in July 2025 pulled 1,119 field situation reports via Winlink in one event (winlink.org news). Winlink is wireless, works VHF and HF, and is a form of packet radio — all three, per pool G2E12. `[pool-supported: G2E02, G2E03, G2E09, G2E10, G2E12, G2E13, G8C03, G8C05, G8C06, G8C10]`
- AREDN (pool G2E11) is the OTHER emcomm data tool: high-speed mesh networking on microwave amateur allocations (converted Wi-Fi gear) for emergencies and community events — not an HF mode; readers should file it under "data when the internet is down at the site," with Winlink-HF as "email when everything between here and the outside world is down." Mesh resilience is the selling point: if one node fails, a packet may still reach its target via an alternate node (pool G8C09). `[pool-supported: G2E11, G8C09]` `[practice]`

## 7. Logging & QSLing

*The contact isn't finished until it's logged — and for DX, confirmed.*

- Why keep a log: the pool's answer — to help you reply if the FCC requests information about your station (pool G2D08); the operator's answers — awards, QSLing, and "when did I last work that station?" Practice: log in UTC (dates flip at 0000Z), and let software export ADIF, the interchange format every logging and award system eats. `[pool-supported: G2D08]` `[practice]`
- FACT — **LoTW (Logbook of The World)**: the ARRL's web-accessed database where submitted electronic logs cross-match into confirmations — no paper cards needed — and it feeds award progress for WAS and DXCC, membership in which takes confirming contacts with 100 countries (arrl.org/logbook-of-the-world). `[practice]`
- FACT — **The bureau route**: ARRL members mail prefix-sorted DX cards to the Outgoing QSL Service in Newington (published rate as of 2026-07-24: $3.00 for 1–10 cards); cards flow to counterpart bureaus in roughly 225 DXCC entities, and incoming cards arrive via your call-district bureau. Sort cards going to a QSL manager under the MANAGER's call; no US-to-US bureau cards unless one station was DX at the time (arrl.org/outgoing-qsl-service). Cheap but slow — months to years. `[practice]`
- Direct QSLing: mail your card to the address on the station's QRZ.com page with return postage or an SASE — the norm for rare DX and DXpeditions, which publish their QSL route ("via the bureau," "direct only," "via manager," or online request systems). The station's QRZ page is the authority on which route works. `[practice]`
- Electronic alternatives beyond LoTW (eQSL, QRZ Logbook) are fine for fun and some sponsors' awards, but ARRL award credit (DXCC, WAS) runs on LoTW or paper cards — set beginner expectations accordingly. `[practice]`
- FACT — "QSL" the Q-signal means "I have received and understood" (pool G2C09) — the QSL card is literally that confirmation written out, which is why "QSL?" on the air can mean either "did you copy?" or "will you send a card?" depending on context. Ethics-manual norm: be willing to exchange cards; charging more than return-postage costs for a direct card is considered unethical. `[pool-supported: G2C09]` `[practice]`

## 8. CW & Q-code culture

*The abbreviations actually heard on HF, and the manners around them.*

- FACT — The pool's five Q-signals are heard daily: QRS? = send slower (G2C02), QRL? = is this frequency in use? (G2C04), QSL = received and understood (G2C09), QRN = troubled by static (G2C10), QRV = ready to receive (G2C11). Beyond the pool, the ethics manual's list that leaks into phone as nouns and verbs: QRM (interference), QSB (fading), QSY (change frequency), QTH (location), QRT (going off the air), QRZ (who called me?), QRX (stand by) — "the QSB is rough tonight," "let's QSY up 5." `[pool-supported: G2C02, G2C04, G2C09, G2C10, G2C11]` `[practice]`
- FACT — Prosigns: KN = listening only for specific station(s) (G2C03), AR = end of a formal message (G2C08). Also heard constantly: SK (end of contact), BK (break), AS (stand by), and the friendly "dit-dit" many ops send to close a CW QSO (Ethics doc §II.9.19). `[pool-supported: G2C03, G2C08]` `[practice]`
- FACT — CW shorthand that jumped to speech: **73** = best regards (already plural — the ethics manual says never "73s"), **88** = love and kisses, **OM/YL**; on-key staples include FB (fine business = excellent), ES (and), TU (thank you), DE (from), CUL (see you later) (Ethics doc §II.1, §II.9.28). `[practice]`
- Answering a CW CQ: no faster than the CQ was sent (pool G2C05) — a slow operator answered at 25 WPM will QRS you or give up. Zero beat = match your transmit frequency to his (G2C06); with both stations running narrow filters (G8B09) and the pool's 150–500 Hz CW separations (G2B04), a call 300 Hz off may simply never be heard. `[pool-supported: G2C05, G2C06, G2B04, G8B09]`
- FACT — QSK (full break-in) = the transmitting station can receive between code characters and elements (pool G2C01) — the other op can break you mid-word; you'll hear non-QSK rigs' relays clacking between elements instead. `[pool-supported: G2C01]` `[practice]`
- RST culture on CW: the T scale rates tone 1–9, and suffixes carry the real news — "C" flags chirp (pool G2C07); a "599C" report on a vintage rig is the polite nudge that its oscillator is drifting. `[pool-supported: G2C07]` `[practice]`

## Watch items (pool framing vs. common practice; time-sensitive pins; unverified claims)

1. **The opposite sideband conventions — the pool tests both, pin them precisely.** Voice: LSB below 10 MHz, USB at 10 MHz and above (G2A01/A02/A04/A09). Digital AFSK: RTTY → LSB (G2E01); JT65/JT9/FT4/FT8 → USB (G2E05) on EVERY band, 80/40 m included (WSJT-X manual and ARRL OTA both say USB, flatly). Do not print an origin story for the RTTY-LSB custom without a source — none of the references fetched for this note documents it, so this note deliberately states the practice only.
2. **FT8 watering holes evolve.** Pool pins 14.074–14.077 MHz (G2E15); the ARRL OTA table (Jul/Aug 2022) pins the HF list used here; 6 m/60 m values come from the DX Engineering OnAllBands table. Conventions have shifted before (regional alternates exist, e.g., 50.323 intercontinental on 6 m) — re-verify against the current WSJT-X default frequency table and ARRL before print.
3. **Clock tolerance wording.** Pool G2E07: ~1 second. ARRL OTA: within 2 seconds, closer is better; WSJT-X system requirements: ±1 s. Use the pool's 1 second in anything exam-facing; operating boxes may add "sync to NTP; signals visible but nothing decodes = fix the clock."
4. **Net frequencies are custom, not assignments.** 14.300 (MMSN/GECOA) and 14.325 (HWN) are traditions operating under 97.101(b) — even HWN asks permission if the frequency is busy. Never write "the emergency frequency" as if allocated; teach "center of activity by long custom."
5. **Contest "59(9)" vs. honest reports.** G2D11's rationale (reports let stations adapt) is everyday practice; in contests the report is a fixed formality. Teach both so a new General neither sends a real "53" in Sweepstakes nor believes every "59" in a pileup.
6. **"LSB below 14 MHz" shorthand.** Some study aids (e.g., the local ham-radio-clicker gen-3 explanation) phrase the convention as "USB at 14 MHz and above / LSB below" — that is the pool's G2A01 question wording, not the convention. The boundary is 10 MHz (Ethics doc), and 30/17/12 m are USB. Keep the book on the 10 MHz framing.
7. **ISS SSTV is time-sensitive.** 437.550 MHz Robot36 verified on ariss.org 2026-07-24 (matches the Technician canon pin of 2026-07-23); ARISS modes and frequencies have changed before — recheck ariss.org close to print. Same for HWN/MMSN schedules.
8. **Bureau fees and QSL logistics change.** The $3.00 / 1–10-card rate and the served-countries list are as published on arrl.org 2026-07-24 — either cite them "as of" or describe the process without prices.
9. **QRP power levels are custom.** The pool defines QRP only as "low-power transmit operation" (G2D10). The ~5 W (~10 W SSB) figures are club/award custom, not regulation — present them as custom or omit.
10. **UNVERIFIED items: none.** Every FACT line carries a pool ID or a URL fetched and content-checked 2026-07-24. Deliberately excluded for lack of a citable source: the RTTY-on-LSB origin story, exact FT4 watering-hole frequencies (FT4 appears only in color backed by the WSJT-X manual), specific DXpedition anecdotes, and any "most operators do X" quantifications.
