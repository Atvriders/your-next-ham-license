## 4. Station Setup & Good Practice

The first HF transceiver arrives in one box, but the station around it does not. The supporting cast piles up fast: a power supply under the bench, an antenna tuner, a wattmeter, a computer and an interface, maybe a linear amplifier someday. Book 2's VHF handheld needed nothing but a charged battery; an HF station is a system you assemble yourself, and what goes where — and why — decides how well it hears, how cleanly it transmits, and how politely it shares a house with every other electronic device in it. In this chapter you'll learn the station end to end: the receiver's noise-fighting toolbox, tube amplifiers and the ALC line that keeps them honest, the test gear that tells you the truth about your signal, grounding and bonding at HF, the S meter and the band-edge arithmetic every SSB operator must do, and the wiring that powers mobile and portable stations. This is pool subelement G4: 60 questions, five of which will be on your exam.

### The HF station, end to end

Start with the map. The RF path runs in a straight line: transceiver, optional linear amplifier, directional wattmeter, antenna tuner, coax feed line, antenna. Everything else hooks in around that spine. A 13.8 V power supply good for roughly 20 A feeds a 100-watt transceiver. The microphone — or the CW paddle — carries your audio or keying in. A computer joins through an isolated interface that carries audio plus transmit/receive switching in one direction, while a separate CAT control line carries frequency and mode commands in the other. Each box has exactly one job, and the figure shows where each sits in the signal and control flows.

Read the map twice. The first pass follows the RF: your voice becomes a transmitted signal at the transceiver, gains muscle in the amplifier if one is fitted, passes the wattmeter's inline sensor, runs through the tuner, and heads up the feed line to the antenna — with received signals flowing the same path in reverse. The second pass follows control: the supply feeds everything, the computer talks to the rig through its isolated interface (isolation keeps hum and RF out of the audio), and the amplifier's ALC line back to the rig limits drive power. The amplifier is drawn dashed because plenty of fine stations never own one; everything else on the map is the everyday HF baseline.

Two of those boxes earn their own section next — the amplifier and the tuner — but the transceiver itself hides a toolbox the pool tests piece by piece. Modern HF receivers fight three different kinds of noise with three different tools, and the exam keeps them scrupulously separate. Learn them as a matched set and three questions fall out for free.

The notch filter handles the carrier. When an interfering unmodulated carrier lands inside your receiver's passband — a steady key-down signal from the next kilohertz over — the notch filter removes that interfering carrier (G4A01). It carves a narrow, deep rejection right where the carrier sits and leaves the rest of the passband alone.

The noise blanker handles pulses. Ignition systems, power-line buzz, and some switching supplies fire short, intense bursts of noise, and a noise blanker mutes the receiver's gain for the duration of each noise pulse (G4A03), punching tiny holes in time exactly where the noise lived. Between pulses the receiver runs normally, so a signal buried under ignition racket becomes readable.

DSP noise reduction handles random noise — the steady hiss of band noise. It works by finding the predictable structure of speech or CW and suppressing what does not fit, and its trap is excess: turn the noise reduction control up too far and the desired signals become distorted (G4A07). Carrier, pulses, random hiss — notch, blanker, DSP. Three tools, three targets, three questions.

Three more receiver features round out the set. When a strong nearby signal pumps your receiver's front end toward overload, switch in the receive attenuator: it inserts a fixed loss ahead of everything, desired signal and crusher alike, and prevents overload from the strong signal (G4A13). On CW, the "reverse sideband" switch can move an interfering signal out of the passband (G4A02) — flipping which sideband the receiver listens to shifts the interferer's audio pitch the opposite way, often dropping it outside the filter while your contact stays put. And dual VFOs enable split operation, transmitting on one frequency while listening on another (G4A12) — the pileup technique chapter 2 put to work on the air.

One last box lives between your paddle and the rig. An electronic keyer automatically generates the dots and dashes of Morse code (G4A10): you steer with the paddle, and the keyer forms perfectly timed elements. Nearly every modern transceiver has one built in.

That is the receiver half of pool group G4A — notch filter, noise blanker, DSP noise reduction, attenuator, reverse sideband, split, and the keyer.

### Amplifiers, ALC, and the antenna tuner

A vacuum-tube linear amplifier is the most traditional box in the shack, and its tune-up is a two-knob dance the pool tests directly. Key down at reduced drive and adjust the TUNE control for a pronounced dip in plate current (G4A04): the dip marks resonance, the point where the plate tank circuit is tuned to the operating frequency. Off the dip, plate current soars and the tube burns power as heat instead of making RF. Then set the LOAD control — sometimes labeled COUPLING — for the desired output power without exceeding the tube's maximum allowable plate current (G4A08). LOAD sets how tightly the antenna load couples into the tank, and too light a coupling overheats the tube just as surely as a missed dip. The whole dance takes a few seconds once learned: dip the plate current with TUNE, bring the power up with LOAD, and recheck that the dip still holds.

Between the transceiver — the exciter, in amplifier language — and the amplifier runs one small line with a big job: ALC, automatic level control. The amplifier samples its own condition and feeds a voltage back to the exciter, and the exciter uses it to throttle its drive. An ALC connection between exciter and amplifier prevents excessive drive (G4A05), and excessive drive is the root of flat-topping, splatter, and angry neighbors on the band. On SSB voice the ALC should kick only on your loudest syllables; if the ALC meter pins on every word, you are driving too hard.

But one family of modes requires that hand off the throttle entirely. With AFSK data modes — RTTY, PSK31, and the other audio-fed digital modes — the ALC must be inactive, because its gain-riding action distorts the signal (G4A11). A data waveform depends on a clean, unvarying envelope, and ALC pumping the gain up and down warps it. Set the audio drive so the ALC indication stays at zero and let the waveform through untouched.

One more amplifier courtesy protects hardware instead of spectrum. When you key a transceiver connected to an external amplifier, the amplifier's relays must physically switch the antenna from the transceiver's receive path over to the amplifier's output before any RF arrives. Delaying the RF output after the keying line activates gives the relays time to make that switch (G4A09). Transmit into the relays while they are still moving — hot-switching — and the contacts arc and pit; a season of hot-switching ruins an expensive relay. Most modern rigs offer an adjustable keying delay for exactly this reason.

Finally, the box with the most misunderstood job in radio: the antenna tuner, also sold as a coupler or transmatch. Here is the truth the pool tests twice — once here and again in chapter 9 (G9A08). A tuner increases power transfer from the transmitter to the feed line by making the transmitter see its design load: 50 ohms, as far as the rig can tell (G4A06). It does not change the SWR on the feed line to the antenna. The mismatch at the antenna feed point is still there; the standing waves on the coax are still there. The tuner simply hides them from the transmitter, so the rig's output stage runs happily into the load it was designed for. "The tuner fixes the antenna's SWR" is the classic wrong mental model — it fools the radio, not the feed line.

Amplifiers, ALC, hot-switching, and the tuner finish pool group G4A.

### The test bench

Every serious shack accumulates test gear, and the General pool expects you to know which instrument answers which question. The oscilloscope is the most versatile: it contains horizontal and vertical channel amplifiers (G4B01) — one steering the trace across time, the other across voltage — so the screen draws a picture of a waveform. The screen's graticule turns the picture into measurements: volts per division vertically, time per division horizontally. That picture is exactly why an oscilloscope beats a DVM for viewing complex waveforms (G4B02): a meter reports one number, while the scope shows the shape. It is the best instrument for checking the keying waveform of a CW transmitter (G4B03), where the rise and fall time of each dot decides whether you sound clean or clicky. And to display an RF envelope, feed attenuated transmitter RF output into the scope's vertical input (G4B04) — attenuated, because raw transmitter RF would cook the input stage.

Meters keep their own honors. A voltmeter uses high input impedance so that connecting it does not load the circuit under test (G4B05); a low-impedance meter would drag the circuit's voltage down and lie to you about it. Between meter types, the DMM's advantage is higher precision (G4B06) — three and a half digits settle arguments an analog needle cannot. But when you are adjusting a circuit for a peak or a null, rocking a control back and forth hunting the maximum or the minimum, the analog meter wins (G4B09), because a swinging needle shows trend and direction instantly while digits dither.

Then come the RF instruments. The two-tone test is the standard check of an SSB transmitter: feed two non-harmonically related audio tones into the microphone input and examine the output, and what you are analyzing is linearity (G4B07, G4B08). A linear transmitter outputs exactly the two tones you gave it; a nonlinear one adds intermodulation products you can watch on the scope or a spectrum display. The planted wrong answer is carrier suppression — the two-tone test does not measure it.

A directional wattmeter sits inline in the feed line and reads forward power heading toward the antenna and reflected power coming back, and from that pair the SWR follows (G4B10). It works on the air, at full power, during normal operation, which makes it the everyday instrument for watching antenna-system health: creeping reflected power is the first sign of a corroded connector or a feed line taking on water.

> **The math, if you want it:** SWR follows from forward and reflected power as $SWR = (1+\sqrt{P_r/P_f})/(1-\sqrt{P_r/P_f})$ — reflected over forward, under the square roots. Chapter 9 drills the numbers; here the instrument is the point.

The antenna analyzer answers a different question with the transmitter off. It connects to the antenna and feed line in place of the rig (G4B11), puts out its own low-power test signal, and sweeps — reading SWR and impedance across frequency, and it can measure the characteristic impedance of a coax line (G4B13). One caution the pool tests: nearby strong signals can corrupt its SWR readings (G4B12), because the analyzer's tiny receiver cannot always tell its own signal from the broadcast station down the road. Know what it cannot do, either: an analyzer cannot measure an antenna's front-to-back ratio or your transmitter's output power.

The dummy load completes the bench: a 50-ohm non-inductive resistor on a heat sink that replaces the antenna entirely, so you can tune up and test at full power with nothing on the air and nobody to bother. Reach for it when checking a newly built amplifier, chasing a key-click complaint, or burn-testing a rig after a repair — any time the test matters and the radiation does not. ### RFI, grounding, and bonding

HF means longer antennas, stronger fields, and more chances for your RF to visit places it was never invited — a complaint called RFI, radio-frequency interference. The mechanism is usually rectification: a transistor junction inside the affected device detects your RF the way a diode detector would, and the device's amplifier dutifully amplifies the garbage. The cures form a short list the pool tests item by item. In audio circuits, a bypass capacitor shunts RF to ground (G4C01): wired across an audio input, the capacitor is a low-impedance shortcut to ground at radio frequencies and nearly an open circuit at audio, so the RF leaves before it can be rectified. When a cable itself is the antenna — RF flowing along the outside of the shield as common-mode current — place a ferrite choke on the cable (G4C08): the ferrite puts impedance in that current's path and chokes it off. And when interference arrives as wideband hash across many frequencies at once, suspect arcing at a poor electrical connection (G4C02) — a corroded fence joint, a loose clamp on a power line — because every tiny arc is a broadband spark transmitter.

What does your signal sound like in the neighbor's powered speakers? Less intelligible than you fear, which is exactly the exam point. SSB comes through as distorted speech (G4C03): rectification inside the device mangles your envelope beyond recognition, so the pool's "clearly audible speech" choice is the trap. CW comes through as on-and-off humming or clicking (G4C04) — keyed thumps, not dits and dahs.

Inside your own shack, grounding does two jobs that trace to the same physics. A wire that is a fine conductor at 60 Hz can be a high impedance at your operating frequency — long enough to be resonant, for instance — and RF burns and hot chassis come from exactly that: a ground connection with high impedance at the operating frequency (G4C05, G4C06). That is why shack ground leads stay short, wide, and direct: at RF the current hugs the conductor's surface, so a wide flat strap beats a long round wire. The lightning-protection ground system plays by one more rule: never solder its joints — clamp or weld them — because lightning heat destroys solder (G4C07). Outside, every feed line should enter through a lightning arrestor on a grounded entrance panel, with the panel, the shack's ground bus, and every ground rod bonded together on a short, direct path.

Bonding is the inside half of the discipline. Bond your equipment enclosures together — a short strap from each chassis to a single-point ground bus behind the bench — to minimize ground loops (G4C09). The telltale symptom of a ground loop is hum on your transmitted audio (G4C10): current circulating between chassis at slightly different potentials rides your signal out the antenna. The same bonding minimizes RF hot spots in the shack (G4C11) — chassis that bite your fingers or a microphone that tingles at full power. And the safety rule underneath everything: ground all metal enclosures, so hazardous voltages can never appear on the chassis (G4C12).

### Speech processing, S meters, and the band edges

A speech processor is a loudness machine, and understanding what it raises — and what it must not raise — answers three exam questions. Speech is naturally peaky: brief loud syllables towering over quiet gaps. The processor compresses those dynamics, raising your average power while the peaks stay within legal limits (G4D01), and the increased average power is what increases apparent loudness at the far end (G4D02). The trap answer is "increases peak power" — the peaks stay where they were while the average rises to meet them. Misadjusted, a processor makes things worse three ways at once: distorted speech, excess intermodulation, and excessive background noise (G4D03). Overcompression pumps the room noise up between your words and splatters energy outside your channel, so a little processing goes a long way.

The S meter on your receiver measures received signal strength (G4D04), and it comes with a calibration anchor the pool loves: one S unit is approximately 6 dB (G4D06). Since 6 dB is four times power, raising your signal from S8 to S9 at the other station's receiver takes approximately four times the transmit power (G4D07). And the big-station brag "20 dB over S9" means 100 times more powerful than S9 (G4D05) — 20 dB is a power ratio of 100. Real S meters are optimistic instruments, every rig's calibration a little different, which is why the pool adds "assuming a properly calibrated S meter"; the anchors hold regardless. Chapter 5 makes decibel math formal; here the anchors do the work.

> **Worked example:** Your contact reads you S8 and asks you to try for S9. One S unit ≈ 6 dB ≈ 4 times power, so your 100 W becomes 400 W — a four-hundred-watt favor for one needle-width of improvement, which is why brute power is the most expensive way to buy an S unit. Meanwhile the big station down the band is "20 dB over S9": that is $10^{20/10} = 100$ times the power of an S9 signal. The formal dB math arrives in chapter 5; the anchors do the exam work here.

SSB adds one piece of station arithmetic every General must do in their head, because the rig lies to you by omission. On SSB the display shows the suppressed-carrier frequency — where the carrier would be if there were one — but your signal is 3 kHz wide and sits entirely to one side of that number. On USB the signal occupies the carrier frequency up to carrier + 3 kHz; on LSB it occupies carrier − 3 kHz up to the carrier. At a band edge, that one-sidedness is the difference between legal and out of band (G4D08, G4D09).

> **Worked example:** Your LSB dial reads 7.178 MHz on 40 meters. The signal occupies 7.175–7.178 MHz — legal by a hair, because the General 40 meter phone segment starts at 7.175 MHz, the G1A05 boundary from chapter 1 meeting you again. Flip to USB with the dial at 14.347 MHz and the signal occupies 14.347–14.350 MHz, inside the 20 meter band's 14.350 MHz upper edge. The rules of thumb follow directly: with 3 kHz LSB, stay at least 3 kHz above a lower band edge (G4D10); with 3 kHz USB, stay at least 3 kHz below an upper band edge (G4D11).

The distractors in these questions plant 1.5 kHz — half the bandwidth — and 1 kHz spacings; the full 3 kHz occupied bandwidth is the keyed answer every time. Speech processors, S meters, and sideband arithmetic are pool group G4D.

### Mobile and portable power

HF in the car, or HF in the field, adds power and antenna problems the home station never meets. Start with the antenna, because the antenna is the weak link: antenna efficiency is the biggest limit on an HF mobile station's performance (G4E05). A mobile whip on 40 or 80 meters is physically far shorter than a quarter wavelength, so it is electrically lengthened with loading inductance and, up top, a capacitance hat — a small disc or spokes of metal that electrically lengthens the physically short whip (G4E01) while easing the loading coil's job. At the very tip sits a corona ball, a smooth sphere that bleeds off the intense RF voltage at the whip's end to prevent corona discharge (G4E02). The price of all that shortening is sharpness: shortened antennas have high Q and therefore very limited operating bandwidth (G4E06), so expect to retune the whip as you move across a band.

Power wiring is the second mobile discipline, and the pool is blunt about it. A 100-watt HF rig draws 20 A or more on transmit peaks, so it connects directly to the battery with heavy-gauge wire, fused (G4E03). The vehicle's cigarette-lighter or auxiliary socket wiring cannot carry the current (G4E04) — it is thin, shared with other loads, and fused for a phone charger, not a transmitter. Fuse the leads at the battery end, route them away from the vehicle's computer wiring, and let the battery itself smooth the supply. And once mobile, your receiver meets the vehicle's own noise: the charging system, the fuel delivery system, and control computers — all of them (G4E07). That is why the noise blanker from earlier in this chapter earns its keep in a mobile installation.

Portable and emergency power brings the solar panel, four facts deep. The cells in a panel are wired in series-parallel — series strings for useful voltage, paralleled for useful current (G4E08). One silicon photovoltaic cell produces about 0.5 V open-circuit in full sun (G4E09), which is why even a "12-volt" panel carries dozens of cells in series. A series diode between panel and battery stops the battery discharging back through the panel at night (G4E10). And lithium iron phosphate batteries — the light, high-performance portable option, a fraction of the lead weight for field day or an emergency kit — require a charge controller with the panel (G4E11), because LiFePO4 chemistry does not forgive sloppy charging.

Mobile and portable power is pool group G4E — and with it, subelement G4 is fully covered.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G4 — Amateur Radio Practices — is 60 pool questions in five groups (G4A–G4E), and your exam draws five of them, one per group. The ten below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each.

> **G4A06** What is the purpose of an antenna tuner?
> A. Reduce the SWR in the feed line to the antenna
> B. Reduce the power dissipation in the feedline to the antenna
> C. Increase power transfer from the transmitter to the feed line
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: C** — the tuner makes the transmitter see its design load; the SWR on the feed line to the antenna is unchanged.

> **G4A09** What is the purpose of delaying RF output after activating a transmitter’s keying line to an external amplifier?
> A. To prevent key clicks on CW
> B. To prevent transient overmodulation
> C. To allow time for the amplifier to switch the antenna between the transceiver and the amplifier output
> D. To allow time for the amplifier power supply to reach operating level
> **Answer: C** — the delay lets the amplifier's relays switch the antenna before RF arrives; hot-switching damages them.

> **G4B03** Which of the following is the best instrument to use for checking the keying waveform of a CW transmitter?
> A. An oscilloscope
> B. A field strength meter
> C. A sidetone monitor
> D. A wavemeter
> **Answer: A** — only the scope shows the shape of each dot's rise and fall, so it is the instrument for a CW keying waveform.

> **G4B10** Which of the following can be determined with a directional wattmeter?
> A. Standing wave ratio
> B. Antenna front-to-back ratio
> C. RF interference
> D. Radio wave propagation
> **Answer: A** — it reads forward and reflected power, and SWR follows from the pair.

> **G4C05** What is a possible cause of high voltages that produce RF burns?
> A. Flat braid rather than round wire has been used for the ground wire
> B. Insulated wire has been used for the ground wire
> C. The ground rod is resonant
> D. The ground wire has high impedance on that frequency
> **Answer: D** — a ground wire with high impedance at the operating frequency leaves RF voltage on the chassis to bite you.

> **G4C08** Which of the following would reduce RF interference caused by common-mode current on an audio cable?
> A. Place a ferrite choke on the cable
> B. Connect the center conductor to the shield of all cables to short circuit the RFI signal
> C. Ground the center conductor of the audio cable causing the interference
> D. Add an additional insulating jacket to the cable
> **Answer: A** — the ferrite choke puts impedance in the path of common-mode current flowing on the outside of the cable.

> **G4D05** How does a signal that reads 20 dB over S9 compare to one that reads S9 on a receiver, assuming a properly calibrated S meter?
> A. It is 10 times less powerful
> B. It is 20 times less powerful
> C. It is 20 times more powerful
> D. It is 100 times more powerful
> **Answer: D** — 20 dB is a power ratio of 100, so the signal is 100 times more powerful than S9.

> **G4D08** What frequency range is occupied by a 3 kHz LSB signal when the displayed carrier frequency is set to 7.178 MHz?
> A. 7.178 MHz to 7.181 MHz
> B. 7.178 MHz to 7.184 MHz
> C. 7.175 MHz to 7.178 MHz
> D. 7.1765 MHz to 7.1795 MHz
> **Answer: C** — LSB occupies carrier − 3 kHz up to the carrier, landing the lower edge exactly on 7.175 MHz, the General phone boundary from G1A05 — taught in chapter 1.

> **G4E03** Which of the following direct, fused power connections would be the best for a 100-watt HF mobile installation?
> A. To the battery using heavy-gauge wire
> B. To the alternator or generator using heavy-gauge wire
> C. To the battery using insulated heavy duty balanced transmission line
> D. To the alternator or generator using insulated heavy duty balanced transmission line
> **Answer: A** — heavy-gauge fused wire straight to the battery carries the transmit current; the alternator and the lighter socket are the wrong taps.

> **G4E09** What is the approximate open-circuit voltage from a fully illuminated silicon photovoltaic cell?
> A. 0.02 VDC
> B. 0.5 VDC
> C. 0.2 VDC
> D. 1.38 VDC
> **Answer: B** — one silicon photovoltaic cell gives about 0.5 V in full sun; 1.38 V is a mercury cell, not a solar cell.

### Key Takeaways

- The notch filter, the noise blanker, and DSP noise reduction are three different tools — carrier, pulses, and random hiss — and the receive attenuator prevents overload from strong signals.

- Tune a tube amplifier for the pronounced dip in plate current, set LOAD for power without exceeding maximum plate current, keep ALC inactive on AFSK data modes, and delay RF so amplifier relays never hot-switch.

- The antenna tuner fools the radio, not the feed line: it makes the transmitter see its design load but never changes the SWR between tuner and antenna.

- On the test bench, the scope owns waveforms and CW keying, the DMM owns precision, the analog meter owns peaks and nulls, and the directional wattmeter turns forward and reflected power into SWR.

- A bypass capacitor and a ferrite choke cure most RFI; bond chassis together to kill ground loops — hum on your transmitted audio — and RF hot spots, and never solder lightning-protection grounds.

- One S unit ≈ 6 dB ≈ 4× power, and "20 dB over S9" is 100× S9; on SSB the dial shows the suppressed carrier, with LSB hanging below it and USB above it, so keep a full 3 kHz inside every band edge.

- Wire a 100-watt mobile rig directly to the battery with heavy-gauge fused wire, and remember the solar set: about 0.5 V per silicon cell, a series diode against night-time back-discharge, and a charge controller for LiFePO4.
