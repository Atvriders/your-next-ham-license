## 10. Safety & RF Exposure at General Depth

A General license raises the stakes on everything Book 2 taught you about safety: up to 1.5 kW PEP instead of a handheld's five watts, antennas strung through trees and stacked on towers, long coax runs through walls, and amplifiers that keep lethal voltages on their plates. The good news is that the rules and habits you are about to learn are exactly what make all of that routine rather than risky. In this chapter you'll learn what RF energy actually does to the human body, how the FCC's exposure rules work and how to evaluate your own station against them, and the electrical, grounding, tower, and workbench practices that keep a bigger station safe to build and to run. This is pool subelement G0: 25 questions, two of which will be on your exam.

### What RF energy does — and what it doesn't

Book 2 gave you the safety floor in one line: RF burns are real, never touch a live antenna, fuse the power leads at the battery. Now the exam wants the why underneath, starting with the most basic question of all — what can RF energy do to a body? The established effect is **heating** (G0A01): RF fields make tissue molecules jostle, and jostling is heat, the same physics a microwave oven uses at 2.4 GHz. RF is **non-ionizing radiation**, meaning its photons are far too weak to break chemical bonds or damage DNA the way X-rays and gamma rays can, so "radiation poisoning" and the other distractors borrowed from ionizing-radiation language are simply the wrong hazard. The real question is never whether RF is radioactive but how much heating a person near your antenna is absorbing.

Three properties of your transmitted signal answer that question, and the pool keys **all three** (G0A02). **Frequency** matters because the body absorbs some bands more efficiently than others, which is why the maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limits vary with frequency — the strictest limits sit in the VHF range around 50 MHz, near whole-body resonance. **Power density** matters because exposure is the RF power arriving per unit area where the person stands, set by your power, your antenna's gain, and above all distance — an inverse-square gift that works in your favor every time. And **duty cycle** matters because exposure averages over time: a transmitter keyed half the day delivers half the average field of one keyed continuously.

The three together explain why two identical rigs can land in different places on compliance: the 6-meter operator faces the strictest limits of any band the pool touches, the Yagi owner has concentrated the power density in one direction, and the all-day digital enthusiast runs triple the duty cycle of a casual phone operator. Frequency you cannot negotiate, but power density and duty cycle are yours to arrange — and both improve quickly with antenna height, because every extra foot of separation shrinks the density where people stand.

That third variable earns its own vocabulary. **Duty factor** is the fraction of peak envelope power your mode actually delivers while transmitting, and your operating style adds a second factor — the fraction of time you transmit at all. Multiply the two and you have the number the limits care about, because **time averaging** means the total RF exposure averaged over a certain period (G0A04). The averaging windows are minutes long, so what counts is not your peak power but your average. That is the whole logic behind the pool's duty-cycle rule: **a lower duty cycle permits greater power levels to be transmitted** (G0A07) for the same average exposure. It is also why an evaluation asks about your operating habits, not just your hardware: the same rig is a different exposure source for a weekend contest operator than for a once-a-week ragchewer.

> **Worked example:** Two 100 W PEP stations, same band, same antenna. The CW operator's duty factor is roughly 40 percent while keyed, and he keys the transmitter half the time: $P_{avg} = 100 \times 0.4 \times 0.5 = 20\ W$ of average exposure contribution. The FT8 operator runs a 100 percent duty factor in every other 15-second slot: 100 × 1.0 × 0.5 = 50 W — two and a half times the exposure from the same "100-watt" rig. An SSB ragchewer is gentler still, averaging roughly 20 percent of PEP while keyed and talking half the time, for about 10 W. Same transceiver, three very different exposure pictures — which is exactly why a lower duty cycle buys headroom under the limits.

One more pair of terms frames the subject. The rules divide the world into a **controlled environment**, where people know they are near RF sources and the limits are looser, and an **uncontrolled environment**, where the general public wanders unaware and the limits are stricter. Your own household members may be evaluated against the occupational/controlled limits; your neighbors and passers-by get the general-population/uncontrolled ones. When you evaluate, you evaluate the places people can actually reach, against the right limit for each.

So which stations do the rules reach? Essentially all of them: the FCC's RF-exposure rules apply to **all stations with a time-averaged transmission of more than one milliwatt** (G0A12). One milliwatt — the most-misremembered number in the subelement. Not 500 watts PEP, not only antennas below a wavelength, and no general amateur exemption. Treat one milliwatt as the floor under everything else in this chapter.

> **Worked example:** How close is a real station to that floor? A 100 W rig at a 10 percent overall duty cycle averages 10 W — ten thousand times the threshold. Transmit for only 36 seconds out of an hour and the time-averaged transmission is still 1 W, a thousand times over the line. There is no practical amateur station below one milliwatt, so plan to evaluate your station rather than to claim an exemption.

Heating, the three exposure variables, time averaging, duty cycle, the two environments, and the one-milliwatt threshold are pool questions G0A01, G0A02, G0A04, G0A07, and G0A12.

### Evaluating your station

Since essentially every station is covered, the rules hand you a job. A station that doesn't meet the exemption criteria must **perform an RF exposure evaluation in accordance with FCC OET Bulletin 65** (G0A06) — the FCC Office of Engineering and Technology's guidance document, with a Supplement B written for the amateur service. OET-65 is not paperwork you file; it is homework you keep. Its tables and formulas turn your band, power, antenna gain, feed-line loss, and duty factor into distances within which the MPE limits could be exceeded. If the places people can reach lie outside those distances, the station is compliant. For a typical 100 W HF station with a dipole in the clear, the table lookup often ends the exercise in minutes, because the required separations at that power are modest. Add a kilowatt amplifier or a high-gain beam and the distances grow — which is exactly why the General-class power increase is when this chapter stops being theory.

How may you show compliance? Three ways, and the pool keys **all these choices are correct** (G0A03): **calculation based on FCC OET Bulletin 65**, **calculation based on computer modeling**, and **measurement of field strength using calibrated equipment**. Any one of the three stands on its own. And here is a warning you have now earned: "all these choices are correct" is genuinely keyed on G0A02 and G0A03, but the very same phrase is a *distractor* on G0A05, G0B05, G0B07, G0B08, and G0B09. Learn content, never answer shapes.

{{fig:ch10-mpe-evaluation}}

Evaluation is not a one-time event. The ongoing duty is to **perform a routine RF exposure evaluation and prevent access to any identified high-exposure areas** (G0A08) — and "routine" means again whenever the station changes. A new amplifier, a new band, a new antenna, a different mix of modes, a tower that grew taller: each change reopens the question, because each one moves power density somewhere it wasn't before. The habit that keeps you honest is to re-run the numbers — or the model, or the measurements — as part of any station upgrade, the same way you would re-check SWR after moving an antenna.

And if the evaluation says a limit could be exceeded? The rule is simple and the verb is strong: **take action to prevent human exposure to the excessive RF fields** (G0A05, 47 CFR §97.13(c)(2)). The toolbox has three levers, alone or combined: reduce power, increase separation (raise the antenna, move it farther from people), or restrict access to the high-field area — a fence, a locked roof hatch, a sign. Notice that "all these choices are correct" does *not* rescue you here; mitigation is specific, not optional.

Two siting cases get their own questions. If the main lobe of your directional antenna could expose a neighbor beyond the limit, **take precautions to ensure that the antenna cannot be pointed in their direction when they are present** (G0A10) — in practice, anything from setting rotator stops to simply not beaming at the house next door while the family is in the yard. Indoors, an attic or closet antenna is acceptable only if **MPE limits are not exceeded in occupied areas** (G0A11) — the bedroom under the attic dipole is an occupied area, so run the numbers before you fall in love with the installation.

Finally, if you choose the measurement route, the instrument matters. Accurately measuring an RF field takes **a calibrated field-strength meter with a calibrated antenna** (G0A09). Your SWR meter doesn't qualify, and neither does your receiver's S meter — compliance is a measurement, and only calibrated equipment makes it one.

The evaluation duty and its three methods, the routine re-evaluation habit, mitigation, antenna siting, and the measuring instrument complete pool group G0A: G0A03, G0A05, G0A06, G0A08, G0A09, G0A10, and G0A11.

### Wiring and the National Electrical Code

The G0B half of this subelement leaves RF entirely and walks into the shack, the breaker panel, and the back yard. Start at the panel. A four-conductor 240 VAC circuit carries two hot wires, a neutral, and a ground — 240 V between the hots for big loads, 120 V from either hot to neutral for ordinary ones — and the exam asks which of them get fuses or circuit breakers: **only the hot wires** (G0B01). Never the neutral, never the ground. The reason is worth the memory hook: the overcurrent device exists to disconnect the source of the hazard, and if a fuse in the neutral blew first, the circuit would look dead while the equipment sat connected to the hot legs — a chassis can end up at line voltage with nothing to warn you. Protect the hots, always.

Wire size and breaker size are a matched pair, and the pool asks it in both directions. Given the breaker: a 20-ampere circuit breaker requires **AWG number 12** wire minimum (G0B02). Given the wire: AWG number 14 wiring pairs with a **15-ampere** fuse or circuit breaker (G0B03). Both numbers come from the National Electrical Code's ampacity tables, and the logic is that the breaker protects the wire, not the appliance: the wire must carry everything its breaker will let through without heating. Remember that AWG runs backwards — bigger number, thinner wire — and learn the pair as a pair: 20 A with AWG 12, 15 A with AWG 14.

Whose rules are these? The **National Electrical Code (NEC)**, formally NFPA 70, covers the **electrical safety of the station** (G0B06) — wiring, grounding, overcurrent protection. Not bandwidth, not RF exposure, not emissions, whatever the distractors offer. One more protective device earns a question: the **ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI)**, the fast-acting outlet you know from bathrooms and kitchens. A GFCI runs both conductors through a small sensing transformer: when every milliampere outbound returns on the neutral, the magnetic fields cancel, and an imbalance of just a few milliamperes trips the contacts in a fraction of a cycle. The imbalance happens when **current flows from one or more of the hot wires directly to ground** (G0B05) — for instance through a person — and the missing return current is exactly what the device detects. For a shack fed from a garage or basement circuit, or a Field Day generator in the rain, that speed is the difference between a scare and a tragedy. Hot-wire fusing, the ampacity pairs, the NEC, and the GFCI are pool questions G0B01, G0B02, G0B03, G0B05, and G0B06.

### Grounding, lightning, and towers

Chapter 4 built the shack side of grounding — a single-point bus, short straps, bonded chassis. The lightning side adds three placement rules, and all three start from one principle: keep strike current outside, give it a short path down, and never let it choose between two "separate" grounds. First, the station's lightning-protection ground system belongs **outside the building** (G0B04) — the whole point is to divert a strike before it comes indoors. Second, lightning arrestors mount **where the feed lines enter the building** (G0B13), on the entrance panel at the wall, so a surge is shunted to ground at the boundary rather than inside your operating position. Third, and most tested: lightning-protection ground rods **must be bonded together with all other grounds** (G0B11) — the electrical service ground, the tower ground, the entrance-panel ground, all connected. Separate "independent" grounds are the hazard, because a strike that raises one ground thousands of volts above another will move that difference through your house wiring and your equipment to even things out. Chapter 4's companion rule applies at every joint in this system: clamp or weld, never solder, because strike heat destroys it. One of G0B11's distractors demands right-angle bends in ground wires — real practice avoids sharp bends, but the keyed requirement is bonding, so read the whole question before reaching for a half-remembered rule of thumb.

Towers concentrate every hazard at height, and the pool's two climbing questions are one-item checklists. Before you climb with a safety harness, **confirm that the harness is rated for the weight of the climber and that it is within its allowable service life** (G0B07) — check the webbing, stitching, and buckles before trusting them, retire a harness that has arrested a fall, and treat the manufacturer's service-life limit as a hard date. A secondhand harness with an unknown history is not safety equipment. Before you climb a tower that supports electrically powered devices — rotators, remote switches, amplifiers, lights — **make sure all circuits that supply power to the tower are locked out and tagged** (G0B08): breaker open, locked so no one can close it, and tagged with who locked it and why. Lockout/tagout is the industrial habit that guarantees nobody re-energizes the circuit while your hands are on the steel — and the circuits feeding the tower include the transmitters whose antennas live on it, because a climber beside a keyed antenna is an RF-exposure problem from the first half of this chapter, not just a shock problem. Lightning placement, bonding, the harness check, and lockout/tagout are pool questions G0B04, G0B07, G0B08, G0B11, and G0B13.

### Everyday shop hazards

Three questions remain, and they are the quiet, everyday kind. Emergency generators belong **in a well-ventilated area** (G0B09) — outdoors, away from windows and doors — because the killer is **carbon monoxide**: odorless, colorless, and produced by every gasoline engine. A generator in a garage, even with the door open, or on a porch beneath a window can feed exhaust into living space; outside and clear of the house is the only arrangement. The distractors suggesting you insulate the generator from ground or store fuel right beside it are written backwards on purpose. At the workbench, lead-tin solder's danger is **lead contaminating food if hands are not washed carefully after handling the solder** (G0B10): wash your hands after every soldering session, and keep food and drink off the bench entirely. The hazard is chronic rather than acute — lead accumulates in the body — so hygiene, not heroics, is the defense. And inside a big power supply or amplifier, the **interlock** exists **to ensure that dangerous voltages are removed if the cabinet is opened** (G0B12) — a switch on the cover that kills the high voltage the moment the lid lifts. Never defeat it, and remember it only guarantees the input side: filter capacitors hold their charge, which is why chapter 7's bleeder resistor matters. Generator ventilation, solder hygiene, and the interlock round out pool group G0B — G0B09, G0B10, and G0B12.

None of this chapter exists to frighten you off the air. It exists so that the day you flip on 1.5 kW or hang a beam at 50 feet, you have already done the arithmetic and the wiring that make it ordinary. Do the evaluation once, keep it with your station records, revisit it at every change, and the subject is closed. Safe stations are not lucky stations; they are evaluated, bonded, and well-ventilated ones.

**FACT:** RF energy's established effect on body tissue is heating — RF is non-ionizing radiation, so "radiation poisoning" is not the hazard.

**FACT:** All stations with a time-averaged transmission of more than one milliwatt are subject to the FCC's RF-exposure rules.

**FACT:** A station that doesn't meet the exemption criteria must perform an exposure evaluation per FCC OET Bulletin 65, and acceptable ways to show compliance are OET-65 calculation, computer modeling, or measurement with calibrated field-strength equipment — any of the three.

**FACT:** Wire must handle the breaker's rating: a 20 A circuit needs AWG 12 minimum, and AWG 14 wire pairs with a 15 A fuse or breaker.

**FACT:** The lightning-protection ground system goes outside the building, lightning arrestors mount where feed lines enter the building, and all ground rods must be bonded together with the other premises grounds.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G0 — Electrical and RF Safety — is 25 pool questions in two groups (G0A, G0B), and your exam draws two of them, one per group. The ten below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each — including the two questions where "all these choices are correct" is genuinely keyed (G0A02, G0A03) and the one-milliwatt threshold (G0A12).

> **G0A01** What is one way that RF energy can affect human body tissue?
> A. It heats body tissue
> B. It causes radiation poisoning
> C. It causes the blood count to reach a dangerously low level
> D. It cools body tissue
> **Answer: A** — heating is the established effect; RF is non-ionizing, so "radiation poisoning" is borrowed ionizing-radiation language.

> **G0A02** Which of the following is used to determine RF exposure from a transmitted signal?
> A. Its duty cycle
> B. Its frequency
> C. Its power density
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: D** — exposure depends on frequency, power density, and duty cycle, all three — genuinely keyed "all choices" here.

> **G0A03** How can you determine that your station complies with FCC RF exposure regulations?
> A. By calculation based on FCC OET Bulletin 65
> B. By calculation based on computer modeling
> C. By measurement of field strength using calibrated equipment
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: D** — OET-65 calculation, computer modeling, or calibrated measurement: any one of the three shows compliance.

> **G0A06** What must you do if your station fails to meet the FCC RF exposure exemption criteria?
> A. Perform an RF exposure evaluation in accordance with FCC OET Bulletin 65
> B. Contact the FCC for permission to transmit
> C. Perform an RF exposure evaluation in accordance with World Meteorological Organization guidelines
> D. Use an FCC-approved band-pass filter
> **Answer: A** — fail the exemption criteria and the job is an exposure evaluation per FCC OET Bulletin 65.

> **G0A07** What is the effect of modulation duty cycle on RF exposure?
> A. A lower duty cycle permits greater power levels to be transmitted
> B. A higher duty cycle permits greater power levels to be transmitted
> C. Low duty cycle transmitters are exempt from RF exposure evaluation requirements
> D. High duty cycle transmitters are exempt from RF exposure requirements
> **Answer: A** — exposure averages over time, so a lower duty cycle buys headroom for higher power; nothing exempts you.

> **G0A12** What stations are subject to the FCC rules on RF exposure?
> A. All commercial stations; amateur radio stations are exempt
> B. Only stations with antennas lower than one wavelength above the ground
> C. Only stations transmitting more than 500 watts PEP
> D. All stations with a time-averaged transmission of more than one milliwatt
> **Answer: D** — the applicability floor is a time-averaged transmission of more than one milliwatt, which reaches essentially every amateur station.

> **G0B01** Which wire or wires in a four-conductor 240 VAC circuit should be attached to fuses or circuit breakers?
> A. Only the hot wires
> B. Only the neutral wire
> C. Only the ground wire
> D. All wires
> **Answer: A** — overcurrent devices go on the hot wires only; a blown neutral fuse can leave a chassis at line voltage.

> **G0B02** According to the National Electrical Code, what is the minimum wire size that may be used safely for wiring with a 20-ampere circuit breaker?
> A. AWG number 20
> B. AWG number 16
> C. AWG number 12
> D. AWG number 8
> **Answer: C** — the NEC ampacity pair: a 20 A breaker needs AWG 12 minimum, and the inverse pair is AWG 14 with 15 A.

> **G0B05** Which of the following conditions will cause a ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) to disconnect AC power?
> A. Current flowing from one or more of the hot wires to the neutral wire
> B. Current flowing from one or more of the hot wires directly to ground
> C. Overvoltage on the hot wires
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: B** — a GFCI trips on current leaking from a hot wire directly to ground; the missing return current is what it detects.

> **G0B11** Which of the following is required for lightning protection ground rods?
> A. They must be bonded to all buried water and gas lines
> B. Bends in ground wires must be made as close as possible to a right angle
> C. Lightning grounds must be connected to all ungrounded wiring
> D. They must be bonded together with all other grounds
> **Answer: D** — every ground rod bonds together with the other premises grounds; separate "independent" grounds are the hazard.

### Key Takeaways

- RF's established effect on body tissue is heating — RF is non-ionizing radiation, so "radiation poisoning" is the wrong hazard entirely.

- Exposure depends on frequency, power density, and duty cycle — all three; time averaging means the total exposure averaged over a period, so a lower duty cycle permits higher power for the same exposure.

- The exposure rules reach every station with a time-averaged transmission of more than one milliwatt — plan to evaluate, not to claim an exemption.

- Evaluate per FCC OET Bulletin 65 by calculation, computer modeling, or calibrated field-strength measurement — any of the three — then re-evaluate routinely and whenever the station changes, and act to prevent human exposure if a limit could be exceeded.

- In a 240 VAC four-conductor circuit, only the hot wires get fuses or breakers; a 20 A circuit needs AWG 12 minimum, AWG 14 pairs with a 15 A breaker, and the NEC covers the station's electrical safety.

- Lightning grounds live outside the building, arrestors mount where feed lines enter, and all ground rods must be bonded together with the other premises grounds.

- Before climbing, confirm the harness is rated for the climber's weight and within its service life, and lock out and tag every circuit feeding the tower.

- Run generators in well-ventilated areas against carbon monoxide, wash hands after handling lead solder, and never defeat a power-supply interlock.
