## 0. The Upgrade: Why General, and How This Book Works

Maybe it was a 10-meter opening last weekend. The band was alive, your Technician license lets you run SSB on one slice of it, and you worked a station on another continent with the radio and the wire antenna you already own — and when the opening closed, you wanted more. More bands, more modes, more hours of the day when the world is reachable from your shack. That wanting-more is exactly what the General class license is for, and this book is the course that gets you there. In this chapter you'll learn what the General license actually opens, what the single exam between you and it looks like, what happens at the exam session and in the days after you pass — including how you can legally be on the air with your new privileges the very same afternoon — and how this book is organized to take you from licensed Technician to passed General.

### What the General License Opens

The one-sentence version is ARRL's own: "The General class license grants some operating privileges on all Amateur Radio bands and all operating modes. This license opens the door to world-wide communications."

Unpack that against where you stand today. As a Technician, your everyday world is above 30 MHz — the VHF and UHF bands where handhelds, mobile rigs, repeaters, and satellites live, where contacts are local and regional unless a satellite pass or a band opening says otherwise. Below 30 MHz your Technician privileges are real but narrow: CW segments on 80, 40, and 15 meters, plus that one SSB phone slice of 10 meters, all capped at 200 W PEP. You proved you could use them. General removes the fences.

Passing the upgrade exam gives you transmitting privileges on every MF and HF band from the 2200-meter band through 10 meters — phone, CW, and digital **emission modes** in the segments the rules assign to each — and you keep everything above 30 MHz, which Technician, General, Advanced, and Amateur Extra all share. On 160, 60, 30, 17, 12, and 10 meters the General share is the entire band. Power moves up with the spectrum: where your Technician HF segments cap you at 200 W PEP, a General control operator may run up to 1.5 kW PEP on most HF allocations. A few band-specific limits sit below that ceiling — 30 meters stays at 200 W PEP for everyone, and 60 meters has its own power rules — and chapter 1 pins every one of them down exactly.

The honest map has edges, and you should see them now rather than after the exam. On four bands — 80/75, 40, 20, and 15 meters — the lowest slices of each band remain Advanced and Amateur Extra territory. General does not convey everything; it conveys some operating privileges on every band, which is a different and genuinely huge thing. Hold the shape of it: every band, every mode family, worldwide reach, full legal power — with a handful of Extra-only slices that give you a reason to keep climbing later.

Why is it worth the effort? Because of what those **privileges** feel like on the air. HF phone — SSB voice crossing oceans on ordinary antennas — becomes routine instead of a special event. The digital segments open wide on every band: FT8, RTTY, PSK31, and their relatives all have room to breathe. Contests and DXing stop being spectator sports. And the deeper theory you pick up along the way makes you measurably better at running the station you already have.

### The Upgrade Exam: Element 3

First, the best news in this chapter: you take one exam, not two.

**FACT:** To upgrade from Technician to General you pass examination Element 3 only — "General Class operator: Elements 2 and 3" — because your Technician license already credits Element 2.

The exam you passed for your Technician ticket was Element 2, and the FCC credits it toward General; the only new hurdle is **Element 3**, the written exam on General-class privileges. One practical caution hides inside that credit: it rests on your Technician license being unexpired or inside its renewal grace period. If your ten-year term is anywhere near up, renew first and upgrade second.

The exam's shape will feel familiar, because it is built exactly like the one you already passed.

**FACT:** "Element 3: 35 questions concerning the privileges of a General Class operator license. The minimum passing score is 26 questions answered correctly."

Thirty-five questions, and 26 correct answers pass — you can miss nine and still walk out an upgrader. The exam is drawn from the public **question pool**: 423 questions organized into ten **subelements**, G1 through G9 plus G0 — rules, operating procedures, propagation, amateur radio practices, electrical principles, circuit components, practical circuits, signals and emissions, antennas and feed lines, and safety. Each subelement divides into **groups**, 35 in all, and your exam draws exactly one question from each group, so every exam is automatically balanced across every topic. No surprise questions exist, no out-of-pool material, no tricks: every question on every General exam is printed verbatim in Appendix A of this book, with the keyed answer and a one-line explanation of why.

Now the date that matters, stated as plainly as we can put it.

**FACT:** The 2023–2027 General Class (Element 3) pool is effective for exams from 2023-07-01 and is valid until 2027-06-30, when the successor pool takes effect (four-year rotation; no pools updated or released in 2025 or 2029).

**This book tracks the 2023–2027 pool, and that pool expires on 2027-06-30.** Until that date, the questions you will face match this book exactly. The pool is written and released into the public domain by the NCVEC Question Pool Committee — the same NCVEC whose Technician pool you studied — and this book carries the current document, with all six errata releases incorporated. Over the pool's life, nine questions were withdrawn; those numbers simply do not exist anymore, and Appendix A skips them exactly as the official pool files do, so never go hunting for them. If you are reading this late in the pool's life, the honest advice is simple: schedule your exam before the window closes, because after 2027-06-30 a successor pool takes over and this book's exam-facing material belongs to the old one.

### Exam Day, for Upgraders

The logistics are lighter than your first trip through the system, because most of the identity work is already done. You have an **FRN** — the FCC Registration Number you registered in CORES for your Technician grant — and it stays with you for life, so there is no new registration step. (If you somehow never obtained one, registering in CORES carries no fee and no exam requirement.)

Finding a session works the way you remember. Exams are administered by teams of at least three VEs — Volunteer Examiners — working under a VEC, a Volunteer Examiner Coordinator, and two good finders list their sessions: the ARRL's online exam-session finder and hamstudy.org/sessions. Some teams run remote video-supervised exams online alongside the in-person kind, but whether remote testing is available depends entirely on the individual VE team, so treat it as a question to ask a team, never a plan to count on.

The session fee, for calendar 2026: the ARRL VEC charges $15.00.

That one fee pays for one attempt at each of the three exam elements, so an ambitious afternoon could legally include Element 4 as well — pass more than one element at a sitting and the VEC sends the FCC a single application reflecting the highest class you earned. Candidates younger than 18 pay a reduced session fee of five dollars, and Laurel VEC charges nothing at all for its exams.

Then comes the line item upgraders get to skip.

**FACT:** The FCC's $35 application fee (effective April 19, 2022) applies per application to new-license, renewal, rule-waiver, and vanity-call-sign applications — and upgrade applications are exempt, so an upgrader has no FCC payment step and no 10-day payment window.

If you remember the Technician-course drill — wait for the FCC's email, pay online within ten days — you can let it go. Your upgrade has no FCC payment step at all; the session fee you pay the VE team is the only money in the whole transaction.

What do you bring? One legal photo ID — or two forms of non-photo ID if you have none — your FRN, and the session fee. The application itself, the NCVEC Quick-Form 605, is completed at the session; the 2022 edition is current, and on it your email address and FRN are mandatory. The email matters because the FCC sends all of its correspondence, including your license, by email — so give them an address you actually read. When you hand in your answer sheet the VEs grade it immediately, right there. Pass, and three VEs certify that you are qualified and issue your **CSCE**, the Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination — the same piece of paper you earned once before, and your proof of the pass for the next 365 days.

### After You Pass: Operate Immediately, with /AG

Here is the upgrader's perk, and it is the biggest difference from your first license. When you passed Element 2, you had to wait for your grant to appear in the FCC's Universal Licensing System — the ULS — before you could legally transmit, because a brand-new licensee has no operating authority until the grant shows in the database. You are not a brand-new licensee. Under FCC rule §97.9(b), once two things have happened — your Form 605 requesting the higher operator class has been properly submitted to the administering VEs, and you hold the CSCE showing you passed the required exam — you are authorized to exercise your new General privileges immediately: before the VEC files anything, before ULS changes, before any email arrives. That **upgrade authority** runs until final disposition of the application or 365 days after you pass, whichever comes first.

That authority comes with an identification requirement, and the two are never separated: while you operate under §97.9(b) you must append an indicator to your call sign.

**FACT:** While operating under §97.9(b) upgrade authority the station must append an indicator to the call sign — "for a control operator who has requested a license modification from Novice or Technician to General Class: AG" — separated from the call sign by the slant mark (/) or any suitable word denoting it.

In practice — one VEC's plain reading of the rule — on phone you say your call sign followed by "temporary AG," as in "This is KX9ABC temporary AG," and on CW or digital modes you sign KX9ABC/AG. The suffix is the honest signal that you are exercising privileges your database entry does not show yet. Use your new privileges only with the indicator attached, and only while both conditions hold: Form 605 properly submitted, CSCE in hand.

The rest of the wait is calm. The VE team sends your application to the coordinating VEC, which screens it and forwards everything to the FCC electronically. Your ULS record typically updates within days — typically, not a promise — and the day it shows General, you drop the /AG and never say it again. Two more things stay exactly as they were. Your call sign does not change: the station is reassigned its same call sign when the license is modified, unless you yourself apply for a new one. And your anniversary dates are untouched — the upgrade modifies your existing license rather than starting a new one. Keep the contrast with the Technician course in your pocket, because you will meet new hams who get it wrong: a first-time licensee has no authority at all until the grant appears in ULS, while an existing licensee upgrading operates immediately under §97.9(b). You are the second kind.

### How This Book Works

The architecture is the one that worked for you in the Technician course, retargeted at the General pool. The map is one pool subelement per chapter: G1, the commission's rules, is chapter 1; G2, operating procedures, is chapter 2; and so on in order through G0, electrical and RF safety, which is chapter 10. Every teaching chapter ends with an Exam Focus — a set of verbatim pool questions from that chapter's subelement, each with its keyed answer and a one-line why — so you always know when you have a subelement cold. This chapter is the one exception: it owns no pool questions, so it closes with a checklist instead.

Behind the chapters sit the reference tools. Appendix A carries all 423 pool questions verbatim, in pool order, each with its answer and a one-line explanation naming the chapter that teaches it — your drill ground once the reading is done. Appendix B is the glossary and the General formula set with worked micro-examples. And the book's practice page draws a realistic practice test from the pool whenever you like: 35 questions, one per group, exactly like the real session. Take them until you are passing comfortably, then go sit for the real thing.

Notation is the same convention you already know. Prose in this book writes V for volts and the × sign for multiplication — V = I × R — while the General pool states electrical quantities in words, so the verbatim quotes and the prose never clash. Unit case remains load-bearing: kHz with a lowercase k, MHz with a capital M, mA, pF, exactly as the pool prints them.

What the book assumes is you — a licensed Technician. Ohm's law, repeater basics, Part 97 fundamentals, how a VEC session runs: those are yours already, and they stay in the background where you left them. (If any of it has gone fuzzy, the Technician course — *Your First Ham License: The Technician Course* — is the refresher.) What is new is depth. The math is real this time: reactance and resonance, decibels as honest arithmetic, SWR, peak envelope power. It is all taught step by step, with worked examples using the pool's own numbers, and none of it requires anything you did not already prove you could learn.

And because upgraders think ahead: after General comes Amateur Extra, the class that holds those lowest slices of 80/75, 40, 20, and 15 meters. Extra means passing Element 4 — 50 questions, 37 correct to pass — drawn from its own pool, valid for exams from July 1, 2024 through June 30, 2028. Your Element 3 CSCE counts toward Extra for 365 days, so if the bug bites, the next exam can happen while this one is still warm.

### Your Upgrade Checklist

Everything in this chapter, compressed into what you can actually do, starting today:

1. Confirm your FRN and the email address on your FCC registration are current.

2. Find an exam session through the ARRL session finder or hamstudy.org/sessions — and ask the VE team directly if you need a remote option.

3. Read chapters 1 through 10 in order; each one owns one pool subelement.

4. Drill each subelement in Appendix A, then take practice tests on the book's practice page until you are passing comfortably.

5. The night before, gather your photo ID, your FRN, and the session fee.

6. Pass Element 3 and walk out with your CSCE.

7. Operate on your new General privileges immediately — Form 605 submitted to the VEs, CSCE in hand, signing /AG on every transmission that uses them.

8. Watch ULS for the General grant, and drop the /AG suffix the day it appears.

9. Start planning the antenna your new privileges deserve.

### Key Takeaways

- The General license grants some operating privileges on every amateur band and every mode — the door to worldwide HF — while the lowest slices of 80/75, 40, 20, and 15 meters remain Extra territory.

- You pass Element 3 only: 35 questions drawn one per group from the public 423-question pool, with 26 correct answers needed to pass.

- The 2023–2027 pool is fixed and public, every exam question is in Appendix A verbatim — and the pool expires 2027-06-30, so plan to test inside the window.

- Upgrades are exempt from the FCC's $35 application fee: no payment step, no ten-day payment window, no waiting on a fee email.

- With your Form 605 properly submitted to the administering VEs and your CSCE in hand, you may use your new privileges immediately — signing /AG until your ULS record shows General.

- You keep your call sign, your ULS record typically updates within days, and your Element 3 CSCE counts toward Amateur Extra for 365 days.
