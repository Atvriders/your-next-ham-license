## 9. Antennas & Feedlines at General Depth

On HF, the wire in the air matters more than the box on the desk. As a Technician you could treat the antenna as an accessory — the repeater did the heavy lifting — but a General's signal has to cross a continent or an ocean on its own, and now the antenna and the feed line that connects it to the radio *are* the station. In this chapter you'll learn what makes a feed line a transmission line, what standing waves and SWR actually measure, how high to hang a dipole and how long to cut it, what a Yagi buys you and how to read a gain claim in dBi or dBd, and a whole zoo of specialized antennas from NVIS to the Beverage. This is pool subelement G9: 46 questions, four of which will be on your exam.

### Feed lines: characteristic impedance and loss

Book 2 taught you that coax runs from the radio to the antenna and that 50-ohm coax is the usual choice. Now the line itself gets a working principle. A feed line is a **transmission line** — a pair of conductors whose geometry gives it a **characteristic impedance**, the impedance it presents to energy traveling along it. For a parallel-conductor line, that impedance is set by exactly two things: **the distance between the centers of the conductors and the radius of the conductors** (G9A01). Not by the line's length — a 10-foot piece and a 100-foot piece of the same cable have the same characteristic impedance — and not by the frequency you put on it. Geometry is destiny: cut a line in half and its characteristic impedance does not change.

Two feed-line families cover nearly every amateur station. **Coaxial cable** is the unbalanced family: a center conductor inside a tubular braided shield, the two separated by a plastic dielectric, all under an outer jacket. Amateur coax is built to about 50 ohms, and it is forgiving to route — tape it to a tower leg, coil the spare behind the desk, and it behaves the same. **Ladder line**, and its flat plastic cousin **window line**, is the balanced family: two parallel wires held at a fixed spacing by spacers, with air as most of the dielectric. That wide, air-spaced geometry is why window line's nominal characteristic impedance is approximately **450 ohms** (G9A03) — a number the pool asks for directly, and far above coax's 50. Balanced line needs to run clear of metal, but it rewards the trouble with strikingly lower loss.

One more line property rides along: the **velocity factor**. RF travels more slowly inside a cable than in free space — about 0.66 times the speed of light in solid-dielectric coax, closer to 0.9 in ladder line — so a physical foot of cable is electrically longer than a foot of air. You never need the number for this exam; it matters on the workbench, when you cut a matching stub or a phasing section to an electrical length.

{{fig:ch09-feedline-basics}}

Loss is the feed line's tax, and the pool pins its unit and its behavior. Feed-line loss is usually expressed in **decibels per 100 feet** (G9A06), and coax's attenuation **increases with frequency** (G9A05) — a cable that is nearly lossless at 3.5 MHz can eat a third of your power at 144 MHz. That is why 80-meter operators shrug at almost any coax while VHF operators buy the expensive stuff. Two subtler effects round out the group. First, **high SWR increases loss in a lossy transmission line** (G9A02): with standing waves on the line, energy sloshes back and forth and pays the loss toll on every pass, so a mismatched antenna on a lossy cable costs you twice. Second, **higher line loss reduces the SWR measured at the input to the line** (G9A11): the reflected wave is attenuated on its way back to your meter, so a long, lossy run can make a badly mismatched antenna look respectable in the shack. Trust the SWR you measure at the antenna end; the shack-end reading is flattered by the cable.

Chapter 5's decibel anchors make the unit tangible. A cable losing 3 dB per 100 feet throws away half your power in every 100-foot run — 100 W in, 50 W out — and 6 dB per 100 feet leaves only a quarter. Check a cable's loss at the highest band you plan to use, because the G9A05 frequency climb means a line that is generous on 40 meters may be greedy on 2 meters. And when you do choose ladder line for its low loss, remember its impedance is 450 ohms, not 50: it pairs naturally with a tuner and a balanced antenna like the center-fed doublet, while coax pairs naturally with everything else.

That chain — geometry, 450 ohms, dB per 100 feet, and the two subtle effects — is pool questions G9A01 through G9A03, G9A05, G9A06, and G9A11.

### Standing waves, SWR, and matching

Book 2 left SWR at "it exists, and 1:1 is perfect." Here is what it actually is. Your transmitter sends **forward power** up the line toward the antenna. If the load at the far end is exactly the line's characteristic impedance, every watt that arrives is absorbed and radiated, and the line is quiet. But if the antenna's **feed-point impedance** differs from the line's, part of the arriving energy has nowhere to go and turns around: that **reflected power** is caused by **a difference between feed line impedance and antenna feed point impedance** (G9A04). The forward and reflected waves pass through each other, alternately adding and canceling, and the result is a **standing wave** — a fixed pattern of voltage maxima and minima along the line. The ratio of the maximum to the minimum is the **standing wave ratio**, SWR. A perfect match reflects nothing, the voltage along the line is flat, and the SWR is 1:1.

The cure follows straight from the cause: to prevent standing waves on a feed line connected to an antenna, **the antenna feed point impedance must be matched to the characteristic impedance of the feed line** (G9A07). Matching can happen at the antenna — the right length, the right feed arrangement, a matching device at the feed point — or with a network somewhere along the line. What matters for the exam is that the mismatch lives at the boundary between the line and the antenna; that is where the reflection is born.

{{fig:ch09-standing-waves}}

Read the figure's numbers and the ratio stops being abstract. A 100 Ω load on a 50 Ω line reflects a third of the arriving voltage. Where forward and reflected waves add, the voltage peaks at four-thirds of the forward wave; where they cancel, it dips to two-thirds. The standing wave ratio is simply the maximum divided by the minimum — four-thirds over two-thirds, or 2:1 — the same answer the impedance division 100 ÷ 50 gives, because the two descriptions are one physics. On a lossless line that ratio is the same everywhere along the run: it is set once, at the load, and only real-world line loss softens it toward the shack end (that was G9A11).

When the mismatch is purely resistive, the SWR is one division problem. Divide the larger impedance by the smaller — $SWR = Z_{load}/Z_0$ or its inverse, whichever comes out at or above 1 — and state the answer with the larger number first. Always. The convention is load-bearing on the exam: 4:1 is an answer choice and 1:4 is a distractor, and they do not mean the same thing.

> **Worked example:** A 200 Ω resistive load on 50 Ω coax: 200 ÷ 50 = **4:1** (G9A09). A 10 Ω resistive load on the same coax: 50 ÷ 10 = **5:1** (G9A10) — divide the bigger number by the smaller either way, so the ratio comes out at 1 or above, and state it larger-number-first. Notice that the 10 Ω load reflects *more* than the 200 Ω load: it is five times below the line impedance instead of four times above.

Now the trap that catches every new General once. Suppose the SWR on your feed line is 5:1, and you adjust a matching network — an antenna tuner — at the transmitter end until it presents 1:1 to the transmitter. What is the SWR on the feed line now? Still **5:1** (G9A08). The tuner changed nothing at the antenna end, so the line between the tuner and the antenna is just as mismatched as before; the standing waves are still there, and a lossy line still pays the extra G9A02 loss for carrying them. What the tuner did is real, but different: it presents the transmitter with its design load, so the transmitter can deliver full power into the system without folding back or overheating. Chapter 4's tuner questions teach the same lesson from the shack side — the tuner makes the radio happy; it does not repair the antenna.

> **Sidebar: what a Smith chart shows.** If you go farther with antennas you will meet the Smith chart — a circular map of impedance and reflection used for matching design. Every possible combination of resistance and reactance is a point on the chart; a mismatched antenna traces a circle around the center as you move along the feed line, and a designer adds a stub here or a capacitor there to walk the point into the center, which is the perfect match. That is the whole story for this book: the General pool does not test the Smith chart at all, so treat this as orientation, not exam prep — no figure, no drill.

Reflected power, the standing wave it builds, the one-division SWR calculation, and the tuner trap — those are pool questions G9A04 and G9A07 through G9A10.

### Dipoles, verticals, and the 468 formula

You already own the two basic HF antennas from Book 2: the half-wave dipole and the quarter-wave vertical. Now they get patterns. A dipole in free space, viewed in a plane containing the conductor, radiates a **figure-eight pattern at right angles to the antenna** (G9B04): maximum signal **broadside to the wire**, nulls off the ends. Aim a dipole at Europe and you put a null there — orient the wire so its broadside faces where you want to talk. A quarter-wave **ground-plane vertical** is the opposite shape: **omnidirectional in azimuth** (G9B03), equal signal in every compass direction, which is why verticals are the default when you cannot choose a favored direction.

One correction to a common mental picture: the figure-eight is a cross-section, not the whole pattern. Rotate that cross-section around the wire and you get the true three-dimensional shape — a doughnut with the wire poked through its hole. Maximum radiation fills the plane broadside to the wire in every direction around it, and the nulls sit only along the wire's own axis. So a horizontal dipole's nulls point at whatever its two ends point at: run the wire east–west and your signal favors north and south, with deep nulls toward the east and west horizons.

Height is the third dimension, and it is measured in wavelengths, not feet — the same wire is low on 80 meters and high on 10. Below a half-wavelength up, a horizontal dipole's high-angle pattern is **almost omnidirectional** (G9B05): the low dipole throws most of its energy nearly straight up and blankets the region around you instead of reaching the horizon. Its feed-point impedance **steadily decreases** as height drops toward a tenth of a wavelength (G9B07). Raise the antenna to half a wavelength or more and the pattern flattens toward the horizon — the low takeoff angles that make long skip and DX. The figure draws both cases, and they are the same physics as the NVIS antenna you will meet shortly.

{{fig:ch09-antenna-heights}}

Where you feed the antenna matters as much as where you hang it. A dipole's feed-point impedance **steadily increases as the feed point moves from the center toward the ends** (G9B08) — the center is the low-impedance point, the ends are high-impedance, and that one sentence explains both the popularity of the center-fed dipole and the matching problem of the end-fed antenna coming up in G9D. The ground-plane vertical has its own adjustment: with its radials horizontal its feed point sits near 35 ohms, and the common way to raise it to approximately 50 ohms is to **slope the radials downward** (G9B02). For a ground-mounted vertical, the radials go **on the surface or buried a few inches below the ground** (G9B06) — they are a ground screen for the antenna field, not an earth connection, so deep burial is wasted digging. And if you can choose a polarization at HF, horizontal's advantage is **lower ground losses** (G9B09) — verticals lose more power into the ground beneath them.

One warning belongs in every new General's ear. A **random wire** connected directly to the transmitter — no tuner, no transformer, just a wire into the antenna jack — can leave **station equipment carrying significant RF current** (G9B01). The rig, the key, and the microphone become part of the antenna's return path, which is how operators collect RF burns on transmit. Give the wire a proper match and a proper ground and the problem goes away.

Then there is the arithmetic every dipole builder uses and the exam expects: the approximate length of a half-wave dipole in feet is $L = 468 / f$ with f in MHz. The 468 constant is shorter than the free-space half wavelength because it already includes the roughly 5 percent **end effect** — the wire's end loading that lengthens the antenna electrically — so the answer is approximate by design, and the pool says "approximate" in the question. Builders cut the wire a foot long and trim to resonance: the formula lands you in the right neighborhood, and the antenna analyzer from chapter 4 walks you the rest of the way.

> **Worked example:** A half-wave dipole cut for 14.250 MHz: 468 ÷ 14.250 = 32.8 — approximately **33 feet** (G9B10). The same formula at 3.550 MHz: 468 ÷ 3.550 = 131.8 — approximately **132 feet** (G9B11), which is why 80-meter dipoles need a big yard. A quarter-wave monopole for 28.5 MHz: 234 ÷ 28.5 = 8.2 — approximately **8 feet** (G9B12), the 234 constant being half of 468 for the quarter-wave case.

Patterns, height, feed-point position, radials, polarization, the random-wire warning, and the two length formulas — that is all twelve questions of pool group G9B.

### Yagis, gain, and matching devices

Add elements to a dipole and it becomes a beam. A **Yagi** is a row of elements mounted along a **boom**: one **driven element** — approximately a half wavelength long (G9C02), the only one connected to the feed line — with a **reflector** behind it and one or more **directors** ahead. The parasitic elements are not fed at all; they reshape the pattern by re-radiating the driven element's field, and their lengths follow one rule the pool drills: **the reflector is longer, and the director is shorter, than the driven element** (G9C03). The mnemonic is "the reflector is the big one behind" — the beam fires off the director end, away from the reflector. Lengthen the boom and add directors and the pattern sharpens: **gain increases** (G9C05).

Beams speak their own pattern language. The **main lobe** is **the direction of maximum radiated field strength from the antenna** (G9C08) — where the beam points. The **front-to-back ratio** is **the power radiated in the major lobe compared to that in the opposite direction** (G9C07), how well the beam rejects what is behind it. Both quantities can be tuned: forward gain, front-to-back ratio, and SWR bandwidth all respond to boom length, element count, and element spacing — all these choices are correct (G9C10) — and **larger-diameter elements increase a Yagi's bandwidth** (G9C01). Fatter elements, wider band; longer boom, more gain; and no setting maximizes everything at once.

Why bother with front-to-back at all? Because a beam listens the way it talks. A good front-to-back ratio means the station thirty degrees off your back corner fades while you work the station dead ahead — on a crowded band, rejection behind you is worth as much as gain ahead. And those same parasitic elements that shape the pattern also drag the driven element's feed-point impedance well below 50 ohms, which is why practical Yagis almost always need one of the matching devices at the end of this section.

{{fig:ch09-yagi-stack}}

Gain claims come in two units, and the exam checks that you know which is the bigger number. Gain in **dBi** is measured against a theoretical isotropic radiator; gain in **dBd** is measured against a half-wave dipole, which itself has 2.15 dB of gain over isotropic. So $dBi = dBd + 2.15$ — the dBi figure is always 2.15 dB **higher** (G9C04), which is why advertisers prefer it.

> **Worked example:** A triband Yagi advertised at 5 dBd is the same antenna as 5 + 2.15 = 7.15 dBi. And a manufacturer quoting 8 dBi for a beam that measures 5.85 dBd is not lying — the units differ, and dBi is the bigger number every time.

Two identical antennas, stacked and fed in phase, buy you the doubling you would expect: two three-element Yagis spaced a half wavelength apart typically show approximately **3 dB higher** gain than one (G9C09). That is chapter 5's anchor working in the air — +3 dB doubles power, and two beams capture twice the power of one — with the antenna system's side benefit that the stacked pair squeezes the main lobe flatter in elevation.

The Yagi's driven element rarely presents 50 ohms once the parasitics pull its impedance down, so beams need matching devices, and the pool names two. A **beta or hairpin match** is **a shorted transmission line stub placed at the feed point** (G9C11) — a U-shaped bar of wire across the feed point that cancels the mismatch. A **gamma match** is a capacitor-and-rod arrangement tapping the driven element off center, and its practical characteristic is that **it does not require the driven element to be insulated from the boom** (G9C12) — a mechanical convenience on a metal-boom beam. Anatomy, lobes, dBi versus dBd, stacking, and the two matches are pool group G9C.

### The specialized zoo

Pool group G9D is a dozen questions on a dozen antennas, each a one-line idea. Tour them in a sensible order and each becomes a vocabulary word with a picture attached.

First, the antenna that breaks every DX instinct. A **near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS)** antenna is **a horizontal dipole placed between 1/10 and 1/4 wavelength above the ground** (G9D01) — deliberately *low*, the exact opposite of the high dipole you want for DX, because its job is to fire nearly straight up and let the ionosphere fold the signal back down over the surrounding few hundred miles. That is short-skip regional coverage on bands like 40 meters during the day, and chapter 3 owns the propagation mode; here you own the height. At the feed-point extreme sits the **end-fed half-wave**: fed at the end of the wire, where the impedance peaks, it presents a **very high** feed-point impedance (G9D02) — G9B08's trend taken to its limit, and the reason end-feds ship with a matching transformer.

The **halo** is a VHF/UHF favorite, a loop bent almost closed, and its maximum radiation is **omnidirectional in the plane of the halo** (G9D03) — horizontal and equal in every direction, which is why mobile weak-signal operators like it. For multiband HF on one wire, **antenna traps** — parallel resonant circuits inserted into the element — exist **to enable multiband operation** (G9D04): each trap isolates the outer wire on its band, so one dipole plays on several. The bill for multiband convenience arrives at the transmitter: a disadvantage of multiband antennas is **poor harmonic rejection** (G9D11), since an antenna resonant on several bands will happily radiate your harmonics on any of them. Chapter 4's harmonic filters earn their keep.

Back with beams for two entries. Vertically stacking horizontally polarized Yagis **narrows the main lobe in elevation** (G9D05) — the stacked pair from G9C09 squeezes its pattern flat toward the horizon. And the **log-periodic** is the bandwidth king: its advantage is **wide bandwidth** (G9D06), and its signature construction is that **element length and spacing vary logarithmically along the boom** (G9D07) — a whole ladder of elements, each resonant somewhere across a broad range, trading the Yagi's concentrated gain for coverage of huge frequency spans.

Then the mobile and receiving exotica. The **screwdriver antenna** — the motorized mobile whip with the distinctive coil at its base — adjusts its feed point impedance **by varying the base loading inductance** (G9D08), the motor driving the coil in and out to retune band to band. The **Beverage** is a long, low wire you will only ever use for one thing: **directional receiving for the MF and low HF bands** (G9D09) — too lossy to transmit through, wonderfully quiet to listen on, and a favorite of 160-meter operators. The **electrically small loop** — less than 1/10 wavelength in circumference — is the pattern mirror of the dipole, and it is the pool's favorite swap: its nulls are **broadside to the loop** (G9D10). Picture both: the dipole's *maxima* are broadside to the wire with nulls off the ends, while the loop's *nulls* are broadside to the loop with maxima in its plane. Students trade the two constantly; keep maxima-with-dipole and nulls-with-loop nailed down and the question is free. Last and simplest, a dipole with **a single central support** — ends sloping down from one mast or tree — is an **inverted V** (G9D12), the one-pole antenna farm. That completes pool group G9D, and with it subelement G9.

Step back and the subelement is one argument told three ways. The feed line is a defined electrical environment whose impedance comes from its geometry; a mismatch at the antenna end reflects power, and the reflection is what SWR measures; and every antenna in the chapter is a different strategy for shaping where the accepted power goes — broadside from a dipole, straight up from an NVIS wire, ahead from a Yagi, everywhere from a vertical. Match the line to the load, cut the wire by the formula, hang it at the height that serves your purpose, and the station in the air finally earns the privileges on the license.

**FACT:** The approximate length of a half-wave dipole in feet is 468 divided by the frequency in MHz (33 feet for 14.250 MHz, 132 feet for 3.550 MHz), and a quarter-wave monopole is 234 divided by the frequency in MHz (8 feet for 28.5 MHz) — the 468 constant includes the roughly 5 percent end-effect shortening.

**FACT:** SWR from a resistive mismatch is Z_load ÷ Z₀ or Z₀ ÷ Z_load, whichever is greater than or equal to 1 — 200 Ω on 50 Ω coax is 4:1, and 10 Ω on 50 Ω coax is 5:1 — and SWR is always stated with the larger number first.

**FACT:** Antenna gain in dBi is 2.15 dB higher than the same gain in dBd — dBi = dBd + 2.15, dBi the bigger number.

**FACT:** A free-space half-wave dipole radiates a figure-eight pattern broadside to the wire with nulls off the ends, while a quarter-wave ground plane is omnidirectional in azimuth.

**FACT:** Two identical Yagis stacked a half-wavelength apart gain approximately 3 dB over one.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G9 — Antennas and Feed Lines — is 46 pool questions in four groups (G9A, G9B, G9C, G9D), and your exam draws four of them, one per group. The ten below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each — including the classic tuner trap, both SWR calculations, and the loop-pattern mirror.

> **G9A03** What is the nominal characteristic impedance of “window line” transmission line?
> A. 50 ohms
> B. 75 ohms
> C. 100 ohms
> D. 450 ohms
> **Answer: D** — window line's wide, air-spaced conductors give a nominal 450 ohms — far above coax's 50.

> **G9A08** If the SWR on an antenna feed line is 5:1, and a matching network at the transmitter end of the feed line is adjusted to present a 1:1 SWR to the transmitter, what is the resulting SWR on the feed line?
> A. 1:1
> B. 5:1
> C. Between 1:1 and 5:1 depending on the characteristic impedance of the line
> D. Between 1:1 and 5:1 depending on the reflected power at the transmitter
> **Answer: B** — the matching network only changes what the transmitter sees; the antenna end is still mismatched, so the line still runs 5:1 — taught in chapter 4.

> **G9A09** What standing wave ratio results from connecting a 50-ohm feed line to a 200-ohm resistive load?
> A. 4:1
> B. 1:4
> C. 2:1
> D. 1:2
> **Answer: A** — divide the larger by the smaller: 200 ÷ 50 = 4, and SWR is always stated larger-number-first.

> **G9A10** What standing wave ratio results from connecting a 50-ohm feed line to a 10-ohm resistive load?
> A. 2:1
> B. 1:2
> C. 1:5
> D. 5:1
> **Answer: D** — 50 ÷ 10 = 5 — the same larger-over-smaller division, stated 5:1, never 1:5.

> **G9B04** What is the radiation pattern of a dipole antenna in free space in a plane containing the conductor?
> A. It is a figure-eight at right angles to the antenna
> B. It is a figure-eight off both ends of the antenna
> C. It is a circle (equal radiation in all directions)
> D. It has a pair of lobes on one side of the antenna and a single lobe on the other side
> **Answer: A** — a free-space dipole radiates broadside to the wire, with nulls off the ends.

> **G9B10** What is the approximate length for a 1/2 wave dipole antenna cut for 14.250 MHz?
> A. 8 feet
> B. 16 feet
> C. 24 feet
> D. 33 feet
> **Answer: D** — 468 ÷ 14.250 ≈ 32.8 feet — approximately 33 feet.

> **G9C04** How does antenna gain in dBi compare to gain stated in dBd for the same antenna?
> A. Gain in dBi is 2.15 dB lower
> B. Gain in dBi is 2.15 dB higher
> C. Gain in dBd is 1.25 dBd lower
> D. Gain in dBd is 1.25 dBd higher
> **Answer: B** — dBi = dBd + 2.15, and dBi is always the bigger number.

> **G9C09** In free space, how does the gain of two three-element, horizontally polarized Yagi antennas spaced vertically 1/2 wavelength apart typically compare to the gain of a single three-element Yagi?
> A. Approximately 1.5 dB higher
> B. Approximately 3 dB higher
> C. Approximately 6 dB higher
> D. Approximately 9 dB higher
> **Answer: B** — two identical Yagis capture twice the power, and +3 dB is a doubling — taught in chapter 5.

> **G9D02** What is the feed point impedance of an end-fed half-wave antenna?
> A. Very low
> B. Approximately 50 ohms
> C. Approximately 300 ohms
> D. Very high
> **Answer: D** — fed at the end rather than the center, a half-wave antenna presents a very high feed-point impedance.

> **G9D10** In which direction or directions does an electrically small loop (less than 1/10 wavelength in circumference) have nulls in its radiation pattern?
> A. In the plane of the loop
> B. Broadside to the loop
> C. Broadside and in the plane of the loop
> D. Electrically small loops are omnidirectional
> **Answer: B** — the small loop's nulls are broadside to the loop — the mirror of the dipole, whose maxima are broadside to the wire.

### Key Takeaways

- A parallel-conductor feed line's characteristic impedance is set by conductor spacing and conductor radius — never by length or frequency. Window/ladder line is approximately 450 ohms; amateur coax is about 50.

- Coax loss rises with frequency and is quoted in dB per 100 feet. High SWR makes a lossy line lose more, and line loss makes the SWR you measure in the shack look better than it really is at the antenna.

- SWR from a resistive mismatch is the larger impedance divided by the smaller — 200 Ω on 50 Ω is 4:1, 10 Ω on 50 Ω is 5:1 — always stated larger-number-first. A tuner at the transmitter presents 1:1 to the radio but leaves the line's SWR unchanged.

- A dipole's maxima are broadside to the wire with nulls off the ends; a small loop's *nulls* are broadside to the loop. The exam swaps these constantly — do not let it.

- Dipole length in feet is 468 divided by the frequency in MHz; a quarter-wave monopole is 234 divided by the frequency. Both are approximate by design — the 468 constant includes the end effect.

- On a Yagi, the reflector is longer and the director shorter than the driven element; dBi = dBd + 2.15 with dBi the bigger number; and two identical Yagis stacked a half wavelength apart gain approximately 3 dB over one.
