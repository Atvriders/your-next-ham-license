## 7. Practical Circuits

Follow the 120 V from the wall outlet: through the power cord, into a supply that turns it into clean DC, through the oscillator and amplifier stages that create and boost a radio signal, past the filters that shape it, and out the jack on the back of the rig as RF on its way to the antenna. Every radio you own is that journey wired into a box. In this chapter you'll learn how each stage along the way actually works — how rectifiers and filters turn AC into DC, how to read the pool's one and only schematic figure, why an amplifier's class decides which modes it can serve, and how a software-defined radio moves the whole chain into code. This is pool subelement G7: 38 questions, three of which will be on your exam — and five of the 38 point at a single schematic.

### The linear power supply, opened up

Book 2 treated the power supply as a box: wall AC in, 13.8 V DC out, done. Now the box gets its working principle. A **linear power supply** is a four-stage chain — transformer, rectifier, filter, regulator — and the figure shows the waveform at each handoff, because the waveform is the whole story.

{{fig:ch07-power-supply}}

First the **transformer** steps the 120 V line AC down toward the voltage the rig wants and isolates your equipment from the mains (chapter 5 worked the turns-ratio math). What leaves the transformer is still AC — a smooth sine wave swinging positive and negative — and the **rectifier**'s job is to make it one-directional. The tool is the diode from chapter 6, a one-way valve for current, and the simplest arrangement is the **half-wave rectifier**: a single diode in series with the load. Only one diode is required (G7A04), and it conducts for half of each AC cycle — **180 degrees** (G7A05) — passing the positive half and blocking the negative half. The output is DC in the sense that it never reverses, but it arrives as pulses with dead gaps between them.

The **full-wave rectifier** fixes the gaps by flipping the negative half of every cycle upright, so it converts the full **360 degrees** of each cycle (G7A06). Every half-cycle now delivers a pulse, which is why the unfiltered output of a full-wave rectifier into a resistive load is a series of DC pulses at **twice the frequency of the AC input** (G7A07) — the **ripple frequency** doubles. Twice as many pulses per second means twice as many chances for the filter to refill its reservoir, and that is exactly why full-wave output is easier to filter smooth.

> **Worked example:** A 60 Hz mains transformer feeding a full-wave rectifier: both halves of every cycle get flipped upright, so the raw DC pulses arrive at 2 × 60 = 120 Hz. The filter capacitor gets 120 charging chances per second — twice what a half-wave supply gives it — which is why full-wave supplies need less capacitance for the same ripple.

Two circuits produce full-wave output, and the pool keeps them straight by counting diodes. The classic **center-tapped** circuit uses two diodes and a **center-tapped transformer** — the transformer's secondary is tapped at its midpoint, and each diode serves one half of the winding on alternate half-cycles. Asked which rectifier type uses two diodes and a center-tapped transformer, the keyed answer is simply **full-wave** (G7A03). The **full-wave bridge** is the other circuit, and it uses **four** diodes. Two diodes means center-tap, four means bridge — the distractors are built to blur that count.

After the rectifier comes the **filter**, built from **capacitors and inductors** (G7A02). The filter capacitor charges at the peak of each pulse and drains into the gaps, so the pulses flatten into nearly steady DC with a small residual wobble — the ripple in the figure's third inset. An inductor (a choke) resists changes in current and smooths the flow further. Across those filter capacitors sits the **bleeder resistor**, whose job appears only after you switch off: it **discharges the filter capacitors when power is removed** (G7A01). Big capacitors can hold a charge long after the plug is pulled, so the bleeder is a safety device, not a refinement — it is what keeps a "dead" supply from biting the person who opens the case.

Last in the chain, the **regulator** holds the output voltage steady as the load current changes, so the transmitter's changing appetite doesn't wobble the voltage feeding the rest of the rig. Everything upstream of it is allowed to sag a little — line voltage drifts, ripple rides through the filter — and the regulator takes up the slack, trading a few volts of headroom for a clean, constant rail. A well-fed oscillator is a stable oscillator, which is why this last stage quietly matters to everything downstream of it.

The alternative architecture skips the heavy iron entirely. A **switchmode power supply** chops the power at high frequency — tens to hundreds of kilohertz rather than 60 Hz — and does its transforming and filtering up there. Because transformers and filter components shrink dramatically as frequency rises, its high-frequency operation allows the use of **smaller components** (G7A08): a switchmode replacement for a bench linear supply is a fraction of the weight and runs cooler, at the price of switching noise you may have to chase out of your receiver on some bands. That chain — transformer, rectifier, filter, regulator, plus the bleeder and the switchmode alternative — is pool questions G7A01 through G7A08.

### Reading the Figure G7-1 schematic

The 2023–2027 General pool contains exactly one graphic, **Figure G7-1**, and five questions — G7A09 through G7A13 — ask you to identify its numbered symbols. The figure is redrawn for this book with the same components, the same labels, and the same numbered callouts as the official version, so what you practice on here is what the exam shows you.

{{fig:ch07-pool-fig-g71}}

Start with the overall shape. The **schematic** is a two-stage circuit: a variable-frequency oscillator on the left, built around the FET, feeding an amplifier/buffer stage on the right, built around the NPN transistor. A single +DC supply rail runs across the top, fed from the open-circle terminal labeled "+DC" at top right, and the RF leaves through the open-circle "OUT" terminal at the right. Every ground symbol is drawn as three slanted strokes of decreasing length, longest on top. Unnumbered support parts — bias resistors, bypass and coupling capacitors, a series resistor in the rail — fill out the design as they would in any discrete two-stage circuit; only the eleven numbered symbols are exam-relevant.

The circuit story is short. Symbol 1, the FET, is the oscillator's amplifying device, and its frequency-setting tank is the tapped inductor, symbol 7, with the varactor diode, symbol 4, doing the voltage tuning. Symbol 2, the NPN, amplifies and buffers the oscillator's RF. The solid-core transformer, symbol 6, couples the amplified output to the OUT terminal. And the Zener diode, symbol 5, shunts the +DC rail to ground, holding the rail voltage steady. Learn those five and you can answer all five figure questions — but the distractors raid the other symbols, so drill all eleven as one symbol-recognition set:

- Symbol 1 — **field effect transistor** (an N-channel JFET), center of the drawing: a circle containing a vertical channel bar, the gate lead entering from the left with a filled arrowhead pointing inward, into the channel. Asked by G7A09.

- Symbol 2 — **NPN junction transistor**, right-center: a circle with a vertical base bar, base lead from the left, collector out the top to the transformer, emitter out the bottom with a filled arrowhead pointing outward, away from the base — "Not Pointing iN." Asked by G7A11.

- Symbol 3 — ordinary **PN junction diode**, left of symbol 1 in a vertical branch to ground: a filled triangle pointing down onto a horizontal cathode bar. Not asked.

- Symbol 4 — **varactor diode**, far left: a filled triangle pointing up into a straight cathode bar with a curved, arc-shaped second plate above it — diode plus capacitor, a voltage-variable capacitor used for tuning. Not asked, but a distractor magnet.

- Symbol 5 — **Zener diode**, top center-right, shunting the +DC rail to ground: a filled triangle pointing up into a cathode bar whose two ends bend diagonally into the classic "Z" wings. Asked by G7A10.

- Symbol 6 — **solid-core transformer**, right: primary and secondary coils separated by two straight vertical core lines, the secondary's top end to the OUT terminal and its bottom end grounded. Asked by G7A12.

- Symbol 7 — **tapped inductor**, lower left: a single vertical coil, bottom end grounded, with a connection — the tap — leaving the coil partway up. Asked by G7A13.

- Symbol 8 — **polarized (electrolytic) capacitor**, top center, from the rail to ground: one straight plate and one curved plate. Not asked.

- Symbol 9 — **fixed resistor**, lower right: a vertical zigzag from the transistor's emitter node to ground. Not asked.

- Symbol 10 — polarized (electrolytic) **coupling capacitor**, left-center, horizontal in the long gate/signal wire: one straight plate, one curved plate. Not asked.

- Symbol 11 — **variable resistor**, upper left: a vertical zigzag with a diagonal arrow drawn through it. Not asked.

The discrimination students miss is the three diode variants — 3 plain, 4 varactor, 5 Zener. All three are a filled triangle pointing into a bar, so the identity lives entirely in the details around the bar: triangle down onto a plain bar is the ordinary diode; triangle up into a bar with a curved second plate is the varactor; triangle up into a bar with bent "Z" wings is the Zener. G7A10's distractor magnet is varactor-4 versus Zener-5 — when you meet a diode symbol in this figure, look for the wings before you commit.

Here is the drill that makes the five questions free. Cover the numbered list and point at each callout in the drawing, naming the component and its job in the circuit out loud; then work the list backward, picturing each symbol from its name. Run the three diodes twice — once forward (3 plain, 4 varactor, 5 Zener) and once by feature (wings mean Zener, curved plate means varactor, plain bar means ordinary diode). On the exam, the figure prints beside the questions with exactly these eleven callouts, so the recognition you build on the redraw transfers directly; nothing about the layout changes, only the drawing style.

Notice also how the five questions raid each other: their answer options are drawn from symbols 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 11, so partial knowledge still faces plausible distractors. The map to memorize is G7A09 to symbol 1 (FET), G7A10 to symbol 5 (Zener), G7A11 to symbol 2 (NPN), G7A12 to symbol 6 (solid-core transformer), and G7A13 to symbol 7 (tapped inductor).

### Amplifier classes, neutralization, and oscillators

An amplifier's **class** describes what fraction of each input cycle the amplifying device actually conducts, and the conduction angle trades linearity against efficiency. The figure draws the four standard classes as waveforms — dashed curve for the input drive, solid curve for the output current.

{{fig:ch07-amp-classes}}

**Class A** conducts **100 percent** of the cycle (G7B04): the device never switches off, so the output tracks the input faithfully — the most linear class, and the least efficient, because current flows even when there is no signal to amplify. **Class B** conducts half the cycle, 180 degrees, with a push-pull pair restoring the full waveform; **class AB** sits between them, conducting more than half. **Class C** conducts less than half the cycle — a narrow pulse of current per cycle — which makes it the **highest-efficiency** class (G7B02). The catch is distortion: a class C stage chops the waveform, so any information carried in the amplitude is destroyed. That limits class C to constant-envelope modes — **FM** is the keyed example (G7B11) — while SSB and AM, which live in the amplitude, are ruled out. SSB work demands a **linear amplifier**, defined as one in which the output preserves the input waveform (G7B10).

The trade shows up in real gear. An SSB final runs class AB — linear enough to keep the waveform honest, far less wasteful than class A. An FM or CW final can run class C and convert most of its DC input into RF instead of heat, because those modes carry nothing in the amplitude. Efficiency is not an abstract virtue: every watt short of 100 percent comes out of the heat sink as temperature, which is why high-duty-cycle modes punish inefficient stages.

**Neutralizing** an amplifier means canceling the internal feedback path — through the device's own internal capacitance — that can turn an amplifier into an unwanted oscillator. The purpose of neutralization is to **eliminate self-oscillation** (G7B01); it has nothing to do with modulation, whatever the distractors imply. And **efficiency** itself has a precise definition the pool asks for directly: $\eta = P_{out} / P_{in}$, the RF output power divided by the DC input power (G7B08).

> **Worked example:** A final amplifier drawing 13.8 V × 10 A = 138 W from the supply while delivering 100 W of RF runs at η = 100 ÷ 138 ≈ 72 percent efficiency — the other 38 W is heat the heat sink must move. That heat is why a class C final can run a smaller heat sink than a class A final of the same RF output.

An **oscillator** is where the signal comes from in the first place. The pool's anatomy of a sine-wave oscillator is exactly two parts: **a filter and an amplifier operating in a feedback loop** (G7B07) — the amplifier supplies gain, the filter selects the frequency, and the loop feeds just enough of the output back to the input to sustain the oscillation. In an **LC oscillator**, what sets the frequency is the **inductance and capacitance in the tank circuit** (G7B09) — chapter 5's resonance formula is the math underneath. You have just seen one in Figure G7-1: symbol 7's tapped coil, with its associated capacitance, is the tank that sets the VFO's frequency. Together these are the analog questions of pool group G7B.

### A few digital building blocks

Three G7B questions step away from analog entirely, and each is a one-line idea. An **AND gate** does what the name says: its output is high **only when both inputs are high** (G7B03) — one input low is enough to force the output low. A **binary counter** accumulates states, one per clock pulse, and each added bit doubles the count.

> **Worked example:** A 3-bit ripple counter: each bit doubles the state count, so 2³ = **8 states** (G7B05) — 000 through 111 in binary — before it rolls over to 000 again. A 4-bit counter would run through 16 states before rolling over.

A **shift register** is **a clocked array of circuits that passes data in steps along the array** (G7B06) — each clock pulse moves every stored bit one position down the line, which is how serial data gets marched into and out of digital hardware. Gates, counters, and shift registers close out pool group G7B.

### Transceivers, filters, and software-defined radio

Pool group G7C opens with the SSB chain — how single sideband is generated and recovered — and it has three parts. At the transmit end, a **balanced modulator** mixes the audio with the RF carrier and cancels the carrier itself, producing **double-sideband suppressed-carrier RF** (G7C02): two sidebands, no carrier. A **filter** then strips one of the two sidebands (G7C01), leaving true single sideband — the filter method of SSB generation, and the reason the crystal-lattice or mechanical filters in an SSB rig are such prized parts. At the receive end comes the mirror operation: a **product detector** mixes the incoming SSB signal with a locally generated replacement carrier and extracts the modulating audio (G7C04) — it is the detector used in a single-sideband receiver. One related question sits nearby: an impedance-matching transformer at a transmitter output exists to **present the desired impedance to the transmitter and feed line** (G7C03) — one line here, because chapters 5 and 9 handle matching in depth.

Filters earn their own vocabulary, and the four filter questions are pure terminology measured against the curves in the figure.

{{fig:ch07-filter-responses}}

**Insertion loss** is attenuation *inside* the passband (G7C07) — the slice of your wanted signal the filter eats even though it is supposed to pass it. The **cutoff frequency** of a low-pass filter is the point where the output power falls to half the input power — the **half-power point** (G7C12). **Ultimate rejection** is the maximum stopband rejection (G7C13) — how deep the filter can dig outside the passband. And a band-pass filter's bandwidth is measured between the **upper and lower half-power frequencies** (G7C14). The pair to keep apart is inside versus outside: insertion loss lives in the passband, ultimate rejection in the stopband.

On receivers, the pool asks what sensitivity depends on, and the honest answer is everything on the list: input gain, demodulator bandwidth, and noise figure — all three (G7C08). Gain sets how much the weak signal is lifted above the receiver's own floor, demodulator bandwidth sets how much channel noise you accept along with it (narrower admits less noise, which is why CW pulls out weak signals SSB loses), and **noise figure** sets how much noise the receiver itself contributes. Learn the three contributors rather than the answer shape, because the same "all these choices are correct" phrasing is a distractor elsewhere in this subelement.

Two synthesis technologies round out the radio. A **direct digital synthesizer (DDS)** generates its output numerically, which gives **variable output frequency with the stability of a crystal oscillator** (G7C05) — the agility of a VFO disciplined by a crystal reference. **DSP filters** do their filtering arithmetically on digitized samples, so **a wide range of filter bandwidths and shapes can be created** (G7C06) — responses no analog filter can realize, changed in software instead of in hardware.

The two technologies meet in modern rigs: a DDS chip tunes the radio with crystal-referenced precision while a DSP engine shapes the receiver's selectivity, so the same front panel knob can offer a dozen filter widths that once would have cost a dozen hardware filters.

And then the radio disappears into code. In a **software-defined radio (SDR)**, the incoming signal is split into two components 90 degrees apart — the **I and Q signals**, and 90 degrees is the keyed separation (G7C09). With the signal carried as I/Q pairs, **all types of modulation can be created with appropriate processing** (G7C10), and the classic receiver jobs — filtering, detection, and modulation — are all performed in software (G7C11). The whole chain this chapter followed through hardware becomes arithmetic. That completes pool group G7C.

Put the journey back together and the rig stops being a row of boxes. The 120 V from the wall comes down the chain you opened at the start of this chapter — stepped, rectified, filtered, regulated — and feeds an oscillator much like the one in Figure G7-1, whose tank sets the frequency and whose buffer hands the signal forward. Amplifier class decides what modes the stages after it may honestly carry; filters carve the signal down to the one sideband you mean to send; and at the far end of the chain, software increasingly does the work that hardware used to do. Same journey, every radio — now you can walk it stage by stage.

**FACT:** A half-wave rectifier uses one diode and converts 180 degrees of each AC cycle; a full-wave rectifier converts 360 degrees, so its unfiltered output is DC pulses at twice the AC line frequency; and the center-tapped full-wave circuit uses two diodes and a center-tapped transformer.

**FACT:** Power-supply filters are built from capacitors and inductors, a bleeder resistor discharges the filter capacitors when power is removed (a safety device), and a switchmode supply's high-frequency chopping is what allows smaller, lighter components.

**FACT:** Amplifier class describes what fraction of the cycle the device conducts: class A conducts 100 percent of the cycle (linear but inefficient), class C has the highest efficiency but distorts amplitude so it suits constant-envelope modes only (FM yes; SSB and AM no), and a linear amplifier preserves the input waveform — required for SSB.

**FACT:** A balanced modulator produces double-sideband suppressed-carrier RF, a filter strips one sideband to make SSB, and a product detector recovers the audio in an SSB receiver.

**FACT:** In an SDR the I and Q signals are 90 degrees apart, I/Q processing lets software create any modulation type, and filtering, detection, and modulation all happen in software.

### Key Takeaways

- A half-wave rectifier uses one diode and conducts 180 degrees of each cycle; a full-wave rectifier conducts 360 degrees, so its unfiltered output is DC pulses at twice the line frequency — the reason full-wave is easier to filter.

- Two diodes plus a center-tapped transformer is full-wave; four diodes is a bridge. The bleeder resistor that discharges the filter capacitors when power is removed is a safety device.

- Drill Figure G7-1's eleven symbols as one set — especially the three diodes, 3 plain, 4 varactor, 5 Zener, told apart by the details around the cathode bar.

- Class A conducts 100 percent of the cycle; class C is the most efficient but distorts amplitude, so it suits FM and never SSB — SSB requires a linear amplifier.

- Neutralization cancels internal feedback to eliminate self-oscillation; efficiency is RF output power divided by DC input power.

- The SSB chain is balanced modulator first (double-sideband suppressed carrier), then a filter (strips one sideband), then a product detector (recovers the audio).

- In an SDR the I and Q signals are 90 degrees apart, and filtering, detection, and modulation all happen in software.
