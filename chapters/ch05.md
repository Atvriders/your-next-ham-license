## 5. AC Theory: Reactance, Impedance, Resonance

Open an antenna tuner and there is surprisingly little inside: a coil or two, some capacitors, and a way to rearrange them. Yet that modest parts list is what stands between your transmitter and a load it may refuse to drive, and the same coil-and-capacitor partnership runs your filters and sets whether your antenna resonates on the band you want. What makes these two components so useful is that they behave differently at every frequency — they are the machinery behind radio's most honest answer, "it depends on the frequency." In this chapter you'll learn the theory that makes that answer precise: reactance, impedance, resonance, RMS and peak envelope power, decibels, transformers, and the rules for combining components. It is the most mathematical chapter in the book, and it is written for the math-shy upgrader: every formula is stated plainly and then used at least once with the exam's own numbers, so you can watch each one work before you meet it on the test. This is pool subelement G5: 40 questions, three of which will be on your exam.

### Reactance: AC meets the coil and the capacitor

Book 2's circuits ran on DC thinking: a resistor opposes current, turns electrical energy into heat, and behaves the same way at any frequency. Two components you met there play by different rules once the current alternates. An inductor — a coil of wire, measured in henries — stores energy in a magnetic field. A capacitor — two conductors facing each other across an insulator, measured in farads — stores energy in an electric field. Neither one dissipates anything; each borrows energy from the circuit and hands it back a fraction of a cycle later. But while the energy is in transit, both components push back against the alternating current, and that pushback has a name.

Reactance is the opposition to AC current flow caused by capacitance or inductance (G5A02, G5A03, G5A04). It is measured in ohms, the same unit as resistance (G5A09), and its letter is X (G5A11). The ohms are real — a reactance limits AC current exactly as a resistor of the same value would — but the resemblance ends there. Resistance converts electrical energy into heat, gone for good. Reactance stores the energy and returns it to the circuit later in the cycle, so a pure reactance does not warm up at all. Keep that distinction straight and half of this chapter falls into place: resistance dissipates; reactance stores and returns.

Watch the energy for one cycle and the distinction becomes physical. In one quarter-cycle the magnetic field around a coil grows and swallows energy from the circuit; in the next it collapses and hands every bit back. A capacitor does the same dance with its electric field, charging and discharging. Twice per cycle each component borrows the energy and twice per cycle returns it, so over a whole cycle the net consumption is zero — no heat, no warmth, nothing wasted. The opposition you measure is the borrowing itself: current must flow to build those fields, and the component's unwillingness to be filled on demand is the reactance.

Here is the part the exam cares about most, and it is genuinely two facts, not four. An inductor's reactance rises as frequency rises (G5A05) — the faster the current alternates, the harder the coil's ever-changing magnetic field fights it. A capacitor's reactance falls as frequency rises (G5A06) — the faster the alternation, the less time the capacitor has to charge up and push back, until at high frequencies it barely impedes the current at all. Inductor up, capacitor down. And notice what never appears in either rule: amplitude. The pool plants choices claiming that increasing the amplitude of the applied AC changes the reactance, and it does not. Frequency is the only lever.

The inductor's formula says it outright: $X_L = 2\pi f L$ — frequency in hertz, inductance in henries, reactance in ohms. Multiply and the reactance grows in direct proportion to frequency, a straight line through the origin. Double the frequency and you double the opposition, which is why a coil that is nearly transparent to 60 Hz hum can be a brick wall at 7 MHz.

> **Worked example:** Take the pool's own 20 mH inductor (G5C11) to 7 MHz: $X_L = 2\pi f L$ = 2π × 7×10⁶ × 0.020 ≈ 880,000 Ω — about 880 kΩ of opposition. The same inductor at 60 Hz: 2π × 60 × 0.020 ≈ 7.5 Ω. Same coil, two frequencies, reactance differing by more than a hundred thousand to one — that is G5A05's "reactance increases with frequency" made concrete.

One bookkeeping habit makes every formula in this chapter painless: convert to base units before you compute. The prefix ladder from Book 2 — pico, nano, micro, milli, base, kilo, mega — runs in steps of a thousand, so 20 mH becomes 0.020 H, 100 µF becomes 100×10⁻⁶ F, and 7 MHz becomes 7×10⁶ Hz. Every worked example in this chapter converts first and computes second, and the habit will save you from the unit-mix traps the pool sets later.

The capacitor runs the same movie in reverse. Its formula is $X_C = 1/(2\pi f C)$ — frequency sits downstairs now, so reactance shrinks as frequency climbs. The pool's 100 µF capacitor (G5C09) at 60 Hz works out to 1/(2π × 60 × 100×10⁻⁶) ≈ 26.5 Ω, and at 120 Hz that has halved to 13.3 Ω. Double the frequency, half the reactance: G5A06 in numbers. You will do this computation properly, step by step, in the next section.

Notice that the two formulas keep the frequency directions straight for you. In the inductor's, f sits upstairs: bigger f, bigger X. In the capacitor's, f sits downstairs: bigger f, smaller X. If the two rules ever blur together in the exam room, recover them from that single observation — frequency upstairs for the coil, downstairs for the capacitor — and G5A05 and G5A06 answer themselves.

One more idea seeds everything that follows: phase. In a resistor, voltage and current rise and fall together, peaks aligned. A reactance throws them out of alignment, because storing and returning energy takes time. In an inductor the voltage leads the current — the coil's field pushes back against every change in current, so the current always arrives late. In a capacitor the voltage lags the current — charge must flow in before any voltage can appear across the plates. That quarter-cycle timing difference is why reactance and resistance cannot simply be added, and it is the seed of both impedance and resonance.

{{fig:ch05-time-constants}}

The same store-and-return behavior shows up when DC is switched into these components, and one figure is worth a paragraph even though the pool tests none of it. A capacitor charging through a resistor climbs to 63 percent of the supply voltage after one time constant τ = R × C and is effectively full by 5τ; discharging, it falls to 37 percent after one τ. An inductor's current builds and decays on the same kind of clock with τ = L / R. No time-constant arithmetic appears on the exam — but these curves are the mechanism behind G5A02 through G5A06: inductors and capacitors oppose AC by storing and returning energy, never by dissipating it.

That is the reactance family of pool group G5A: the definition, the ohm, the letter X, and the two frequency directions.

### Impedance and resonance

Once a circuit contains both resistance and reactance, you need the umbrella term. Impedance, letter Z, is the ratio of voltage to current — Ohm's law generalized to AC circuits (G5A08). Flip it upside down and you have its reciprocal, admittance (G5A07). The distractors in that corner of the pool are pure vocabulary — conductance, susceptance, reluctance — so file the pair this way: impedance opposes, admittance admits, and the others belong to different stories.

Resistance and reactance both live inside impedance, but they do not add like grocery bills, because they act at different moments of the AC cycle — the phase idea from the last section. They combine as perpendicular arrows on a plane: resistance along the horizontal axis, reactance along the vertical, inductive above and capacitive below. The circuit's total opposition is the diagonal of that right triangle, and the diagonal comes from Pythagoras, not from addition.

The magnitude is $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$, and the angle the diagonal makes with the resistance axis — the phase angle, φ = arctan(X/R) — tells you how far the circuit leans reactive.

> **Worked example:** 50 Ω of resistance in series with 50 Ω of inductive reactance — the pool's favorite 50-ohm number paired with an equal reactance. The magnitude is $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + X^2}$ = √(50² + 50²) = √5,000 ≈ 70.7 Ω, at a phase angle of arctan(50/50) = 45°. Not 100 Ω. Resistance and reactance add as perpendicular vectors, because only the resistance dissipates power.

{{fig:ch05-impedance-vectors}}

Now put a coil and a capacitor in the same circuit and sweep the frequency. Since X_L climbs while X_C falls, there is exactly one frequency where the two are equal — and because their phase effects point in opposite directions, voltage leading in the coil and lagging in the capacitor, at that frequency they cancel each other completely. That is resonance: inductive reactance and capacitive reactance cancel (G5A12). What the cancellation leaves behind depends on how the pair is wired, and the pool's classic trap is to swap the two cases. In a series LC circuit, cancellation leaves only the circuit's small resistance in the current's path, so the impedance goes very low (G5A01). In a parallel LC circuit, the current sloshes back and forth between coil and capacitor and almost none is demanded from the source, so the impedance goes very high. Series low, parallel high.

The wiring difference is worth a slower look, because it powers half the filters and tuners in the hobby. Wire the coil and capacitor in series and drive the pair from a source: at resonance the coil's push and the capacitor's push are equal and opposite at every instant, so the source feels only the leftover resistance — a nearly free pass for that one frequency. Wire them in parallel instead and the pair becomes a private loop at resonance: the coil discharges into the capacitor and the capacitor back into the coil, round and round, asking almost nothing from the source — a nearly complete roadblock for that one frequency. Same two components, same resonance, opposite behavior — which is why the G5A01 distractor that swaps them catches so many candidates.

The resonant frequency itself comes from the component values: $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ — frequency in hertz, inductance in henries, capacitance in farads.

> **Worked example:** Start with the pool's 100 µF capacitor (G5C09) at 60 Hz: $X_C = 1/(2\pi f C)$ = 1/(2π × 60 × 100×10⁻⁶) ≈ 26.5 Ω. Now pair the pool's 10 mH inductor (G5C10) with that 100 µF capacitor and ask where they resonate: f = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π × √(0.010 × 100×10⁻⁶)) = 1/(2π × 0.001) ≈ 159 Hz. At 159 Hz the inductor's reactance is 2π × 159 × 0.010 ≈ 10 Ω, and the capacitor's works out to the same 10 Ω — X_L equals X_C and the two cancel, which is exactly G5A12's resonance.

How selective is a resonance? That quality has a name — Q, the quality factor — and it is the sharpness of the resonance curve. A high-Q circuit shows a deep, narrow impedance dip, or a tall, narrow peak: it responds vigorously at its resonant frequency and rejects the neighbors. A low-Q circuit responds more gently across a wider span. Q is the reason a good filter passes the station you want and not the one twenty kilohertz up the dial, and chapter 7's filters are resonance and Q put to work. The exam treats Q as a concept only, so a concept it stays.

{{fig:ch05-resonance-curves}}

Resonance also explains how mismatches get fixed. Impedance matching at RF can use a transformer, a pi-network, or a transmission-line section — and the pool's keyed answer is all three (G5A10). Your antenna tuner is a pi-network of variable reactances, adjusted until they cancel the reactance the antenna presents; transformers earn their own section at the end of this chapter; and a carefully cut length of transmission line transforms impedance by standing-wave arithmetic, which chapter 9 revisits. You will meet resonance once more there wearing a different costume: a resonant antenna is simply one whose inductive and capacitive reactances cancel at the operating frequency, leaving a purely resistive feed point.

That completes pool group G5A: impedance, admittance, the matching trio, and the resonance pair.

### Power, RMS, and PEP

Book 2's power law needs only a one-line refresher: P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R — three forms of one relationship, and you pick the form that matches the two quantities you have. The General pool runs the same three forms with one-step-harder numbers, so warm up on its own drills. 400 VDC across 800 Ω: P = V²/R = 400²/800 = 200 W (G5B03). 12 V at 0.2 A: P = V × I = 2.4 W (G5B04). 7.0 mA through 1,250 Ω: P = I² × R = 0.007² × 1,250 ≈ 0.061 W, about 61 mW — watch the milli, because the watt-sized distractors feed on unit slips (G5B05). And run one backwards: a 50 Ω load dissipating 1,200 W has V = √(P × R) = √(1,200 × 50) ≈ 245 V RMS across it (G5B12). One more DC idea while we are here: in a parallel circuit, the total current equals the sum of the branch currents (G5B02).

AC adds a question DC never asks: which voltage do you mean? A sine wave's voltage changes moment to moment, so "the voltage" could be the peak, the peak-to-peak span, or something else. The something else is RMS — root mean square — the AC value that heats a resistor exactly like the same value of DC (G5B07). It is the only honest yardstick for power, and for a sine wave the conversions are two fixed numbers: V_rms = V_peak/√2 ≈ 0.707 × V_peak, and V_pp = 2√2 × V_rms ≈ 2.828 × V_rms. The pool drills both directions. 17 V peak is 17 × 0.707 ≈ 12 V RMS (G5B09). 120 V RMS is 120 × 2.828 ≈ 339.4 V peak-to-peak (G5B08) — your wall outlet's 120 V is the RMS figure, and the sine behind it actually swings 339.4 V from crest to trough. The pair 0.707 and 2.828 is worth memorizing outright; together they move among the three yardsticks in one step.

The heating equivalence is not an abstraction — it is how your station actually measures things. A dummy load is just a 50 Ω resistor turning RF into heat, and a wattmeter's job is to report the power doing that heating. Quote a voltage as peak when the resistor feels RMS and every power you compute comes out wrong by a factor of two, four, or worse. That is why the three yardsticks exist, and why moving among them is exam math rather than trivia.

{{fig:ch05-rms-peak-pep}}

PEP — peak envelope power — is where RMS thinking meets real signals. An SSB signal's amplitude follows your voice: the RF peaks trace out an outline called the modulation envelope, swelling on loud syllables and collapsing between words. Average power under that shifting envelope is modest and hard to pin down, so the legal and practical measure of a transmitter's muscle is the power at the very crest of the envelope, computed from the peak RF voltage. It is PEP, not average power, that the 1,500-watt limit from chapter 1 measures. Take the peak voltage, convert it to RMS, and apply P = V²/R. Fold the two steps into one and peak-to-peak voltage goes straight to power:

$PEP = V_{pp}^2/(8R)$

> **Worked example:** 200 volts peak-to-peak across a 50-ohm dummy load (G5B06). Long way: peak is half of peak-to-peak, so 100 V; RMS of the peak is 100/√2 ≈ 70.7 V; power is 70.7²/50 ≈ 5,000/50 = 100 W. Short way: $PEP = V_{pp}^2/(8R)$ = 200²/(8 × 50) = 40,000/400 = 100 W. The same drill at 500 volts peak-to-peak gives 500²/(8 × 50) = 625 W (G5B14). The distractor trap is skipping the halving: converting 200 V peak-to-peak straight to RMS yields (200/√2)²/50 = 400 W — choice D, wrong. Halve to peak first; the √2 conversion is the whole game.

One special case keeps two more questions free. For an unmodulated carrier — a steady signal with no speech envelope at all — the envelope is flat, so the peak is the average: PEP equals average power, a ratio of 1.00 (G5B11). A 1,060 W average unmodulated carrier is therefore a 1,060 W PEP carrier (G5B13). No halving, no √2 — the pool chose 1,060 so the answer is the same number with no arithmetic at all, and the trap is reaching for a conversion anyway.

That is pool group G5B's power family: the three DC forms with harder numbers, the RMS conversions, PEP, and the unmodulated-carrier special case.

### Decibels for real

Book 2 gave you the decibel anchors: 3 dB ≈ double the power, 6 dB ≈ four times the power, 10 dB = ten times the power. Chapter 4 added the S-meter pair — one S unit ≈ 6 dB and "20 dB over S9" = 100 times the power. Now the General course does something new with an old friend: it makes the defining formula itself exam math. A decibel figure is ten times the base-10 logarithm of a power ratio:

$dB = 10 \log_{10}(P_2/P_1)$

Every anchor you already know falls out of it. log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.301, so doubling power is 10 × 0.301 ≈ 3 dB — G5B01's keyed answer, "approximately 3 dB," derived instead of memorized. log₁₀(4) ≈ 0.602 gives the 6 dB anchor, log₁₀(10) = 1 gives 10 dB, and log₁₀(100) = 2 gives the 20 dB anchor. And the payoff for thinking in logarithms is that gains and losses stack by simple addition, which raw ratios never do.

Watch the anchors do real station arithmetic. An amplifier gives you 10 dB of gain and the feed line costs 3 dB, so the chain nets 7 dB. What is 7 dB in plain power? Break it into anchors you own: 10 dB minus 3 dB is ten times divided by two — a factor of five. One hundred watts into that chain behaves like 500 W where it counts. No calculator, no log key: just the 3 and 10 anchors composed. That composability is the entire reason radio engineers think in decibels, and the same add-the-gains, subtract-the-losses habit returns in chapter 8's link budgets.

The pool asks you to run the formula backwards exactly once, and it is the only non-round decibel computation on the exam: what percentage of power loss is equivalent to a loss of 1 dB (G5B10)? Setting the ratio P₂/P₁ = 10^(−0.1) = 0.794 means 79.4 percent of the power survives the loss — a 20.6 percent loss, choice C.

> **Worked example:** A 1 dB cable loss, worked from the definition $dB = 10 \log_{10}(P_2/P_1)$ with dB = −1: P₂/P₁ = 10^(−1/10) = 10^(−0.1) = 0.794. Feed 100 W into that cable and 79.4 W comes out the far end — a 20.6% loss, exactly G5B10's keyed answer. Run the same formula forward on a doubled power and you get 10 × log₁₀(2) = 10 × 0.301 ≈ 3 dB (G5B01).

> **The math, if you want it:** A logarithm answers one question: how many times must I multiply ten by itself to reach this number? log₁₀(10) = 1, log₁₀(100) = 2, log₁₀(1,000) = 3 — and log₁₀(2) ≈ 0.301, because 10^0.301 ≈ 2. Multiplying ratios adds their logarithms, so any gain you can build from doublings and tenfolds is mental arithmetic: four times is two doublings, 0.301 + 0.301 ≈ 0.602, and ten times that is ≈ 6 dB; eight times is three doublings, ≈ 9 dB. For the non-round cases any scientific calculator — including the one built into your phone — has a log key, and the pool's only demand of that kind is G5B10's 10^(−0.1) = 0.794.

Keep the two exam numbers side by side and they are easy to hold: doubling adds about 3 dB, and losing 1 dB keeps about four-fifths of your power. Those two, plus the stacking-by-addition habit, cover pool questions G5B01 and G5B10 — and they rhyme with chapter 4's S-meter anchors, one S unit ≈ 6 dB and 20 dB = 100 times.

### Transformers and combining components

A transformer is two coils sharing one magnetic field, and its action is mutual inductance (G5C01): alternating current in the primary winding builds a changing magnetic field, and that changing field induces a voltage in the secondary winding. There is no electrical connection between the windings — the energy crosses as magnetism. That isolation is not a side benefit; it is half of why transformers exist, letting AC cross a boundary that DC and ground currents cannot.

The voltage ratio equals the turns ratio: $V_s = V_p \times (N_s/N_p)$, with N_p and N_s the primary and secondary turns. More secondary turns than primary steps the voltage up; fewer steps it down. And because the transformer does not know which winding you chose to feed, running it backwards inverts the ratio — a 4:1 step-down transformer driven into its secondary multiplies the applied voltage by four (G5C02). Current behaves opposite to voltage, since the power the secondary delivers has to come from the primary: what the secondary gains in voltage it gives back in current, so a step-up transformer's primary carries the higher current and is wound with heavier wire (G5C05).

Impedance is where the exam's trap lives. A load connected to the secondary reflects back to the primary not as the turns ratio but as its square: $Z_p/Z_s = (N_p/N_s)^2$. That square is what makes transformers so useful as matching devices between unlike impedances — and it manufactures the two classic wrong answers, the ratio that forgot to square and the ratio that squared twice.

{{fig:ch05-transformer}}

> **Worked example:** 120 VAC applied to a 500-turn primary with a 1,500-turn secondary (G5C06): voltage follows the turns ratio, so 120 × (1500/500) = 120 × 3 = 360 V. Now the matching case: an antenna's 600 Ω feed point onto 50 Ω coax (G5C07) is a 12:1 impedance ratio, and impedance transforms as the square of the turns, so the turns ratio is √12 ≈ 3.5:1. The 12:1 and 144:1 choices are the forget-the-square and square-twice traps.

Finally, the combination rules — and they come with a mercy. Resistors and inductors behave alike: values add in series and add by reciprocals in parallel. Capacitors do exactly the opposite: reciprocals in series, plain addition in parallel. Learn one row and you know the whole table.

The reciprocal method deserves one slow pass, because it is the only new arithmetic here. For anything that combines by reciprocals: flip each value, add the flips, then flip back. The pool's 10, 20, and 50 Ω in parallel (G5C03): the flips are 1/10, 1/20, and 1/50, which add to 0.17, and 1/0.17 ≈ 5.9 Ω. Notice the answer comes out smaller than the smallest branch — it must, because every added parallel path gives the current one more way through. Two resistors are quicker still: 100 Ω with 200 Ω gives ≈ 67 Ω (G5C04), again below the smaller branch.

Every combination answer carries a built-in sanity check. Series resistors and inductors must come out larger than the largest branch; parallel ones, smaller than the smallest. Capacitors flip the checks: series comes out smaller than the smallest, parallel larger than the largest — plain addition, after all. Run the check before you commit to an answer and most slips announce themselves.

The pool drills every remaining cell of the table. Series inductors add like resistors: 20 mH + 50 mH = 70 mH (G5C11). Parallel inductors take reciprocals: three 10 mH coils give 3.3 mH (G5C10). Series capacitors take reciprocals — and here the smaller-than-the-smallest result surprises everyone the first time: three 100 µF capacitors give just 33.3 µF (G5C09), and 20 µF in series with 50 µF gives 1/(1/20 + 1/50) ≈ 14.3 µF (G5C12). Parallel capacitors simply add, which sets up the pool's craftiest drill in this group, because G5C08 mixes units on purpose: two 5.0-nanofarad capacitors and one 750-picofarad capacitor in parallel. Convert before you add — 750 pF is 0.750 nF — then 5 + 5 + 0.750 = 10.750 nF, trailing zero and all, exactly as the keyed answer prints it. Two last consequences round out the group: to increase capacitance, add a capacitor in parallel (G5C13); to increase inductance, add an inductor in series (G5C14).

That closes pool group G5C — and with it, all 40 questions of subelement G5. Step back and the chapter's math turns out to be three ideas wearing different clothes. Frequency moves reactance — up for coils, down for capacitors — and where the two meet, resonance cancels them. Power questions are Book 2's power law plus one habit: convert every voltage to RMS before you square it. And ratios — of power, of turns, of impedance — are the decibel and transformer arithmetic that moves numbers between the antenna and the wattmeter. You will use all three constantly: the tuner of chapter 4, the filter shapes of chapter 7, and the feed-point impedances of chapter 9 are this chapter with the training wheels off.

**FACT:** Reactance is the opposition to AC current flow caused by capacitance or inductance — its unit is the ohm, its letter is X, and it opposes AC without dissipating power.

**FACT:** An inductor's reactance rises with frequency; a capacitor's reactance falls with frequency.

**FACT:** PEP is computed from the peak envelope voltage using PEP = (V_pp/(2√2))²/R = V_pp²/(8R) — 200 V peak-to-peak across 50 Ω is 100 W PEP, and 500 V peak-to-peak across 50 Ω is 625 W PEP.

**FACT:** Decibels follow dB = 10·log₁₀(P₂/P₁): a power increase to double is approximately 3 dB, and a 1 dB loss leaves 0.794 of the power — a 20.6% loss.

**FACT:** Impedance transforms as the square of the turns ratio — matching 600 Ω to 50 Ω is a 12:1 impedance ratio, so the turns ratio is √12 ≈ 3.5:1.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G5 — Electrical Principles — is 40 pool questions in three groups (G5A, G5B, G5C), and your exam draws three of them, one per group. The ten below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each.

> **G5A01** What happens when inductive and capacitive reactance are equal in a series LC circuit?
> A. Resonance causes impedance to be very high
> B. Impedance is equal to the geometric mean of the inductance and capacitance
> C. Resonance causes impedance to be very low
> D. Impedance is equal to the arithmetic mean of the inductance and capacitance
> **Answer: C** — at resonance the reactances cancel, so a series LC circuit's impedance goes very low — "very high" is the parallel case.

> **G5A05** How does an inductor react to AC?
> A. As the frequency of the applied AC increases, the reactance decreases
> B. As the amplitude of the applied AC increases, the reactance increases
> C. As the amplitude of the applied AC increases, the reactance decreases
> D. As the frequency of the applied AC increases, the reactance increases
> **Answer: D** — inductive reactance rises with frequency (X_L = 2πfL); amplitude changes nothing.

> **G5A12** What occurs in an LC circuit at resonance?
> A. Current and voltage are equal
> B. Resistance is cancelled
> C. The circuit radiates all its energy in the form of radio waves
> D. Inductive reactance and capacitive reactance cancel
> **Answer: D** — at resonance X_L equals X_C and the two reactances cancel.

> **G5B01** What dB change represents a factor of two increase or decrease in power?
> A. Approximately 2 dB
> B. Approximately 3 dB
> C. Approximately 6 dB
> D. Approximately 9 dB
> **Answer: B** — doubling power is 10·log₁₀(2) ≈ 3 dB.

> **G5B06** What is the PEP produced by 200 volts peak-to-peak across a 50-ohm dummy load?
> A. 1.4 watts
> B. 100 watts
> C. 353.5 watts
> D. 400 watts
> **Answer: B** — PEP = V_pp²/(8R) = 200²/(8 × 50) = 100 W; 400 W is the forgot-to-halve trap.

> **G5B08** What is the peak-to-peak voltage of a sine wave with an RMS voltage of 120 volts?
> A. 84.8 volts
> B. 169.7 volts
> C. 240.0 volts
> D. 339.4 volts
> **Answer: D** — V_pp = 2√2 × V_rms ≈ 2.828 × 120 ≈ 339.4 V.

> **G5B10** What percentage of power loss is equivalent to a loss of 1 dB?
> A. 10.9 percent
> B. 12.2 percent
> C. 20.6 percent
> D. 25.9 percent
> **Answer: C** — a 1 dB loss leaves 10^(−0.1) = 0.794 of the power, a 20.6% loss.

> **G5C06** What is the voltage output of a transformer with a 500-turn primary and a 1500-turn secondary when 120 VAC is applied to the primary?
> A. 360 volts
> B. 120 volts
> C. 40 volts
> D. 25.5 volts
> **Answer: A** — voltage follows the turns ratio: 120 × (1500/500) = 360 V.

> **G5C07** What transformer turns ratio matches an antenna’s 600-ohm feed point impedance to a 50-ohm coaxial cable?
> A. 3.5 to 1
> B. 12 to 1
> C. 24 to 1
> D. 144 to 1
> **Answer: A** — impedance transforms as the turns ratio squared, so 12:1 impedance needs √12 ≈ 3.5:1 turns.

> **G5C08** What is the equivalent capacitance of two 5.0-nanofarad capacitors and one 750-picofarad capacitor connected in parallel?
> A. 576.9 nanofarads
> B. 1,733 picofarads
> C. 3,583 picofarads
> D. 10.750 nanofarads
> **Answer: D** — parallel capacitors add: 5 nF + 5 nF + 0.750 nF = 10.750 nF — convert the units first.

### Key Takeaways

- Reactance X opposes AC without dissipating power — measured in ohms, caused by capacitance or inductance. An inductor's reactance rises with frequency; a capacitor's falls; amplitude plays no part.

- At resonance X_L equals X_C and the reactances cancel: series LC impedance goes very low, parallel LC very high.

- Impedance combines resistance and reactance as perpendicular vectors, |Z| = √(R² + X²) — 50 Ω + j50 Ω is 70.7 Ω at 45°, not 100 Ω.

- RMS is the DC-equivalent heating value: V_rms ≈ 0.707 × V_peak and V_pp ≈ 2.828 × V_rms — so 17 V peak ≈ 12 V RMS and 120 V RMS ≈ 339.4 V peak-to-peak.

- PEP = V_pp²/(8R): 200 V peak-to-peak into 50 Ω is 100 W — halve to peak and convert to RMS before squaring, or you land on the 400 W distractor. For an unmodulated carrier, PEP equals average power.

- Decibels follow dB = 10·log₁₀(P₂/P₁): doubling power is ≈ 3 dB, and a 1 dB loss leaves 0.794 of the power — a 20.6% loss.

- Transformer voltage follows the turns ratio and impedance follows its square — 600 Ω to 50 Ω is 12:1, so √12 ≈ 3.5:1 turns — and capacitors combine backwards from resistors and inductors.
