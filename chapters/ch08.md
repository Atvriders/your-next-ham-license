## 8. Signals & Emissions

Speak into the microphone of your HF rig and your voice becomes a small, wobbling voltage. Between that voltage and the ionosphere, the radio has decisions to make: which property of an RF carrier your voice will steer, how the result gets moved onto the exact frequency you dialed, and how much of the spectrum the finished signal may occupy. Key a digital mode instead and the same decisions happen to data — a keyboard's worth of text squeezed into tones or phase shifts a few dozen hertz wide. In this chapter you'll learn the language for all of it: the three modulations and the envelope that betrays an overdriven signal, the mixer arithmetic inside every superheterodyne receiver, the two bandwidth calculations the exam actually makes you do, and the digital modes — RTTY, PSK31, FT8, WSPR — that own the lower ends of the HF bands. This is pool subelement G8: 42 questions, three of which will be on your exam.

### The three modulations and the envelope

Book 2 taught the modes as labels — FM for the repeater, SSB for HF, CW when conditions turn mean. General wants the next level down: what each one physically varies. A carrier on its own, a steady sine wave at one frequency, says nothing. Modulation is how you make it say something — you pick one property of that wave and let the information steer it. Three properties are available, which gives exactly the definition set the pool tests in G8A02, G8A03, and G8A05. Learn the three as one unit, because the distractors are invented vocabulary like "phase convolution" and "frequency transformation" — words that sound plausible and mean nothing.

- **Amplitude modulation (AM)** varies the signal's amplitude — its instantaneous power level follows the modulating waveform (G8A05). Your voice literally redraws how tall the wave is.

- **Frequency modulation (FM)** varies the signal's instantaneous frequency (G8A03) — the amplitude stays put and the frequency swings with the audio.

- **Phase modulation (PM)** varies the signal's phase angle (G8A02) — the wave's position within its own cycle.

FM and PM are close cousins — both are angle modulation, and wiggling either one wiggles the other — but the exam keeps them distinct, and one question is built to catch the reflex. Before the trap, the clean case. **Direct FSK** (frequency-shift keying) means the digital data signal drives the oscillator frequency itself (G8A01): the oscillator jumps between frequencies under the data's command, with no audio tones anywhere in the chain. That genuinely is FM, just with a square-wave modulating signal.

Now the trap, and it is the subtlest modulation question in the pool. Attach a **reactance modulator** — a circuit whose reactance varies with the modulating signal — to an RF amplifier stage *after* the oscillator, and the emission produced is **phase modulation** (G8A04). The reflex answer is FM, because reactance modulators are famous from FM transmitters. But direct FM wiggles the oscillator itself; once the oscillator's output exists and you push its timing around in a later amplifier stage, you are nudging the phase of an already-made wave — and nudging phase is PM. Where the modulator sits decides the answer: at the oscillator, FM; after the oscillator, PM.

Amplitude modulation earns its own inspection because three more G8A questions live there. Picture an AM signal on an oscilloscope: a fast RF sine whose height swells and shrinks with your voice. Connect the peaks of that RF waveform and the outline you trace is the **modulation envelope** (G8A11) — your voice waveform redrawn in the peaks. Drive the transmitter too hard and the peaks stop tracing your voice: the amplifier runs out of headroom and clips the envelope flat at the top. That is **flat-topping** — signal distortion caused by excessive drive or speech levels (G8A10). A clipped envelope is no longer a scaled copy of your voice; it contains sharp edges, sharp edges mean energy where none belongs, and on the band the result is an overmodulated signal occupying **excessive bandwidth** (G8A08) — the splatter you hear tearing up neighboring contacts. This is the same story chapter 2's ALC discipline tells from the operating side: drive the rig until the ALC just begins to work on peaks, never harder.

Bandwidth is exactly what single sideband was invented to save. Ordinary AM spends spectrum on a carrier plus two sidebands carrying identical information; SSB suppresses the carrier and one sideband and puts everything into the one that remains. Among the phone emissions the pool lists, **single sideband** is the narrowest (G8A07) — about 3 kHz of spectrum for a voice signal, against roughly twice that for AM and far more for FM. That completes the analog core of pool group G8A: G8A01 through G8A05, G8A07, G8A08, G8A10, and G8A11.

### Digital modulation and the link budget

Phase-shift keying moves the carrier's phase among a set of discrete angles, one symbol at a time. Basic BPSK uses two phases, 0° and 180°, so each symbol carries one bit. **Quadrature PSK (QPSK)** uses four — 0°, 90°, 180°, and 270° — so each symbol carries **two bits** (G8A12): with $2^2 = 4$ states per symbol, you ship twice the data at the same symbol rate in the same bandwidth. QPSK31 applies that trick inside PSK31's narrow channel, and the pool keys "all these choices are correct" for its properties (G8A06): it **is sideband-sensitive** — tune it with the wrong sideband selected and it won't decode — its encoding **provides error correction**, and its **bandwidth is approximately the same as BPSK31**, because the error-correction overhead exactly spends the capacity the second bit per symbol buys. Learn the three facts, not the answer shape — the same "all these choices are correct" line shows up as a distractor elsewhere in this subelement.

FSK went digital as well, and produced the most popular HF mode of the modern era: **FT8 is 8-tone FSK** (G8A09) — eight closely spaced tones, keyed one at a time in tiny hops. Its signal reports are not S-units. An FT8 report of "+3" means a **signal-to-noise ratio of +3 dB in a 2.5 kHz bandwidth** (G8C15): the software measures your signal against the noise in a standardized SSB-width channel and prints plain decibels, the same decibels chapter 5 drills. Positive numbers are healthy contacts, and negative numbers still decode — which is the mode's whole reason to exist, and the next section's story.

The last two G8A questions are arithmetic in words. A **link budget** accounts for everything that happens to a signal between two stations, as seen at the receiver: **add the transmit power and the antenna gains, and subtract all the losses** (G8A13). Start with 50 dBm (100 W) at the transmitter, subtract 2 dB of feed-line loss, add 8 dB of transmit antenna gain, subtract 130 dB of path loss, add 3 dB of receive antenna gain — the receiver sees −71 dBm. The **link margin** then answers "how much to spare?": it is the received level **minus the minimum the receiver needs** (G8A14). If that receiver works down to −80 dBm, the margin is 9 dB, and the link survives 9 dB of worsening before it fails. Every HF contact you make has this arithmetic underneath it; the ionosphere just keeps rewriting the path-loss term. That finishes pool group G8A — G8A06, G8A09, and G8A12 through G8A14 — with G8C15 riding along.

### Mixing, superhets, and the deviation math

Nearly every rig you have ever used is a superheterodyne, and every superheterodyne is built on one trick: combine two signals and new frequencies pop out. The process of combining two signals to produce those new frequencies is **heterodyning** (G8B03), the circuit that does it is the **mixer**, and what comes out is the **sum and difference** of the local-oscillator and RF frequencies (G8B11). Feed a mixer 14.250 MHz from the antenna and 23.250 MHz from the local oscillator and the output contains 37.500 MHz and 9.000 MHz. Keep the one you want — the 9.000 MHz difference — and let a filter throw the rest away.

{{fig:ch08-heterodyne}}

That difference frequency is the **intermediate frequency**, the IF, and it is the superhet's great bargain: all the precise, narrow, expensive filtering happens at one fixed frequency no matter where on the dial you sit. Tuning the radio means sliding the incoming station *onto* the IF, so **a superheterodyne receiver is tuned by varying its local oscillator** (G8B01) — the front-panel knob steers the LO, the LO parks a different station on the IF, and the IF strip never retunes.

The scheme has one built-in gremlin. Any signal sitting one IF away from the LO mixes down to the same IF, and there are always two such signals — one above the LO and one below. The one you didn't want is the **image**: an unwanted response **twice the IF away** from the desired signal (G8B02). Twice the IF — not twice the signal frequency, which is what the distractors bet you'll compute.

> **Worked example:** A superhet with a 455 kHz IF: the image response sits twice the IF away — 910 kHz from the desired signal. Listening on 14.250 MHz with the LO at 14.705 MHz, a signal on 15.160 MHz also lands on 455 kHz, and the receiver's front-end filtering has to kill it before the mixer ever sees it. Twice the IF, not twice the signal frequency.

Transmitters run the same arithmetic in reverse when they need a frequency the oscillator can't reach directly. A **multiplier** stage outputs **a harmonic of its input** (G8B04): feed it 12.21 MHz, select the twelfth harmonic, and out comes 146.52 MHz. That is exactly how a VHF FM transmitter reaches its operating frequency from a lower-frequency oscillator — a stable, low-frequency source multiplied up to 2 meters.

Multipliers multiply more than the carrier, and that fact powers the chapter's two exam calculations. First: how much spectrum does an FM signal occupy? Carson's rule gives the practical answer, $BW \approx 2 \times (D + f_{max})$ — twice the sum of the peak deviation and the highest modulating frequency.

> **Worked example:** An FM transmitter with 5 kHz peak deviation and 3 kHz maximum audio: BW ≈ 2 × (5 + 3) = **16 kHz** (G8B06). That number should look familiar from Book 2 — it is why 2-meter repeater channels sit 15 to 20 kHz apart.

Second: where does the deviation come from? Pass the carrier through a multiplier chain and the deviation multiplies by the same factor as the carrier — swing the oscillator a few hundred hertz and twelve multiplications later you have the full 5 kHz at the antenna.

> **Worked example:** A 146.52 MHz FM transmitter built on a 12.21 MHz oscillator: the multiplication factor is 146.52 ÷ 12.21 = 12, and deviation multiplies by the same factor — so for 5 kHz output deviation the oscillator needs only 5,000 ÷ 12 = **416.7 Hz** of deviation (G8B07). The chain gives you two gifts at once: a VHF carrier from a low-frequency oscillator, and full deviation from a gently wiggled one.

Those are the only two calculations G8 asks of you, and both use pool numbers, so the exam versions are free points once the arithmetic feels routine. That covers G8B01 through G8B04, G8B06, G8B07, and G8B11.

### Intermodulation, duty cycle, and bandwidth discipline

The mixer you just met is a deliberate, well-behaved non-linearity. Give nature an *accidental* non-linearity — a corroded connector, an overloaded amplifier, a rusty bolted joint — and two strong signals mixing there spawn spurious products on frequencies nobody intended: **intermodulation** (G8B12). The products are named by their **order**, the sum of the mixing coefficients, and the pool cares about one pattern: **odd-order products land closest to the original frequencies** (G8B05). Closeness is what makes them dangerous — an even-order product lands far enough away for a filter to catch, while an odd-order one drops right into your passband.

> **Worked example:** The order of an intermodulation product is the sum of its mixing coefficients: 2F1 − F2 → 2 + 1 = 3 — odd, so it lands close in. Its listed siblings run 3F1 − F2 → 4 and 5F1 − 3F2 → 8 — both even. Of the choices the pool offers, **2F1 − F2** is the odd-order product (G8B13).

Back to deliberate signals — some are simply harder on the transmitter than others. **Duty cycle** is the fraction of time the finals spend at full output, and the high-duty-cycle modes — FT8, RTTY, FM — key the transmitter at essentially 100 percent for whole sequences at a stretch. Chapter 7 showed where the wasted watts go: heat. Run one of these modes at a rig's rated PEP long enough and **the average power can exceed the transmitter's rating even though the PEP is fine** (G8B08). That is why the field custom on the digital bands is to run well below the rig's rated power — the weak-signal modes don't need brute force, and the finals appreciate the mercy. Custom, not exam law — but your future amplifier will thank you.

Receivers have a matching discipline. Noise arrives spread across the whole passband, so the narrower you filter without clipping the signal, the less noise you admit with it: **matching the receiver bandwidth to the mode gives the best signal-to-noise ratio** (G8B09). In practice that is roughly 3 kHz for SSB, 500 Hz or so for CW and RTTY, and the full width open for an FT8 window, where you want to watch dozens of stations at once. The transmit-side mirror of the same law: **higher symbol rates require wider bandwidth** (G8B10) — more symbols per second means more spectrum, which is why 45.45-baud RTTY fits in a CW-width channel.

On the exam, that symbol-rate principle is all you need — but since you are about to operate under the real rules, one update. The FCC's December 2023 Order replaced the old HF symbol-rate limits with a **2.8 kHz authorized-bandwidth standard below 28 MHz**: the rule now caps how wide your signal may be rather than how fast your symbols may go. No question in the 2023–2027 pool tests a maximum symbol rate, so if an older study guide drills a baud number, let it go. The current rule and the pool share the shape of the same idea — bandwidth is the currency, and symbol rate is one way to spend it.

That closes pool group G8B: G8B05, G8B08 through G8B10, G8B12, and G8B13.

### The digital modes in depth

The oldest of the HF data modes still works for a living, mostly in contests. **RTTY** — radio teletype — sends the 45.45-baud **Baudot code**, a **5-bit code with additional start and stop bits** (G8C04) wrapped around each character. Its two tones, 170 Hz apart by the convention chapter 2 pinned, are named **mark and space** (G8C11) — mark is the resting tone, space the other one. And if a properly tuned RTTY signal refuses to decode, chapter 2's checklist applies: reversed mark and space, wrong baud rate, or wrong sideband.

PSK31 took the opposite design philosophy: go slow, go narrow, stay conversational. Its **Varicode** assigns **short codes to the common letters** — e and t cost two or three bits — so an average message ships faster than any fixed-length code would allow, and the flip side is the pool's favorite corollary: **uppercase letters take longer to send** (G8C12, G8C08). That is why PSK31 operators really do type in lowercase, and it isn't laziness. You will find the mode hanging out at **14.070 MHz on 20 meters**, near the bottom of each band's data segment, on the same tour chapter 2 walked.

Two more modes exist less for conversation than for measurement. **WSPR** — Weak Signal Propagation Reporter — is a beacon mode: your station transmits its **call sign, grid locator, and power level** in 2-minute sequences (G8C02), receiving stations upload their reports to WSPRnet, and the resulting map of who heard whom answers the oldest antenna question there is. One evening of WSPR at a few watts is the zero-effort way to compare two antennas. FT8 you met in chapter 2 as an operating procedure; here the exam cares about its physics — it **decodes far below the noise** (G8C07), so deep that a report of −11 dB is a perfectly routine contact. That is precisely why its reports print as decibels of SNR instead of S-units.

Packet radio and its descendants worry about delivery, and four questions cover the machinery. Each frame carries its **routing and handling information in the header** (G8C03) — addresses and control data riding ahead of the payload. When a frame arrives damaged, the receiver answers **NAK**, negative acknowledgment, which means **"please retransmit"** (G8C05); the sender tries again, and that retry loop is ARQ, automatic repeat request. But mercy has limits: too many failed retries and the link **drops the connection** (G8C06) — better a clean disconnect than an endless argument. Notice that G8C06 is one of the questions where "all these choices are correct" appears as a distractor rather than the key, the mirror image of G8A06 — the retry-limit answer stands alone. The alternative philosophy is **forward error correction (FEC)**: send **redundant information along with the data** so the receiver can repair errors itself, without asking for a repeat (G8C10) — the right choice when there is no return channel or no time for one. And when a whole node fails rather than a single frame, a **mesh network** routes around the damage — a packet may still reach its target via an alternate node (G8C09), the trick behind AREDN, the amateur emergency data network from chapter 2.

All of these modes are watched, not just heard. A **waterfall display** paints a scrolling map of a slice of band: **frequency on the horizontal axis, time on the vertical axis, and signal strength as color or brightness** (G8C14). Learn to read one and every mode shows a signature — the figure draws three.

{{fig:ch08-digital-waterfalls}}

FT8 appears as short tone bursts inside alternating 15-second slots, many stations sharing one dial frequency at different audio pitches; PSK31 is a single narrow continuous trace about 31 Hz wide; RTTY is two steady traces, mark and space, 170 Hz apart. The waterfall doubles as a diagnostic instrument: a clean signal is a tidy rectangle, while **vertical lines flanking a signal indicate overmodulation** (G8C13) — splatter made visible, the same crime as G8A08's excessive bandwidth seen from the receiving end.

Last, voice went digital too. The three **digital voice** modes in amateur use are **DMR, D-STAR, and System Fusion** (G8C16) — DMR imported from the commercial two-way world, D-STAR and System Fusion built for the amateur bands, all three carrying your voice as data with error correction standing in for static. That completes pool group G8C — G8C02 through G8C16 — and with it the subelement.

**FACT:** AM varies the signal's amplitude (instantaneous power), FM varies its instantaneous frequency, and PM varies its phase angle.

**FACT:** Direct FSK means the digital signal drives the oscillator frequency itself, and a reactance modulator attached to an RF amplifier stage after the oscillator produces phase modulation.

**FACT:** Mixing two signals is heterodyning — a mixer outputs the sum and difference of the local oscillator and RF frequencies, a superheterodyne receiver is tuned by varying its local oscillator, and the image is an unwanted response twice the IF away from the desired signal.

**FACT:** Carson's rule gives FM bandwidth as approximately 2 × (peak deviation + highest modulating frequency) — 5 kHz deviation with 3 kHz audio gives 16 kHz — and deviation multiplies through a multiplier chain by the same factor as the carrier, so a 146.52 MHz transmitter with a 12.21 MHz oscillator (factor 12) needs 5 kHz ÷ 12 = 416.7 Hz of oscillator deviation for 5 kHz output deviation.

**FACT:** FT8 is 8-tone FSK exchanging minimal messages in timed 15-second transmit/receive sequences, and an FT8 signal report of "+3" means a signal-to-noise ratio of +3 dB in a 2.5 kHz bandwidth.

### Exam Focus

Pool subelement G8 — Signals and Emissions — is 42 questions in three groups (G8A, G8B, G8C), and your exam draws three of them, one per group. The ten below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each — including the reactance-modulator trap and both calculation questions.

> **G8A04** What emission is produced by a reactance modulator connected to a transmitter RF amplifier stage?
> A. Multiplex modulation
> B. Phase modulation
> C. Amplitude modulation
> D. Pulse modulation
> **Answer: B** — the modulator acts on an RF amplifier stage after the oscillator, so it shifts the phase of an already-generated wave — that is PM, not FM.

> **G8A06** Which of the following is characteristic of QPSK31?
> A. It is sideband sensitive
> B. Its encoding provides error correction
> C. Its bandwidth is approximately the same as BPSK31
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: D** — all three are true — QPSK31 is sideband-sensitive, its encoding provides error correction, and it fits about the same bandwidth as BPSK31.

> **G8A07** Which of the following phone emissions uses the narrowest bandwidth?
> A. Single sideband
> B. Vestigial sideband
> C. Phase modulation
> D. Frequency modulation
> **Answer: A** — SSB suppresses the carrier and one sideband, which makes it the narrowest phone emission.

> **G8A10** What is meant by the term “flat-topping,” when referring to an amplitude-modulated phone signal?
> A. Signal distortion caused by insufficient collector current
> B. The transmitter’s automatic level control (ALC) is properly adjusted
> C. Signal distortion caused by excessive drive or speech levels
> D. The transmitter’s carrier is properly suppressed
> **Answer: C** — flat-topping is distortion caused by excessive drive or speech levels — the clipped envelope then splatters wide.

> **G8B02** What is the term for interference from a signal at twice the IF frequency from the desired signal?
> A. Quadrature response
> B. Image response
> C. Mixer interference
> D. Intermediate interference
> **Answer: B** — the image is an unwanted response twice the IF away from the desired signal — twice the IF, not twice the signal frequency.

> **G8B06** What is the total bandwidth of an FM phone transmission having 5 kHz deviation and 3 kHz modulating frequency?
> A. 3 kHz
> B. 5 kHz
> C. 8 kHz
> D. 16 kHz
> **Answer: D** — Carson's rule: 2 × (5 kHz + 3 kHz) = 16 kHz — the worked example above.

> **G8B07** What is the frequency deviation for a 12.21 MHz reactance modulated oscillator in a 5 kHz deviation, 146.52 MHz FM phone transmitter?
> A. 101.75 Hz
> B. 416.7 Hz
> C. 5 kHz
> D. 60 kHz
> **Answer: B** — deviation divides back through the chain: 146.52 ÷ 12.21 = 12, so 5,000 ÷ 12 = 416.7 Hz — the worked example above.

> **G8B13** Which of the following is an odd-order intermodulation product of frequencies F1 and F2?
> A. 5F1-3F2
> B. 3F1-F2
> C. 2F1-F2
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: C** — the order is the sum of the coefficients: 2 + 1 = 3 is odd, while 3F1−F2 is order 4 and 5F1−3F2 is order 8.

> **G8C04** Which of the following describes Baudot code?
> A. A 7-bit code with start, stop, and parity bits
> B. A code using error detection and correction
> C. A 5-bit code with additional start and stop bits
> D. A code using SELCAL and LISTEN
> **Answer: C** — Baudot is RTTY's 5-bit code wrapped in start and stop bits — the code behind the mark and space tones.

> **G8C15** What does an FT8 signal report of +3 mean?
> A. The signal is 3 times the noise level of an equivalent SSB signal
> B. The signal is S3 (weak signals)
> C. The signal-to-noise ratio is equivalent to +3dB in a 2.5 kHz bandwidth
> D. The signal is 3 dB over S9
> **Answer: C** — an FT8 report is the signal-to-noise ratio measured in a standardized 2.5 kHz bandwidth — not S-units, not dB over S9.

### Key Takeaways

- AM varies the signal's amplitude (instantaneous power), FM varies its instantaneous frequency, and PM varies its phase angle — the three definitions travel as one set, and the distractors are invented words.

- A reactance modulator connected to an RF amplifier stage after the oscillator produces phase modulation, not FM — the pool's subtlest modulation trap. Direct FSK drives the oscillator itself.

- A mixer outputs the sum and difference of the RF and local-oscillator frequencies; a superhet is tuned by varying its local oscillator, and the image sits twice the IF away from the desired signal.

- Carson's rule: FM bandwidth is approximately 2 × (peak deviation + highest modulating frequency) — 5 kHz deviation with 3 kHz audio gives 16 kHz.

- Deviation multiplies through a multiplier chain by the same factor as the carrier: 146.52 ÷ 12.21 = 12, so 5 kHz of output deviation needs only 416.7 Hz at the oscillator.

- Intermodulation comes from signals combining in a non-linear circuit; odd-order products land closest to the originals, and 2F1 − F2 is the odd-order example at order 3.

- Match receiver bandwidth to the mode for the best signal-to-noise ratio; higher symbol rates need wider bandwidth — and the current rule caps HF data emissions at 2.8 kHz authorized bandwidth below 28 MHz.

- FT8 is 8-tone FSK, and a "+3" report means a signal-to-noise ratio of +3 dB in a 2.5 kHz bandwidth; on the waterfall, vertical lines flanking a signal mean overmodulation.
