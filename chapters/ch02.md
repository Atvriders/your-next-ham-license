## 2. Operating on HF

Your upgrade showed in ULS this morning, the /AG suffix is retired, and tonight you spin the big dial to 14.250 MHz, where a voice calls CQ through the static — clear as if he were across town, and he is across an ocean. On VHF you never called CQ; the repeater was always there. HF has its own manners, its own vocabulary, and its own customs, most of them unwritten and nearly all of them tested. In this chapter you will learn the operating craft behind pool subelement G2: which sideband to use and why, how to call CQ and break into a contact, the courtesy rules that keep a crowded band civil, the CW working set of Q-signals and prosigns, DX and contest basics, and the digital modes from RTTY to FT8 to Winlink.

### Phone on HF: one sideband, by convention

HF voice is single sideband, period (G2A05). An SSB signal transmits one sideband while the carrier and the other sideband are suppressed (G2A07). Throwing away the carrier and the duplicate sideband buys the two advantages the pool keys: less bandwidth used and greater power efficiency than other analog voice modes (G2A06). Every watt of transmitter power goes into the sideband that actually carries your voice, and SSB is the narrowest phone emission you can choose — on a crowded band, that is the whole point.

Which sideband? That is set by a convention every HF operator carries in their head, and the pool tests it from several directions. Voice below 10 MHz — the 160-, 75-, and 40-meter bands — is lower sideband. Voice at 10 MHz and above — 20, 17, 15, 12, and 10 meters, plus VHF and UHF SSB — is upper sideband (G2A01–G2A04). Notice what the 10 MHz boundary covers: 30, 17, and 12 meters are all above it, so they are USB bands. The shortcut some study aids repeat, "LSB below 14 MHz," gets the boundary wrong; keep the 10 MHz framing and you will answer every one of these questions correctly. And it is a convention — commonly accepted amateur practice, not regulation or physics (G2A09). Nothing in Part 97 forces it, but a station transmitting LSB on 20 meters will be answered by exactly nobody.

**FACT:** The voice sideband convention is LSB below 10 MHz (160, 75, and 40 meters) and USB at 10 MHz and above (20, 17, 15, 12, and 10 meters, and VHF/UHF SSB) — it is commonly accepted practice, not regulation or physics.

One band breaks the "custom, not law" pattern: 60 meters, where USB phone is written into the FCC rules themselves. The rest of that band's story — the current two-part structure of a contiguous segment plus four channels — is chapter 1's; here, file 60 meters as the exception that proves the convention everywhere else.

The figure maps the 20-meter band as the voluntary band plan zones it: CW at the bottom, digital modes between 14.070 and 14.100 MHz, the beacon at 14.100 MHz, then SSB phone from 14.150 to 14.350 MHz with SSTV at 14.230 and the AM calling frequency at 14.286. Every HF band follows the same skeleton — CW low, phone high, data in between — and the sideband conventions ride along as custom, not law.

Your dial reads the suppressed-carrier frequency, the spot where the carrier would sit if you had not suppressed it. Speech energy spreads about 3 kHz to one side of that number: above the dial reading on USB, below it on LSB. That spread sets the minimum separations the pool makes you memorize — 150 Hz to 500 Hz between CW stations, 2 kHz to 3 kHz between SSB stations (G2B04, G2B05). A CW signal a few hundred hertz wide can nestle between close neighbors; a 3 kHz phone signal cannot. How close to a band edge your sideband may legally hang is arithmetic, and chapter 4 works those problems; here the concept is enough.

**FACT:** When choosing a transmitting frequency, the minimum separation from other stations is 150 Hz to 500 Hz for CW and 2 kHz to 3 kHz for SSB.

> **Worked example:** Your USB dial reads 14.250 MHz on 20 meters, so your signal occupies roughly 14.250–14.253 MHz — the suppressed-carrier frequency plus about 3 kHz of speech energy above it. An SSB neighbor needs 2–3 kHz of separation from your signal; a CW neighbor can live 150–500 Hz away. That asymmetry is exactly why the CW end of every band packs more conversations into less spectrum than the phone end.

Phone procedure adds four more exam points. Breaking into a contact in progress: say your call sign once, dropped into a pause between the other stations' transmissions (G2A08) — not "break," not "breaker," just your call, once, and the stations decide whether to make room. Calling "CQ DX" from the contiguous 48 states invites answers from stations outside the lower 48 (G2A11); if a local answers anyway, courtesy is a quick contact, not a lecture. VOX is hands-free transmit/receive switching operated by your voice (G2A10) — net controls and contesters love the pace, but PTT is the safer default in a noisy shack, because the dog barking at the wrong moment keys the transmitter. And ALC, the automatic level control, is the transmitter's governor: set it with the transmit audio or microphone gain, driving until the ALC just begins to work on speech peaks (G2A12). Past that point the envelope flat-tops, the audio distorts, and your signal splatters wider than it should — one knob that links this question to the overmodulation material in chapter 8.

That is group G2A, twelve questions, plus the two separation questions G2B04 and G2B05.

### Courtesy, emergencies, and the band plan

The first rule of HF etiquette is actual law — §97.101(b): except during emergencies, no amateur station has priority access to any frequency (G2B01). Not nets. Not QSOs already in progress. Not contests, however many stations are on for the weekend. The frequency you have been chatting on for an hour is no more yours than the parking spot you vacated yesterday. When propagation shifts and two conversations land on one dial frequency, good amateur practice is to resolve the interference mutually (G2B03) — one station moves, or both share politely. Emergencies are the single exception, written into §97.101(c): a station handling emergency traffic gets the frequency, full stop.

**FACT:** Except during emergencies, no amateur has priority on any frequency — not nets, not QSOs in progress, not contests — and stations that begin interfering with each other should work it out mutually.

The ritual before you call anything: listen first — really listen, tuning a kilohertz each side, because a station a few hundred miles away can sit in your skip zone, inaudible to you, while his contact hears you perfectly. Then ask. On phone, "Is this frequency in use?" followed by your call sign; on CW, send "QRL?" followed by your call sign (G2B06, and G2C04 from the CW section). If silence answers, call. And choose where to call by the voluntary band plan (G2B07) — the zoning map from the figure. "Voluntary" means no FCC enforcement stands behind it, and it also means the whole system works because operators honor it.

If a station in distress breaks into your contact, everything else stops mattering. The first thing to do is acknowledge the station and determine what assistance may be needed (G2B02) — answer, find out what help is required, and be ready to relay. The distractor "immediately cease all transmissions" abandons someone who may be asking for exactly the help you can give.

Emergency communications has two organizations the exam wants kept straight. RACES — the Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service — is written into Part 97 as the civil-defense service: the control operator of a station transmitting in RACES must hold an FCC-issued amateur operator license (G2B09), certified by the civil defense organization served. Routine RACES training drills and tests may run no more than one hour per week without special authorization (G2B11). ARES, the Amateur Radio Emergency Service, is the ARRL's volunteer body — any amateur can join and drill, no civil-defense appointment required. RACES is the rulebook service; ARES is the volunteer club. Many operators carry both cards.

HF nets run on directed discipline you already know from VHF nets, scaled to coverage a repeater never reaches. A net control station runs the show, and good net management includes having a backup frequency ready in case of interference or poor conditions (G2B10). Two famous nets deserve their frequencies memorized as centers of activity: the Maritime Mobile Service Network meets on 14.300 MHz USB daily, passing traffic for vessels at sea, and the Hurricane Watch Net activates on 14.325 MHz USB by day and 7.268 MHz LSB by night when a storm threatens land. Learn these as traditions operating under §97.101(b), never as "assigned emergency frequencies" — nobody assigned them, and even the Hurricane Watch Net asks permission if the frequency is busy when it needs to activate. Custom, all the way down.

One more band-plan custom carries an exam question: 50.100–50.125 MHz on 6 meters is a voluntary DX window, where by band-plan agreement US stations within the 48 contiguous states make only contacts with stations outside the 48 contiguous states (G2B08). The window keeps a quiet slice where weak DX can be heard — voluntary again, band plan and not FCC law.

That is group G2B, eleven questions.

### CW on HF: Q-signals, prosigns, and break-in

Morse code is no longer a license requirement, but on HF it is alive and working: CW gets through when voice cannot, and every contest weekend proves it. The pool tests a working vocabulary — five Q-signals, two prosigns, and four pieces of technique.

**QRS?** means send slower (G2C02). It is the polite brake pedal — when a station answers your brisk call with QRS?, drop to a speed he can copy.

**QRL?** asks "are you busy?" or "is this frequency in use?" (G2C04) — the CW half of the pre-calling ritual from the last section.

**QSL** means "I have received and understood" (G2C09). The QSL card is literally that confirmation written out, which is why "QSL?" on the air can mean "did you copy?" or "will you send a card?" depending on context.

**QRN** means troubled by static (G2C10) — natural noise, the summertime crash of distant thunderstorms. Keep the classic swap straight: QRM is man-made interference. Static is nature; QRM is humanity.

**QRV** means ready to receive (G2C11) — "I am here and listening, go ahead."

Two prosigns — procedural signals sent as run-together letters — complete the set. **KN** at the end of a transmission means listening only for a specific station or stations (G2C03): it tells the rest of the band to stay out of this exchange. **AR** marks the end of a formal message (G2C08), the period at the end of a radiogram. The distractors are worth knowing as real vocabulary too: SK ends a contact, BK invites a break.

Technique now. Full break-in — QSK — means the transmitting station can receive between code characters and elements (G2C01): the receiver is alive between the dits, so the other operator can break you mid-word, and a non-QSK rig gives itself away with clacking relays. When you answer a CW CQ, use the fastest speed at which you are comfortable copying, but no faster than the CQ (G2C05) — answer a slow sender at 25 WPM and he will QRS you or give up. Zero beat means matching your transmit frequency to the frequency of the received signal (G2C06); with both stations running narrow filters, a call 300 Hz off may simply never be heard. And the RST report on CW adds a tone scale to readability and strength — a "C" suffix on the report means a chirpy or unstable signal (G2C07), the polite nudge that a vintage rig's oscillator is drifting.

Two culture notes round out the group. CW contest shorthand uses cut numbers — letters standing in for digits, N for 9 and T for 0 — so "599" is sent "5NN"; hearing it in November is shorthand, not a mystery prosign. And 73 means "best regards," already plural, never "73s." When you want practice, W1AW, the ARRL headquarters station, sends daily code practice from 5 to 35 WPM — the free on-air CW school.

That is group G2C, eleven questions.

### DXing, contests, and logging

Start with the watchers. Volunteer Monitors are amateur volunteers formally enlisted — via the ARRL, under an agreement with the FCC — to monitor the airwaves for rules violations (G2D01), and the program's objective is to encourage operators to self-regulate and comply with the rules (G2D02). It replaced the old Official Observer program in 2019, and a VM notice is coaching, not a citation. The pool's neat trick: when a station's continuous carrier is holding a repeater on, Volunteer Monitors can localize it by comparing beam headings on the repeater input from their home locations (G2D03) — where the headings cross is the transmitter. Keep the acronyms apart: a Volunteer Monitor is not a Volunteer Examiner (your exam team) and not a frequency coordinator (a VHF/UHF repeater job).

Maps and paths. An azimuthal projection map centered on your own location shows true bearings and distances from that specific spot — the DXer's map (G2D04). Great-circle intuition surprises most new Generals: from the Midwest, Europe is northeast and Japan is nearly north, over the pole. And every path has two directions. Long path means pointing the beam 180 degrees from the station's short-path heading (G2D06) — working the other way around the planet, sometimes with a twilight bonus that chapter 3's gray-line material explains.

Then there is split operation, the pileup survival technique. When a rare DX station comes on, hundreds of callers at once would bury him if everyone transmitted on his frequency, so he splits: he transmits on one frequency and listens on another, announcing "listening 5 to 10 up." You keep your receive VFO on his transmit frequency, set your transmit VFO inside his listening window — never on his own frequency — and you give your own call sign, not his.

{{fig:ch02-split-operation}}

> **Worked example:** A rare DX station is transmitting on 14.205 MHz, announcing "listening 5 to 10 up." His listening window starts at $14.205 + 0.005 = 14.210$ MHz and runs to 14.215 MHz. Keep your receive VFO on 14.205, set your transmit VFO anywhere inside that window — say 14.212 — and give your call once, then listen. Transmitting on 14.205 itself just adds to the roar on his frequency and guarantees you will not be heard. If you ever announce split yourself, the same rule inverts: check that both frequencies are clear before you transmit.

Everyday craft next. The CQ pattern the pool tests (G2D05): repeat "CQ" a few times, then "this is," then your call sign a few times, then pause to listen, and repeat as necessary. Several short CQs beat one endless one, and give the call with the standard NATO phonetic alphabet — Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta (G2D07) — because cute alternates confuse non-English speakers exactly when clarity matters most. Keeping a station log is voluntary — the FCC does not require one — but it helps with your reply if the FCC requests information about your station (G2D08); log in UTC and let software handle the bookkeeping. And signal reports go first in an exchange so each station can operate according to conditions (G2D11): an honest "you're 5 by 3 through the fading" tells the other operator to slow down and repeat what matters.

Contests bend that last rule, and the exam wants both truths. In a contest the report is a fixed formality — "59" on phone and "599" on CW are sent regardless of actual conditions — because the exchange has a job: it is the minimum information that scores. Call sign, signal report, plus whatever the sponsor defines. Real ARRL formats: November Sweepstakes wants a serial number, a precedence code, your call, a check (the two-digit year you were first licensed), and your ARRL section; Field Day wants transmitter class and section; IARU HF wants RST and ITU zone; in ARRL DX, W/VE stations send RST and state or province while DX stations send RST and power. What never changes is identification: contest rules never override FCC rules, so you identify your station according to normal Part 97 procedure mid-contest (G2D09). One truce holds the sport together: no contests on the WARC bands — 30, 17, and 12 meters — because those bands are narrow, and a contest weekend would crowd out everyone else. A new General's practical upshot: 17 meters stays civil on the biggest weekends.

{{fig:ch02-contest-exchange}}

QRP means low-power transmit operation (G2D10) — the pool defines it no further. The roughly 5-watt figure you will hear quoted is club and award custom, not a regulation; Sweepstakes even runs a QRP entry class. The appeal is the sport of it: five watts and a wire, working stations a kilowatt would barely notice.

QSLing closes the loop. "QSL," from the CW section, means "I have received and understood" (G2C09), and the QSL card is that confirmation written out. Modern confirmation runs three ways. LoTW — Logbook of The World — is the ARRL's database, where submitted electronic logs cross-match into confirmations with no paper at all, feeding WAS and DXCC award progress. The bureau route moves paper: outgoing cards flow through the ARRL's outgoing service to counterpart bureaus overseas, incoming cards arrive via your call-district bureau — cheap but slow, months to years. And direct mail with return postage handles the rare ones, following whatever route the DX station publishes.

That is group G2D, eleven questions.

### Digital operating on HF

HF digital modes put a sound card between you and the ionosphere, and they run their own sideband conventions — a pair that points in opposite directions, and the pool tests both. AFSK RTTY is traditionally sent using LSB (G2E01): audio tones fed into an SSB transmitter, lower sideband, by long tradition. The JT modes — JT65, JT9, FT4, and FT8 — are always sent using USB, on every band, including 80 and 40 meters where voice is LSB (G2E05). FT8 on 40 meters: USB. RTTY on 40 meters: LSB. Opposite conventions, same band, both keyed answers.

**FACT:** AFSK RTTY is traditionally sent using LSB, while JT65, JT9, FT4, and FT8 are always sent using USB — on every band, including 80 and 40 meters where voice is LSB.

RTTY — radioteletype — is the oldest data mode on HF. Its two tones are named mark and space, and the most common frequency shift between them on the amateur HF bands is 170 Hz (G2E06); 850 Hz was the old commercial standard, a distractor with a mustache. When an FSK signal is apparently tuned in properly but will not decode, the suspect list is short and the pool keys all of it: the mark and space frequencies may be reversed, you may have the wrong baud rate selected, or you may be listening on the wrong sideband (G2E14). The mechanics of the Baudot code underneath are chapter 8's.

FT8 is the mode that swallowed HF: dozens of stations share one dial frequency at different audio pitches on the waterfall, trading minimal messages in alternating 15-second transmit and receive slots. Two disciplines make it work. The clock: your computer time must be accurate to within approximately 1 second (G2E07) — that is the exam-facing number — and the signature symptom of a bad clock is a waterfall full of signals with zero decodes. Sync the clock to a network time source and the decodes come back. Slot manners: answer an FT8 CQ by finding a clear frequency during the alternate time slot to the calling station (G2E04) — never on the caller's own audio offset in his own slot, which just collides with him.

**FACT:** FT8 needs computer time accurate to about 1 second — the signature symptom of a bad clock is a waterfall full of signals with zero decodes.

> **Worked example:** FT8 runs alternating 15-second slots, so a minimal six-message contact — CQ, reply, report, report with R, RRR, 73 — takes six slots and completes in about 90 seconds when each station transmits in the slot opposite the other. If your clock is 3 seconds slow, every transmission lands outside the other station's decode window: the waterfall shows plenty of signals and nothing decodes. Fix the clock, not the antenna.

Where is everyone? The pool keys a common location for FT8 as approximately 14.074–14.077 MHz (G2E15), and most digital mode operations on 20 meters are found between 14.070 and 14.100 MHz (G2E08). PSK31 hangs out at 14.070 MHz, near the bottom of each band's data segment — and its Varicode gives common letters short codes, which is why PSK31 operators really do type in lowercase: uppercase letters take longer to send.

Winlink is amateur radio email, and the pool keys all three descriptions true at once: it is a wireless network for sending and receiving email, it works on both VHF and HF, and it is a form of packet radio (G2E12). You reach the system through gateways — a Winlink "Remote Message Server" is a gateway station (G2E13) — and you establish contact by transmitting a connect message on the station's published frequency (G2E10). Two link protocols carry the traffic. VARA is a digital protocol used with Winlink (G2E02). PACTOR connections are strictly two-station ARQ links — automatic repeat request, error-checked — and joining an existing contact is not possible (G2E09); a PACTOR link in session is a private conversation, not a net you can check into. Interference to a PACTOR or VARA transmission does not sound like static in a voice QSO; it shows up as frequent retries or timeouts, long pauses in message transmission, or failure to establish a connection at all (G2E03), because the protocols answer errors with retransmission until they give up.

One more name, filed under emergency data rather than HF: an AREDN mesh network provides high-speed data services during an emergency or community event (G2E11) — converted Wi-Fi gear on amateur microwave allocations, rerouting around failed nodes. Winlink on HF is email when everything between you and the outside world is down; AREDN is the data network when the internet is down at the site.

That is group G2E, fifteen questions — the biggest group in the subelement.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G2 (groups G2A–G2E) holds 60 pool questions, and your 35-question exam draws 5 of them — one per group. Here are ten, quoted exactly as the pool prints them, spanning all five groups; G2A01, G2E01, and G2E05 are the sideband-convention trio this chapter drilled.

> **G2A01** Which mode is most commonly used for voice communications on frequencies of 14 MHz or higher?
> A. Upper sideband
> B. Lower sideband
> C. Suppressed sideband
> D. Double sideband
> **Answer: A** — the convention puts voice on USB at 10 MHz and above, which covers everything at this question's 14 MHz boundary and beyond; 'suppressed sideband' is an invented mode, and double sideband is a real emission but not the common HF voice mode.

> **G2A07** Which of the following statements is true of single sideband (SSB)?
> A. Only one sideband and the carrier are transmitted; the other sideband is suppressed
> B. Only one sideband is transmitted; the other sideband and carrier are suppressed
> C. SSB is the only voice mode authorized on the 20-, 15-, and 10-meter amateur bands
> D. SSB is the only voice mode authorized on the 160-, 75-, and 40-meter amateur bands
> **Answer: B** — one sideband goes out while the other sideband and the carrier are suppressed; choice A sneaks the carrier back in, and C and D fail because other voice modes remain authorized on those bands.

> **G2B01** Which of the following is true concerning access to frequencies?
> A. Nets have priority
> B. QSOs in progress have priority
> C. Except during emergencies, no amateur station has priority access to any frequency
> D. Contest operations should yield to non-contest use of frequencies
> **Answer: C** — §97.101(b) gives nobody priority — not nets, not QSOs, not contests — and §97.101(c) carves out the one exception, emergencies.

> **G2B08** What is the voluntary band plan restriction for US stations transmitting within the 48 contiguous states in the 50.1 MHz to 50.125 MHz band segment?
> A. Only contacts with stations not within the 48 contiguous states
> B. Only contacts with other stations within the 48 contiguous states
> C. Only digital contacts
> D. Only SSTV contacts
> **Answer: A** — the 6-meter DX window is voluntary band-plan custom: inside the 48 contiguous states you transmit there only to work stations outside them.

> **G2C01** Which of the following describes full break-in CW operation (QSK)?
> A. Breaking stations send the Morse code prosign “BK”
> B. Automatic keyers, instead of hand keys, are used to send Morse code
> C. An operator must activate a manual send/receive switch before and after every transmission
> D. Transmitting stations can receive between code characters and elements
> **Answer: D** — QSK keeps your receiver alive between the dits and dahs, so the other station can break you mid-word; the 'BK' distractor is a different prosign entirely.

> **G2C02** What should you do if a CW station sends “QRS?”
> A. Send slower
> B. Change frequency
> C. Increase your power
> D. Repeat everything twice
> **Answer: A** — QRS? is the polite brake pedal — slow your sending to whatever the other station can copy.

> **G2D06** How is a directional antenna pointed when making a “long-path” contact with another station?
> A. Toward the rising sun
> B. Along the gray line
> C. 180 degrees from the station’s short-path heading
> D. Toward the north
> **Answer: C** — long path points the beam the opposite way around the globe; the gray line is a different tool — taught in chapter 3.

> **G2D09** Which of the following is required when participating in a contest on HF frequencies?
> A. Submit a log to the contest sponsor
> B. Send a QSL card to the stations worked, or QSL via Logbook of The World
> C. Identify your station according to normal FCC regulations
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: C** — contest rules never override Part 97, so normal station identification applies mid-contest; logs and cards are the sponsor's business, not the FCC's.

> **G2E01** Which mode is normally used when sending RTTY signals via AFSK with an SSB transmitter?
> A. USB
> B. DSB
> C. CW
> D. LSB
> **Answer: D** — AFSK RTTY traditionally rides LSB — the mirror opposite of the FT8 family's USB in G2E05, and the pool tests both conventions.

> **G2E05** What is the standard sideband for JT65, JT9, FT4, or FT8 digital signal when using AFSK?
> A. LSB
> B. USB
> C. DSB
> D. SSB
> **Answer: B** — the JT modes use USB on every band, even 80 and 40 meters where voice is LSB — the mirror opposite of G2E01's RTTY custom.

### Key Takeaways

- The voice sideband convention is LSB below 10 MHz (160, 75, and 40 meters) and USB at 10 MHz and above (20, 17, 15, 12, and 10 meters, and VHF/UHF SSB) — commonly accepted practice, not law or physics; 60-meter phone is USB by rule. Digital runs its own opposite pair: AFSK RTTY on LSB, and JT65, JT9, FT4, and FT8 on USB on every band.

- HF voice is single sideband: one sideband transmitted, the carrier and the other sideband suppressed. The dial reads the suppressed-carrier frequency, speech spreads about 3 kHz to one side, and minimum separations are 150–500 Hz for CW and 2–3 kHz for SSB.

- Nobody owns a frequency: except during emergencies, no station has priority — not nets, not QSOs, not contests — and interfering stations work it out mutually. Hear a station in distress and your first move is to acknowledge it and determine what assistance is needed.

- Before calling, listen, then ask — "Is this frequency in use?" or "QRL?" plus your call sign — and follow the voluntary band plan. Net frequencies like 14.300 and 14.325 are centers of activity by custom, never assignments.

- The CW working set: QRS? send slower; QRL? is this frequency in use; QSL received and understood; QRN troubled by static; QRV ready to receive; prosign KN listens only for specific stations and AR ends a formal message; QSK hears between code elements.

- In split operation the DX transmits on one frequency and listens on another — call inside his announced window, never on his transmit frequency, giving your own call once, then listening.

- A contest exchange is the minimum that scores, the 59 or 599 report is a formality, and FCC identification rules still apply mid-contest; everyday contacts get honest reports, exchanged first so both stations can adapt to conditions. No contests on the WARC bands.

- FT8 discipline: USB on every band, computer time accurate to about 1 second (a waterfall full of signals with zero decodes means fix the clock), and answer a CQ on a clear frequency in the alternate time slot.
