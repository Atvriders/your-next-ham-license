## 6. Components & Devices

Open the case of the HF rig you just bought — or picture the schematic of the one you are saving for — and every stage on the board is a small crowd of parts, each doing one job for you at HF: a diode dropping a fixed slice of voltage, a transistor slammed fully on and fully off, a ferrite bead quietly strangling an unwanted current on a cable shield, a connector rated for far higher frequency than you will ever send through it. Book 2 introduced these parts at picture-book level — the resistor resists, the capacitor stores charge, the diode conducts one way. This chapter gives each one its General-depth personality: the thresholds, parasitics, and families the exam actually asks about. In this chapter you'll learn the battery floor that protects your station power, the diode threshold pair the pool loves to swap, why a wire-wound resistor is secretly a coil, how a ferrite's mix sets its working frequencies, and which connectors belong on RF cables and which one never does. This is pool subelement G6 — Circuit Components: 23 questions in two groups, two of which will be on your exam.

### Passives with personality: batteries, capacitors, and parasitics

Start with the part that powers everything else. A standard 12-volt lead-acid battery — the brick in your go-box, the bank behind your emergency station, the one under your car's hood — is "dead" long before its terminals read zero. Lead-acid chemistry punishes deep discharge: run the battery down far enough and lead sulfate hardens onto the plates, permanently shaving capacity off every cycle that follows. For maximum life, a standard 12-volt lead-acid battery should never be discharged below **10.5 V** (G6A01). That is the minimum allowable discharge voltage, and it arrives sooner than you might think — under load, a battery reading 10.5 V is empty for practical purposes. The exam's distractors are 6, 8.5, and 12 volts: 12 is where a rested battery starts, and anything below 10.5 is borrowed against the battery's future.

Inside the battery, **internal resistance** acts like a small resistor wired in series with a perfect voltage source. You cannot remove it; you can only feel it. Draw a heavy load — key the 100-watt rig on a 20 A transmit peak — and some of the battery's voltage is lost across that internal resistor before it ever reaches the rig. The voltage at the terminals sags, the rig's undervoltage protection may trip, and your signal stutters. A battery with lower internal resistance shows less sag under the same load, which is why the pool keys "high discharge current" as the advantage of low internal resistance (G6A02). Note what the keyed answer is not: not longer life, not higher voltage, not faster recharge. Ampere-hours tell you how long the battery runs; internal resistance tells you how hard it can be pushed right now. It is the same physics that lets a healthy car battery crank an engine on a cold morning while a tired one — same voltage on the bench, far more resistance inside — cannot.

> **Worked example:** You can feel internal resistance with arithmetic. Key the rig and suppose the battery must deliver 20 A on a transmit peak. If the pack's internal resistance is 0.1 ohm, that current loses 20 × 0.1 = 2 V inside the battery before a single watt reaches the rig — a fully charged 12.6 V pack shows only 10.6 V at its terminals, a whisper above the 10.5 V floor from G6A01. Halve the internal resistance to 0.05 ohm and the same 20 A costs only 1 V: the terminals hold at 11.6 V, the undervoltage alarm stays quiet, and the full power goes out on the air. That is G6A02 in one line — lower internal resistance means higher available discharge current, not more stored energy. Ampere-hours are the size of the tank; internal resistance is the width of the pipe.

Capacitors come in families, and the pool tests two personalities that it deliberately swaps. The **electrolytic capacitor** is the capacitance pack mule: its one great virtue is high capacitance in a small volume (G6A04), which is why power-supply filters and audio coupling stages are full of them. Everything else about an electrolytic is a compromise — it leaks, its tolerance is loose, it is polarized (wire it backward and it can fail spectacularly), and its internal construction makes it a poor choice at radio frequencies. The low-voltage **ceramic capacitor** has the opposite personality: modest capacitance, decent RF manners, and above all low cost (G6A08) — pennies each, sprinkled across every board in the rig as bypass and coupling parts. Crack open any HF radio and you can spot the division of labor at a glance: the big cans near the power supply are electrolytics, the peppering of little discs around every IC pin is ceramic. The exam plants "high capacitance for volume" in the ceramic question and "low cost" in the electrolytic question, hoping you will grab the familiar phrase first. Volume belongs to the electrolytic; cheap belongs to the ceramic. And neither family is "precise" — tight tolerance is what film and mica capacitors are for, not these two.

Now for the stowaways. Every real component carries **parasitics** — unwanted properties that ride along with the one you wanted. A wire-wound resistor is built exactly the way its name says: resistance wire wound into a coil. A coil is an inductor, so a wire-wound resistor comes with free inductance whether you ordered it or not. At DC and audio frequencies that parasitic inductance is harmless. At HF, where inductive reactance rises with frequency (chapter 5's drill), the coil inside your "resistor" adds reactance that changes from band to band — which is why the pool says the inductance of a wire-wound resistor makes its characteristics at radio frequencies unpredictable (G6A06). The part is fine for a power supply bleeder; do not build an HF dummy load out of one.

Inductors have their own stowaway, and it works in the opposite direction. The turns of a coil sit close together, and wherever two conductors sit close together there is capacitance — so every inductor carries a small parasitic capacitance effectively wired across itself. An inductor in parallel with a capacitor is a resonant circuit, and every real inductor therefore has a **self-resonant frequency**, whether or not anyone printed it on the datasheet. Below that frequency the part behaves like the inductor you paid for. Above it, the parasitic capacitance wins the tug-of-war: operated above its self-resonant frequency, an inductor becomes capacitive (G6A11). This is not exotic knowledge — it is why RF chokes are specified for particular frequency ranges, and why a choke that tames common-mode current beautifully on 40 meters can be useless or worse on 10. Part behavior at HF is frequency behavior.

### Diodes, transistors, and tubes: thresholds and endpoints

A diode is a one-way valve with a price of admission. Push current through it in the forward direction — into the **anode**, out of the **cathode** — and nothing much happens until the voltage across the junction reaches the **forward threshold voltage**. Below the threshold the diode barely conducts; above it, current climbs steeply and the diode pins its forward drop near that threshold value. The threshold is a property of the semiconductor material, and the pool tests it as a deliberate pair. The approximate forward threshold voltage of a germanium diode is **0.3 volts** (G6A03); of a silicon junction diode, **0.7 volts** (G6A05). The two questions share one option set — 0.1, 0.3, 0.7, and 1.0 volts — so each answer sits in the other question as a distractor. Learn them as a pair and the trap disarms itself: germanium 0.3, silicon 0.7. Silicon is the modern default, which is why 0.7 volts shows up everywhere from rectifier drops to the base-emitter junctions of transistors; germanium's lower threshold keeps it alive in small-signal detectors and a few vintage circuits.

The light-emitting member of the family plays by the same rule. An LED is a diode whose junction releases energy as light instead of heat — but only when the junction is actually conducting, which means only when forward biased (G6B08). Reverse-bias it and you get no light at all, just a stressed junction. In a real circuit the LED always keeps a series resistor nearby to set its current, because once past threshold the diode's own drop stays nearly fixed and everything left over lands across the resistor. Every panel lamp, tuner indicator, and "ON AIR" sign in the shack is a forward-biased diode doing its threshold trick with photons as the byproduct.

> **Worked example:** Thresholds cost real volts, and G6A01's battery floor tells you how many you can afford. Suppose you wire two silicon junction diodes in series between your 12-volt battery and a small project circuit — one as a reverse-polarity guard, one as a crude regulator. Each drops about 0.7 volts: 12 − 0.7 − 0.7 = 10.6 V at the project. That is uncomfortably close to the 10.5 V floor where a standard lead-acid battery should stop being discharged — the diodes eat your safety margin before the battery even gets a say. Drop the regulator and keep only the reverse-polarity guard, and the project sees 12 − 0.7 = 11.3 V: cheap insurance against a crossed cable, paid for in volts. The same arithmetic is why a series diode makes sense as a solar panel's nighttime discharge guard (chapter 4's portable-power questions), where one 0.7-volt drop buys protection for the whole battery.

The bipolar junction transistor — the BJT, the NPN and PNP on the panel — can be run two ways, and keeping them apart is the key to G6A07. Run it in its middle, the **active region** between fully off and fully on, and it is a linear device: collector current follows base current smoothly, waveforms in, amplified waveforms out. That is the amplifier's home, and the amplifier classes built on it (A, AB, B, C) are chapter 7's territory. Run it as a **switch**, though, and the middle is forbidden territory — a switch should never linger halfway. A bipolar transistor used as a switch lives at its endpoints: **saturation**, driven hard fully on with maximum collector current and only a fraction of a volt across it, and **cutoff**, based at zero and fully off (G6A07). Relay drivers, keying transistors, and logic outputs all commute between those two endpoints and treat the active region as a corridor to pass through quickly. The exam's distractor list for G6A07 borrows "enhancement and depletion modes" from field-effect vocabulary and "peak and valley" from tunnel-diode lore — neither belongs to a BJT switch.

The field-effect side of the panel answers a different structural question. In a **MOSFET**, the gate — the control electrode — is insulated from the channel by a thin insulating layer (G6A09), historically silicon dioxide, which is where the older name IGFET (insulated-gate FET) comes from. Because the gate never touches the channel, essentially no gate current flows: the gate controls the channel's width purely by its electric field. That gives the MOSFET its enormous input impedance — a voltmeter-like touch that barely loads whatever it watches — and its one famous handling quirk: the insulating layer is thin enough that a static spark from your finger can punch through it, which is why MOSFET parts ship with their leads shorted and why grounded workspaces matter when you solder them. The JFET on the panel is the MOSFET's older cousin — same voltage-controlled channel, but its gate is a reverse-biased junction rather than an insulated plate, so it is sturdier to handle and still presents very high input impedance. Your rig's RF power stages are almost certainly MOSFETs, and its receiver front ends exploit that high input impedance every time you tune.

Tubes are older than every other part on the panel, and they are not gone — the RF deck of a legal-limit HF amplifier is still very likely a vacuum tube, glowing gently while it hands you a kilowatt and a half. A heated **cathode** boils off a cloud of electrons; the **plate**, held at a high positive voltage, collects them; and between the two sits the **control grid**, a mesh that meters the electron flow from cathode to plate (G6A10). A small voltage wiggle on the control grid throttles a large electron stream, and that — a little signal controlling a big one — is amplification, the same job the transistor does with junctions. The grid is a valve handle on the electron flow, which is why the British call tubes valves.

The triode's weakness is built into its geometry: the control grid and the plate are two conductors facing each other, and two facing conductors are a capacitor. At RF, that grid-to-plate capacitance feeds the amplified output straight back into the input, and an amplifier fed by its own output is an oscillator waiting to happen — the self-oscillation that chapter 7's neutralization exists to cancel. The classic fix added a second mesh between control grid and plate: the **screen grid**, held at a steady RF-ground potential, which shields the control grid from the plate. The screen grid exists to reduce grid-to-plate capacitance (G6A12) — that is its whole exam personality. A tube with cathode, control grid, screen grid, and plate is a tetrode; add a third grid and it is a pentode. The big tetrodes in HF amplifiers owe their tame, neutralization-free behavior to that screen.

That completes G6A's semiconductor and tube questions — G6A03, G6A05, G6A07, G6A09, G6A10, and G6A12 — with the LED's G6B08 waiting for you in group G6B, which is where the chapter goes next.

### ICs, ferrites, and the connectors on the back panel

An **integrated circuit** puts an entire circuit — transistors, resistors, sometimes diodes and capacitors, already wired together — onto one chip of semiconductor. "Monolithic" means exactly that: one stone, a single crystal of silicon carrying the whole design. Scale the idea up to microwave frequencies and you get the **MMIC**, the Monolithic Microwave Integrated Circuit (G6B02) — a complete amplifier that works from DC into the gigahertz range, inside a package the size of a match head. MMICs are the invisible gain blocks of modern radio: the low-noise amplifier at the top of a satellite dish, the broadband preamp inside your SDR dongle, the driver stages in handhelds. The exam asks only for the expansion of the acronym, and the distractors — Multi-Mode, Metal Monolayer, Mode Modulated — are invented; "monolithic microwave" is the one that means something.

Digital ICs come in families too, and the pool's comparison is **CMOS** versus **TTL**. TTL — transistor-transistor logic, the bipolar family that defined early digital design — draws substantial current continuously, whether its gates are switching or sitting still. CMOS — complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor logic, built from paired MOSFETs — draws almost nothing except during the instant a gate actually switches states. The advantage the pool keys is power consumption (G6B03): CMOS wins it, and by an enormous margin. That one fact explains the battery-powered world — why your handheld's logic sips while a 1970s TTL board gulped, and why CMOS is the default anywhere milliamps matter. When the distractors offer speed or supply voltage as CMOS's headline advantage, remember what the pool actually asks: power.

The **op-amp** — operational amplifier — is the panel's analog counterweight to all that logic. It is a high-gain differential amplifier on a chip: two inputs, one output, and so much raw gain that external feedback components decide what it actually does. With two resistors of feedback it is a precision amplifier whose gain is the resistor ratio and nothing else; with capacitors added, a filter; with positive feedback, an oscillator. That division of labor is the whole design philosophy — the chip supplies brute-force gain, the surrounding parts supply the discipline. Whatever the surrounding circuit asks, the device itself is unambiguous: an op-amp is an **analog** integrated circuit (G6B06), built for continuous voltages rather than ones and zeros. Chapter 7 puts op-amps to work in active filters and audio stages; here the exam point is the family name.

Ferrites earn three questions, and they all start from what a ferrite is: a dark ceramic made by blending iron oxide with other metal oxides and firing it into a solid that is magnetic but does not conduct electricity — so alternating magnetic fields can live in it without the eddy-current losses that would heat an iron slug. The blend is the whole story. The composition, or **mix**, of materials used determines the performance of a ferrite core at different frequencies (G6B01): one mix is formulated to be lossy across the HF spectrum (exactly what you want in an interference choke), another stays efficient at VHF, another is optimized for switchmode power transformers. A core's part number is really a mix number, and choosing a ferrite means choosing the mix whose frequency behavior matches your job — the wrong mix at your frequency is either an open window or a space heater.

Wind a coil on a ferrite **toroid** — the donut-shaped core — and three advantages arrive together, which is why the pool keys "all these choices are correct" on G6B05. The core's permeability concentrates the magnetic field, giving you large inductance in a small volume — dozens of microhenries where an air coil would sprawl. Cores come in frequency-optimized mixes, so you can pick a toroid whose material is at its best exactly where you operate. And the closed donut geometry keeps the magnetic field almost entirely inside the core: no stray field reaching into neighboring circuits, no coupling to the shield can a quarter-inch away. Large inductance, frequency-optimized cores, self-contained fields — all three, genuinely. One caution, and it applies pool-wide: "all these choices are correct" is a distractor about as often as it is the keyed answer, so answer every question from the content, never from the shape of the option list.

The third ferrite question is the one you already use. Snap a ferrite bead over a coax cable — or wind the coax a few turns through a big toroid — and you have built a **common-mode choke**. The wanted current inside the coax is differential: it flows out on the center conductor and back on the inside of the shield, the two magnetic fields canceling in the core, so the bead ignores it completely. The unwanted current is common-mode: RF flowing along the outside of the shield as if the shield were a single wire, picked up from your antenna or your own transmissions. That current does not cancel in the core — it sees the bead's full impedance placed directly in its path (G6B10) and is choked off. This is the mechanism underneath chapter 4's RFI cures — the bead on the stereo's speaker leads, the toroid choke at the feed point — and it is why a one-dollar part can end an interference complaint. It is also why the cure works on your own station: the same choke on your coax stops your antenna's feed line from becoming part of the antenna, keeping the pattern you paid for.

The last drill in the group is pure recognition: four connectors, four personalities, one of them an impostor.

- **BNC** — the bayonet connector: push on, quarter-turn, locked. Handy on bench gear, handhelds, and anything you connect and disconnect often, with a typical upper frequency limit for low-SWR operation of 50-ohm BNC connectors at about **4 GHz** (G6B04).

- **Type N** — the threaded, weather-sealed workhorse: moisture-resistant and useful to **10 GHz** (G6B07), which is why it stands outdoors on antenna masts and feed-line entries where BNC would drown.

- **SMA** — the small threaded connector, good to **several GHz** (G6B11): the little gold screw-on fitting on handheld radios, SDR receivers, and Wi-Fi gear.

- **RCA phono** — the impostor: not an RF connector at all, but the connector used for low-frequency or DC connections to a transceiver (G6B12) — amplifier keying lines, accessory audio, control signals. The question's other choices (PL-259, BNC, type N) are all genuine RF connectors; the pool wants the odd one out, and it is the same red-and-white plug from the back of a stereo.

The pattern behind the drill is worth keeping: every RF connector trades size, weather sealing, and frequency reach against convenience, and the names line up with the jobs you already know from the shack. BNC where you swap cables daily, type N where the cable lives outdoors, SMA where space runs out, and RCA phono only where the signal is audio, control voltage, or DC. Match the connector to the job's frequency and weather, and the four exam answers follow without memorization.

Those four close out subelement G6's second group — the eleven questions of G6B, covering IC families, ferrites, the LED, and connectors. Together with G6A's passives, semiconductors, and tubes, the whole subelement is now taught: 23 questions, two of them yours on exam day.

**FACT:** A standard 12-volt lead-acid battery should not be discharged below 10.5 V for maximum life, and a battery's internal resistance acts like a series resistor — lower internal resistance means higher available discharge current.

**FACT:** The approximate forward threshold voltage of a germanium diode is 0.3 volts and of a silicon junction diode 0.7 volts, and an LED emits light when forward biased.

**FACT:** A wire-wound resistor's inductance makes its RF behavior unpredictable, and an inductor driven above its self-resonant frequency becomes capacitive.

**FACT:** MMIC stands for Monolithic Microwave Integrated Circuit, CMOS beats TTL on power consumption, and an op-amp is an analog IC.

**FACT:** The BNC connector is a bayonet connector usable to about 4 GHz, the type N connector is moisture-resistant and useful to 10 GHz, the SMA is a small threaded connector good to several GHz, and the RCA phono connector is the non-RF connector used for low-frequency or DC connections to a transceiver.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G6 — Circuit Components — is 23 pool questions in two groups (G6A and G6B), and your exam draws two of them, one per group. The eight below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each.

> **G6A01** What is the minimum allowable discharge voltage for maximum life of a standard 12-volt lead-acid battery?
> A. 6 volts
> B. 8.5 volts
> C. 10.5 volts
> D. 12 volts
> **Answer: C** — 10.5 volts is the floor; discharging a lead-acid battery below it shortens its life.

> **G6A03** What is the approximate forward threshold voltage of a germanium diode?
> A. 0.1 volt
> B. 0.3 volts
> C. 0.7 volts
> D. 1.0 volts
> **Answer: B** — germanium conducts above about 0.3 volts; the 0.7-volt choice is silicon's answer planted beside it.

> **G6A05** What is the approximate forward threshold voltage of a silicon junction diode?
> A. 0.1 volt
> B. 0.3 volts
> C. 0.7 volts
> D. 1.0 volts
> **Answer: C** — silicon junctions conduct above about 0.7 volts; the 0.3-volt choice is germanium's answer.

> **G6A07** What are the operating points for a bipolar transistor used as a switch?
> A. Saturation and cutoff
> B. The active region (between cutoff and saturation)
> C. Peak and valley current points
> D. Enhancement and depletion modes
> **Answer: A** — a switch lives at the endpoints: saturation fully on, cutoff fully off; the active region is the linear amplifier's home.

> **G6A11** What happens when an inductor is operated above its self-resonant frequency?
> A. Its reactance increases
> B. Harmonics are generated
> C. It becomes capacitive
> D. Catastrophic failure is likely
> **Answer: C** — past self-resonance the winding's parasitic capacitance dominates, and the part behaves like a capacitor.

> **G6B01** What determines the performance of a ferrite core at different frequencies?
> A. Its conductivity
> B. Its thickness
> C. The composition, or “mix,” of materials used
> D. The ratio of outer diameter to inner diameter
> **Answer: C** — the ferrite's material mix sets the frequency range where the core performs.

> **G6B02** What is meant by the term MMIC?
> A. Multi-Mode Integrated Circuit
> B. Monolithic Microwave Integrated Circuit
> C. Metal Monolayer Integrated Circuit
> D. Mode Modulated Integrated Circuit
> **Answer: B** — MMIC expands to Monolithic Microwave Integrated Circuit: a complete microwave amplifier on one chip.

> **G6B04** What is a typical upper frequency limit for low SWR operation of 50-ohm BNC connectors?
> A. 50 MHz
> B. 500 MHz
> C. 4 GHz
> D. 40 GHz
> **Answer: C** — BNC holds low SWR to about 4 GHz; above that, the threaded type N or SMA takes over.

### Key Takeaways

- A standard 12-volt lead-acid battery should never be discharged below 10.5 V; low internal resistance — not extra ampere-hours — is what buys high discharge current.

- Diode thresholds come as a pair: germanium 0.3 volts, silicon 0.7 volts, sharing one option set on the exam. An LED emits light only when forward biased.

- Capacitor personalities: electrolytics pack high capacitance into small volume (and nothing else is good about them); low-voltage ceramics are above all cheap. The pool swaps the two answers.

- Parasitics rule at HF: a wire-wound resistor's coil inductance makes its RF behavior unpredictable, and an inductor driven above its self-resonant frequency becomes capacitive.

- A BJT used as a switch lives at saturation and cutoff; a MOSFET's gate is insulated from its channel; in a tube, the control grid meters electron flow and the screen grid reduces grid-to-plate capacitance.

- Ferrites: the mix sets the working frequency range, toroids give large inductance, frequency-optimized cores, and self-contained fields all at once, and a bead chokes common-mode shield current by putting impedance in its path.

- Connectors: BNC bayonet to about 4 GHz, type N moisture-resistant to 10 GHz, SMA small and threaded to several GHz — and the RCA phono is the one that is not an RF connector.
