## 3. Propagation in Depth

Tune across 20 meters at noon and the band roars — stations from three continents stacked shoulder to shoulder. Come back to the same dial spot at midnight and it can sound abandoned, while 40 meters has come alive with exactly the long-distance signals that were missing all day. Nothing about your station changed; the sky did. In this chapter you'll learn what the ionosphere actually is — regions, not a mirror — how the Sun drives it through the solar cycle, how to read a path's MUF and LUF, why skip zones and scatter go together, and how NVIS and the gray line turn that knowledge into contacts. This is pool subelement G3: 37 questions, three of which will be on your exam.

### The ionosphere, properly

Book 2's propagation chapter drew the first dividing line: VHF and UHF travel mostly line-of-sight, while HF can skip off the sky and land hundreds or thousands of miles away. This chapter builds the machinery behind that skip. High above the weather, ultraviolet and X-ray radiation from the Sun strips electrons from the thin upper atmosphere, leaving free electrons stacked in distinct regions. A radio wave entering one of these regions is not bounced off it like light off a mirror — it is refracted, bent gradually as it passes through, and when the bending is strong enough the wave curves back down to Earth. That refracted path is the **skywave**, and everything in HF propagation is the story of when the bending works and when it fails.

How strongly a region bends a wave depends on the wave's frequency. Lower frequencies are bent easily — even modest ionization turns them back — while higher frequencies need dense ionization to refract at all, and above some frequency, for the conditions of the moment, the bending falls short and the wave escapes into space. That single idea, frequency-dependent bending, explains nearly everything in this chapter: why the low bands always come back but get absorbed along the way, why the high bands need a strong Sun, and why every skywave path has both a floor and a ceiling.

The regions stack from lowest to highest as **D**, **E**, **F1**, and **F2** — the pool asks which is closest to Earth's surface, and the answer is the D region (G3C01). The F2 region sits highest, and height is reach: because F2 is the highest region, skip propagation via F2 is longer than via any other region (G3C03). The geometry is intuitive — the higher the refracting region, the farther away the signal comes back down.

If you met sporadic E in the Technician course — those surprise summer openings on 6 and 10 meters — you have already seen how patchy, intense ionization in the E region can refract even VHF signals back to Earth. The same refraction is at work here; on HF it is simply the everyday mechanism rather than a seasonal surprise.

Ionization comes from the Sun, so the stack breathes on a daily rhythm. By day, solar radiation strengthens every region and the D region appears in force. At night the D region fades away, F1 merges into F2, and the surviving F region goes on refracting — thinner, but still useful. The figure shows both states side by side.

The D region earns special attention because it is the **daytime absorber**. It is the most absorbent region for signals below 10 MHz during daylight hours (G3C11): 40, 60, 80, and 160 meter signals launched in daylight are soaked up before they ever reach the refracting regions above, which is exactly why long-distance communication on those bands is harder during the day (G3C05). When the D region fades at sunset, the same bands open for long-distance skywave and stay open until sunrise rebuilds the absorber. The higher bands tell the opposite story — 20 meters and up mostly punch through the D region even at noon, so their limiting factor is not absorption but whether the F region is dense enough to bend them back. That is the subject of the next two sections.

The layer questions live in pool group G3C: the order of the regions, why F2 skip is longest, and what eats the low bands by day.

### The Sun drives everything

If the ionosphere is the engine of HF, the Sun is the throttle, and sunspots are the visible gauge. More sunspots mean more solar radiation, which means more ionization — and more ionization means higher frequencies propagate, because a more densely ionized F region can bend shorter wavelengths back to Earth (G3A01). Solar activity rises and falls over the roughly 11-year **sunspot cycle**, and HF operators plan around it the way sailors plan around seasons.

When solar activity is low, the highest HF bands are the first casualties: 15, 12, and 10 meters become the least reliable bands for long-distance communication (G3A04), sometimes closing for months at a stretch. The band that never quits is 20 meters, which usually supports worldwide propagation during daylight hours at any point in the solar cycle (G3A07) — the dependable daytime default, cycle minimum or cycle maximum.

The pool's favorite trap in this subelement is timing — four disturbance mechanisms, four very different clocks, and distractors that shuffle them. Keep them apart. A solar flare's burst of ultraviolet and X-ray radiation travels at the speed of light and arrives in about 8 minutes (G3A03). It causes a **Sudden Ionospheric Disturbance** — extra ionization slammed into the sunlit D region — and an SID disrupts daytime propagation on the lower frequencies more than on the higher ones (G3A02): the low bands fade first, on the day side, within minutes of the flare. A **coronal mass ejection** is matter, not light: its charged particles take 15 hours to several days to reach Earth and affect propagation (G3A11). And because the Sun's surface rotates on its axis in 26 to 28 days, an active region — and the propagation conditions it causes — tends to come back around on that 26- to 28-day schedule (G3A10). Eight minutes for light, days for particles, four weeks for the rotation that brings the source back.

When those particles arrive, Earth answers with a **geomagnetic storm** — a temporary disturbance in Earth's geomagnetic field (G3A06). For HF the news is worst near the poles: a geomagnetic storm degrades high-latitude HF propagation (G3A08), so transpolar paths suffer first. The same storm has one famous consolation prize: high geomagnetic activity creates auroras that can reflect VHF signals (G3A09), letting 6 meter and 2 meter operators work each other off the auroral curtain — with a distinctive raspy distortion on anything reflected that way. Charged particles streaming from **coronal holes**, the cooler open-field regions on the Sun, disturb HF communication in the same particle-driven way (G3A14).

Three numbers distill all of this into the daily forecast. The **solar flux index** measures solar radio emission at a wavelength of 10.7 centimeters — 2800 MHz (G3A05). It tracks sunspot activity well, but it is a radio measurement, not a count of spots, and the pool plants "another name for the sunspot number" as a distractor — keep that straight. Higher flux means a stronger F region and a higher MUF. The **K-index** is the short-term measure of the stability of Earth's geomagnetic field, updated every three hours (G3A12); the **A-index** is the long-term, daily measure (G3A13). Short versus long is the exam trap — K is the three-hour snapshot, A is the day. For operating: high flux is good news, high K is bad news.

You will meet all three numbers in the daily solar-terrestrial bulletins and in the solar-data widgets of logging software and club websites. Read them as a set: the flux tells you how high the MUF is likely to run today, the K-index tells you whether the geomagnetic field is quiet enough to let that MUF hold, and the A-index smooths the recent K readings into the bigger picture. High flux with a low K is the combination good DX openings are made of; a climbing K warns you to expect degraded high-latitude paths and to aim your expectations — and your beam — away from the poles.

That is all of pool group G3A: the sunspot lever, the four timings, geomagnetic storms, and the three indices.

### MUF, LUF, and choosing a band

Between a dead band and an open one sits a window, and the window has two edges. The **MUF** is the Maximum Usable Frequency for communications between two specific points (G3B08) — your station and Lisbon, say. Read the expansion carefully: the pool plants "Minimum Usable Frequency" as the first distractor, and it is wrong. The **LUF** is the Lowest Usable Frequency for the same two points (G3B07). Both are properties of a path, not of a band — they move as the ionosphere changes over your head and over the far end.

Inside the window, propagation works: radio waves with frequencies below the MUF and above the LUF are refracted back to Earth (G3B05). Above the MUF the bending is too weak and the signal escapes into space. Below the LUF the signal is attenuated — absorbed, mostly in the D region — before it can reach the destination (G3B06). And when conditions squeeze the window shut entirely, the LUF rising above the MUF, no ordinary skywave path exists between those two points at all (G3B11).

Within the window, not all frequencies are equal. The least-attenuated frequency for long-distance skip propagation is just below the MUF (G3B03) — high enough to minimize D-region absorption on every pass, still low enough to be bent back. The distractors offer "just above the LUF" and the critical frequency; the keyed answer is just below the MUF. And what sets the MUF? Everything: the path, the time of day, the season, the level of solar radiation, and ionospheric disturbances — which is why the keyed answer to G3B02 is "all these choices are correct."

> **Worked example:** Today's MUF between your station and Europe is 21 MHz. The rule "operate just below the MUF" picks 17 meters (18.068–18.168 MHz) as the least-attenuated path: just under the ceiling, with minimal absorption per hop. The 15 meter band (21 MHz) sits right at the edge and will flutter in and out as the MUF wobbles, while 20 meters (14 MHz) works reliably but absorbs more on every hop. To sanity-check which band a frequency belongs to, use Book 2's wavelength shortcut $\lambda = 300 / f$ with wavelength in meters and frequency in MHz: 300 / 18.1 ≈ 16.6, so 18.1 MHz lives on the 17 meter band.

Two more terms complete the picture, and the exam loves to swap them because they share the word "critical." The **critical frequency** lives on the frequency axis: at a given incidence angle, it is the highest frequency that is refracted back to Earth (G3C02). The **critical angle** lives on the angle axis: it is the highest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions (G3C04). Launch steeper than the critical angle and the signal punches through the region and is lost to space; launch at or below it and the signal comes back. Frequency axis, angle axis — keep the two apart and the pair of questions is free.

Two practical consequences follow. First, takeoff angle trades against distance on a single hop: a low angle returns far away, a higher angle returns closer in — one reason DX antennas chase low radiation angles (chapter 9 returns to this). Second, the window breathes through the day: the MUF climbs after sunrise as the F region builds, peaks in the afternoon, and sags after sunset, while the LUF drops as the D region fades. The band that is "open" to a given place therefore changes hour by hour — higher by day, lower by night.

### Hops, skip zones, and scatter

One trip up to the ionosphere and back down is a **hop**, and hops have characteristic lengths set by the height of the refracting region. One hop via the F2 region covers approximately 2,500 miles (G3B09); one hop via the lower E region covers approximately 1,200 miles (G3B10). Transoceanic paths are multi-hop chains — signal up, down to the ground or ocean, back up again — and every hop pays a toll: ground-reflection loss at the bounce point and another pass through the absorbing D region at each end.

The pool's distance distractors form a ladder worth learning as a ladder: 1,200 miles is the E-region hop, planted as a distractor in the F2 question; 2,500 miles is the F2 hop; and 12,000 miles is roughly halfway around the planet — long-path territory, not any single hop. Sort the choices against the ladder and the two distance questions answer themselves.

> **Worked example:** An F2 hop covers approximately 2,500 miles, so a 5,000-mile path needs at least two hops. Each hop adds absorption and ground-reflection loss, which is why the just-below-the-MUF choice matters more on long paths than on short ones: two hops' worth of extra absorption on a too-low frequency can take a readable signal down into the noise.

Between the ground wave — the signal that clings to Earth's surface and fades quickly with distance — and the landing zone of the first hop lies a ring where the main signal cannot be heard at all: the **skip zone**. You have met it every time a station 100 miles away is inaudible on 20 meters while stations 2,000 miles away are loud. Your signal skipped over the nearby station entirely.

The distance from the transmitter to where the first hop comes back down is the **skip distance**, and it is not fixed: it grows as the operating frequency approaches the MUF from below — weaker bending, farther landing — and it shrinks as the takeoff angle rises. The skip zone is simply the skip distance drawn as a ring around the station.

Yet the skip zone is not perfectly silent, and the reason is **scatter**. A small part of the signal's energy is scattered by irregularities in the ionosphere and the terrain into the skip zone (G3C08) — only a fraction, which is why scatter signals are usually weak. And scatter is the propagation that lets anything be heard in the skip zone at all (G3C09). Because the scattered energy arrives over several different paths at once, scatter signals often sound distorted (G3C07) with a characteristic fluttering sound (G3C06) — a wavering, hollow quality you will learn to recognize on the air.

One more path oddity belongs here. Every great-circle path has two directions: the short path, the direct way, and the **long path**, the other way around the planet. When conditions support both, your own signal can return to you by both routes — and because the long way around takes noticeably longer, you may hear a slightly delayed echo on your signal (G3B01). Eerie the first time, diagnostic forever after. File long path separately from the gray line coming in the next section — they are different phenomena.

Not every band limitation is solar or geometric. The lower HF frequencies — 160, 80, and 40 meters — characteristically suffer high levels of atmospheric noise or static during the summer (G3B12), the crackle of distant thunderstorms adding up across the hemisphere. Summer static is seasonal weather physics, not a sunspot effect; quiet winter nights are when the low bands shine.

Hops, skip, scatter, long path, and static close out G3B and take care of G3C06 through G3C09.

### NVIS, the gray line, and checking conditions right now

Everything so far has launched signals low toward the horizon for distance. **NVIS** — near vertical incidence skywave — launches nearly straight up on purpose. On a frequency below the critical frequency, even a signal aimed almost vertically is refracted back down, and it lands close: NVIS is short-distance MF or HF propagation at high elevation angles (G3C10), blanketing everything within roughly 300 miles of the transmitter with no skip zone at all. That hole-free regional footprint — mountains, valleys, and the next county covered alike — is exactly what emergency communications needs, and NVIS is the emcomm workhorse for regional coverage: typically 40 meters by day and 80 meters at night, below the critical frequency either way. The antenna side of NVIS (a low horizontal dipole) waits for chapter 9; here the mode itself is the exam point.

{{fig:ch03-nvis}}

The contrast with ordinary skip is the whole point. On a normal low-angle path your signal can sail over the entire state you are trying to serve; on NVIS there is no skip zone to fall into, so the shelter fifty miles away hears you as well as the emergency operations center two hundred miles out — even with hills in the way, because the signal comes down from above instead of threading terrain.

The **gray line** is Earth's day-night terminator — the twilight band that circles the globe at sunrise and sunset. Along the terminator the absorbing D region has gone quiet, as at night, while the F region still refracts, as by day: a short window in which low-band paths that follow the terminator are briefly enhanced. The best gray-line paths put both ends of the contact on the terminator at once, and the geometry moves on within minutes, so the window is brief and prized. Low-band DXers set alarms for it. The gray line carries no exam weight in this subelement — learn it as an operating tool, and keep it mentally separate from long path, which is a direction, not a time of day.

{{fig:ch03-grayline}}

Everything so far has been prediction; the pool also asks how to check propagation right now, from your own station. The modern answer is a network of automated receiving stations on the internet — put out a brief call, then look at where your signal was actually heard (G3B04). The reverse beacon network does exactly that for CW and digital signals: automated receivers around the world report where you were copied, and how strong, within seconds. The oldest check is still running: the NCDXF/IARU beacon network on 14.100, 18.110, 21.150, 24.930, and 28.200 MHz — the five beacon frequencies from chapter 1. Each beacon takes a turn transmitting at known power from a known location, so a quick tune across the five tells you which bands are open and to where. WSPR, the weak-signal reporting mode, maps who heard whom across the globe — treat it as color for now; its exam content lives in chapter 8.

NVIS is G3C10, and the live-conditions check is G3B04. With those two, subelement G3 is fully covered.

Put a full day together and the chapter turns into habit. At the morning gray line you listen low, on 40 and 80 meters, along the terminator. Midday belongs to the high bands — 20 meters always, 17 and 15 when the flux cooperates. Through the afternoon the MUF slides down, the D region fades at sunset, and 40 meters stretches from regional to continental. At night 80 and 160 meters open for real skip, and NVIS on 80 blankets your own region whenever the emergency net needs it. The bands never changed all day; the sky overhead did — and now you can watch it work, the indices in one browser tab and a slow tune across the beacon frequencies in the other.

**FACT:** More sunspots mean more ionization, so higher frequencies propagate — and when solar activity is low, the highest HF bands (15, 12, and 10 meters) become the least reliable, while 20 meters supports worldwide daylight propagation at any point in the solar cycle.

**FACT:** A solar flare's UV and X-ray burst arrives in about 8 minutes (light-travel time) and causes a Sudden Ionospheric Disturbance that hits daytime lower HF frequencies hardest, while a coronal mass ejection's particles take 15 hours to several days to arrive.

**FACT:** The best (least-attenuated) frequency for long-distance communication is just below the MUF, and the MUF depends on the path, time of day, season, solar radiation, and ionospheric disturbances.

**FACT:** One-hop distances are approximately 2,500 miles for the F2 region and approximately 1,200 miles for the E region.

**FACT:** The daytime D region absorbs the low bands — it is the most absorbent region below 10 MHz in daylight, which is why 40, 60, 80, and 160 meters are day-useless for long distance and open at night when the D region fades.

### Exam Focus

Subelement G3 — Radio Wave Propagation — is 37 pool questions in three groups (G3A, G3B, G3C), and your exam draws three of them, one per group. The ten below are quoted verbatim from the pool, answer key included, with a one-line why for each.

> **G3A03** Approximately how long does it take the increased ultraviolet and X-ray radiation from a solar flare to affect radio propagation on Earth?
> A. 28 days
> B. 1 to 2 hours
> C. 8 minutes
> D. 20 to 40 hours
> **Answer: C** — the flare's radiation travels at the speed of light, so it arrives in about 8 minutes — the particle disturbances take days.

> **G3A05** What is the solar flux index?
> A. A measure of the highest frequency that is useful for ionospheric propagation between two points on Earth
> B. A count of sunspots that is adjusted for solar emissions
> C. Another name for the American sunspot number
> D. A measure of solar radiation with a wavelength of 10.7 centimeters
> **Answer: D** — the solar flux index is a radio measurement at 10.7 cm (2800 MHz), not a sunspot count.

> **G3A10** What causes HF propagation conditions to vary periodically in a 26- to 28-day cycle?
> A. Long term oscillations in the upper atmosphere
> B. Cyclic variation in Earth’s radiation belts
> C. Rotation of the Sun’s surface layers around its axis
> D. The position of the Moon in its orbit
> **Answer: C** — the Sun's surface rotates in 26 to 28 days, so active regions — and the conditions they cause — come back around on that schedule.

> **G3B03** Which frequency will have the least attenuation for long-distance skip propagation?
> A. Just below the MUF
> B. Just above the LUF
> C. Just below the critical frequency
> D. Just above the critical frequency
> **Answer: A** — the least-attenuated frequency for long-distance skip is just below the MUF.

> **G3B08** What does MUF stand for?
> A. The Minimum Usable Frequency for communications between two points
> B. The Maximum Usable Frequency for communications between two points
> C. The Minimum Usable Frequency during a 24-hour period
> D. The Maximum Usable Frequency during a 24-hour period
> **Answer: B** — MUF is the Maximum Usable Frequency between two points — “Minimum” is the planted misreading.

> **G3B09** What is the approximate maximum distance along the Earth’s surface normally covered in one hop using the F2 region?
> A. 180 miles
> B. 1,200 miles
> C. 2,500 miles
> D. 12,000 miles
> **Answer: C** — one F2 hop covers approximately 2,500 miles; the E-region hop is about 1,200.

> **G3C02** What is meant by the term “critical frequency” at a given incidence angle?
> A. The highest frequency which is refracted back to Earth
> B. The lowest frequency which is refracted back to Earth
> C. The frequency at which the signal-to-noise ratio approaches unity
> D. The frequency at which the signal-to-noise ratio is 6 dB
> **Answer: A** — at a given incidence angle, the critical frequency is the highest frequency still refracted back to Earth.

> **G3C04** What does the term “critical angle” mean, as applied to radio wave propagation?
> A. The long path azimuth of a distant station
> B. The short path azimuth of a distant station
> C. The lowest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions
> D. The highest takeoff angle that will return a radio wave to Earth under specific ionospheric conditions
> **Answer: D** — the critical angle is the highest takeoff angle that still returns to Earth — steeper punches through.

> **G3C05** Why is long-distance communication on the 40-, 60-, 80-, and 160-meter bands more difficult during the day?
> A. The F region absorbs signals at these frequencies during daylight hours
> B. The F region is unstable during daylight hours
> C. The D region absorbs signals at these frequencies during daylight hours
> D. The E region is unstable during daylight hours
> **Answer: C** — the daytime D region absorbs the low bands, so 40 through 160 meters stay local until night.

> **G3C10** What is near vertical incidence skywave (NVIS) propagation?
> A. Propagation near the MUF
> B. Short distance MF or HF propagation at high elevation angles
> C. Long path HF propagation at sunrise and sunset
> D. Double hop propagation near the LUF
> **Answer: B** — NVIS is high-angle, short-distance MF or HF propagation — a blanket of regional coverage with no skip zone.

### Key Takeaways

- The ionospheric regions stack D, E, F1, F2 from lowest to highest; F2 is highest, so F2 skip is the longest.

- More sunspots mean higher bands open; when solar activity is low, 15, 12, and 10 meters fail first, while 20 meters works worldwide in daylight at any point in the cycle.

- Three clocks, kept apart: a flare's light arrives in about 8 minutes, CME particles in 15 hours to several days, and conditions recur every 26 to 28 days with the Sun's rotation.

- The least-attenuated long-distance frequency is just below the MUF; below the LUF absorption wins, and LUF above MUF means no ordinary skywave path.

- One hop covers approximately 2,500 miles via F2 and approximately 1,200 miles via E; scatter — weak, fluttery, multipath — is all you hear inside the skip zone.

- The daytime D region eats the low bands: 40 through 160 meters stay local in daylight and open at night.

- NVIS launches nearly straight up for hole-free regional coverage out to roughly 300 miles — the emergency-communications mode.
