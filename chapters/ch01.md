## 1. Your HF Privileges & the Rules That Come with Them

The day your General upgrade takes effect, your station stops being a local machine. The HF rig that could only whisper CW in a few narrow slivers when you were a Technician can now run phone on every HF band from 80 through 10 meters — but every new segment arrives with a rule attached: which frequencies, which modes, how much power, who may sit at the controls, and what you may and may not transmit. In this chapter you'll learn your exact band-by-band privileges; the power limits, including the one HF band capped at 200 W and the one band with two power limits at once; the antenna-structure and beacon rules; how examiners, credit, and the famous /AG suffix actually work; and the third-party and automatic-control rules — all of pool subelement G1, 52 questions, worth 5 of the 35 questions on your exam.

### Your new HF map

Everything you could do as a Technician stays exactly as it was: all amateur bands at 50 MHz and above, unchanged. The upgrade adds what lies below 30 MHz — the MF and HF bands where the ionosphere can carry your signal across an ocean. Your new General-class map, band by band: the 2200-meter (135.7–137.8 kHz) and 630-meter (472–479 kHz) bands; all of 160 meters (1800–2000 kHz); the entire 60, 30, 17, 12, and 10 meter bands; and shared segments of the four classic phone bands — 80, 40, 20, and 15 meters.

Those four are the bands where parts remain off-limits, and the pool makes you recite them: 80 meters, 40 meters, 20 meters, and 15 meters (G1A01). The Extra-only slices — some shared with Advanced — are the lower edges: 3.500–3.525 MHz, 3.600–3.800 MHz, 7.000–7.025 MHz, 7.125–7.175 MHz, 14.000–14.025 MHz, 14.150–14.225 MHz, 21.000–21.025 MHz, and 21.200–21.275 MHz. The Extra-exclusive spectrum sits on those four bands only (G1A08); everything else below 30 MHz is yours end to end.

On 80 meters your pieces are 3.525–3.600 MHz and 3.800–4.000 MHz — the second piece is the 75 meter phone segment, which is why operators say "75 meters" for the phone end of that band. On 40 meters: 7.025–7.125 MHz and 7.175–7.300 MHz. On 20 meters: 14.025–14.150 MHz and 14.225–14.350 MHz. On 15 meters: 21.025–21.200 MHz and 21.275–21.450 MHz. In each pair the first segment is CW and data territory, and the second is where phone joins in.

There is a pattern worth memorizing. Wherever General does not get a band's entire phone segment, the General share is the upper frequency portion (G1A11): 14.225–14.350 MHz on 20 meters, 7.175–7.300 MHz on 40 meters, 3.800–4.000 MHz on 75 meters, and 21.275–21.450 MHz on 15 meters. Generals get the top. The pool checks the map with worked exclusions, and the trap is always the same shape: you are prohibited from 7.125–7.175 MHz on 40 meters (G1A05) — but not from 28.000–28.025 MHz, because CW is permitted on the entire 10 meter band, and not from 21.275–21.300 MHz, which sits inside your 15 meter phone segment, so 21.300 MHz is yours (G1A09). The wrong answers are segments you may use; the right answer is the one slice of Extra-only spectrum.

> **Worked example:** Your General 40 meter phone segment starts at 7.175 MHz, and an LSB signal's energy spreads about 3 kHz below the displayed (suppressed-carrier) frequency. Set the dial to 7.178 MHz and your signal occupies 7.175–7.178 MHz — its lower edge resting exactly on the boundary, legal by a whisker; tune slightly lower and part of your signal lands in spectrum you do not hold. That is the pool's own G4D08 example, and chapter 4 drills the full sideband-occupancy arithmetic. For now, file away one idea: the dial frequency is not where your whole signal lives.

**FACT:** The bands with portions where General class licensees cannot transmit are 80 meters, 40 meters, 20 meters, and 15 meters — on 160, 60, 30, 17, 12, and 10 meters General has the entire band.

One vocabulary note before we move on. An **allocation** is the assignment of a band to a radio service, and within an allocation a service can be **primary** or **secondary** — that distinction drives a real operating duty, as the next section shows. Three of the bands you get in full — 30, 17, and 12 meters — are the **WARC bands**, named for the 1979 world radio conference that created them. New words: allocation, primary service, secondary service, harmful interference, WARC bands. This section and the next together cover pool group G1A, 10 questions.

### Mode rules and sharing

Some bands restrict what you may transmit, not just where. The strictest is 30 meters: on 10.100–10.150 MHz only RTTY and data emissions are authorized — no phone and no image anywhere in the band (G1A02, G1A03). CW is welcome there too, because of a wonderfully simple rule: an amateur station may transmit a CW emission on any frequency authorized to the control operator. That one sentence of §97.305(a) is also why the entire 10 meter band, 28.0–29.7 MHz, is CW-legal for you (G1A07), including the parts where phone or data emissions are not allowed.

The rest of 10 meters is sorted by emission type. RTTY and data occupy 28.0–28.3 MHz, phone and image occupy 28.3–29.7 MHz, and repeater operation is confined to the portion of the band above 29.5 MHz (G1A10). A 10 meter repeater lives at the very top of the band — remember that address when repeaters return in the last section of this chapter.

Sharing has rules of its own. On some bands the FCC designates the amateur service as secondary, and the rule states exactly what that means: your stations "must not cause harmful interference to, and must accept interference from, stations in a primary service" (G1A06). **Harmful interference** is interference that seriously degrades, obstructs, or repeatedly interrupts a radio service. As the secondary user you yield every time — you may not interfere, and you may not complain about what you receive.

One geography fact rides along with allocations. The International Telecommunication Union divides the world into three regions, and the United States, Puerto Rico, and the US Virgin Islands are in ITU Region 2 — roughly the Americas. That is a group-G1E question (G1E06) filed here because it belongs with allocations, and it is why every band edge in this chapter is a Region 2 value.

**FACT:** On 30 meters (10.100–10.150 MHz) only RTTY and data emissions are authorized — no phone and no image anywhere in the band.

New words: emission mode, CW emission, phone and image emissions, RTTY and data segments, ITU Region 2. That finishes pool group G1A — 10 questions you can now answer.

### 60 meters as it exists now

60 meters gets its own section because the rules changed after the question pool was written, and this book teaches the band as it exists today. Under the FCC's WRC-15 order (91 FR 1430, effective January 2026), the band has a two-part structure. Part one is a contiguous segment, 5351.5–5366.5 kHz, which you may use anywhere inside at up to 9.15 W ERP. Part two is four discrete channels — 5332, 5348, 5373, and 5405 kHz — each good for up to 100 W ERP. The old 5358.5 kHz channel is gone as a discrete assignment; it lies inside the new segment. Phone on 60 meters is upper sideband, and no emission anywhere on the band may exceed 2.8 kHz of bandwidth.

{{fig:ch01-60m-structure}}

Two pool questions predate the change, and both keyed answers are still exactly right. G1C03 asks the maximum bandwidth permitted on the band's USB frequencies: 2.8 kHz. The rule text moved — the cap now lives at §97.303(h)(3), while the pool's reference line prints the superseded [97.303(h)(1)] — but the answer did not. G1C04 asks what you must do if you use an antenna other than a dipole: keep a record of its gain in your station records. That duty now lives at §97.313(i) (the pool prints [97.303(i)]), again unchanged in substance. Learn the keyed answers as published; the prose here simply points at the sections where those rules now live. The pool's February 2026 errata already withdrew the two questions the amendment had conflicted, so nothing else about 60 meters on the exam is out of date.

ERP — **effective radiated power** — is the band's one genuinely new arithmetic idea. It is your transmitter's PEP multiplied by your antenna's gain relative to a half-wave dipole, and the rule presumes a dipole has a gain of 1, written 0 dBd. Its cousin **EIRP** measures against a theoretical isotropic antenna instead; 60 meters uses ERP, which is why the dipole is the reference. One more practical detail: on the four discrete channels the channel number is the center frequency — your phone, RTTY, or data suppressed-carrier frequency may be set 1.5 kHz below center, while a CW carrier sits at center.

> **Worked example:** Your transmitter puts out 50 W PEP on 60 meters and your antenna has 3 dBd of gain — a factor of 2 over a dipole. ERP = 50 × 2 = 100 W: legal on the four discrete channels, where the limit is 100 W ERP, but not inside the 5351.5–5366.5 kHz segment, where the limit is 9.15 W ERP. Swap to a plain dipole (0 dBd, gain 1) and 9.15 W PEP gets you into the segment. And if you run anything but a dipole on this band, §97.313(i) requires you to keep the manufacturer's gain data or your own gain calculations in your station records — that is exactly G1C04.

**FACT:** On 60 meters (current text), no station may transmit on the four discrete channels 5332, 5348, 5373, and 5405 kHz with a radiated power exceeding 100 W ERP, and no station may transmit in the contiguous 5351.5–5366.5 kHz segment with a radiated power exceeding 9.15 W ERP — see §7.1.

New words: ERP (effective radiated power), EIRP, emission bandwidth, suppressed-carrier frequency. This section covers the two 60 meter questions of pool group G1C; the rest of G1C is next.

### Power limits: the ceiling, the exception, and PEP

The default ceiling for the amateur service is 1.5 kW PEP output, and the pool asks it three ways: the maximum on 12 meters (G1C02), the maximum on 28 MHz for a General control operator (G1C05), and the maximum on 1.8 MHz (G1C06) are all 1500 watts PEP. The one big HF exception is 30 meters: on the 10.10–10.15 MHz segment the limit is 200 W PEP output for everyone (G1C01, asked as 10.140 MHz). You may also meet four other 200 W PEP segments — 3.525–3.60 MHz, 7.025–7.125 MHz, 21.025–21.20 MHz, and 28.0–28.5 MHz — but read the fine print: those caps apply only when the control operator is a Novice or Technician. With your General ticket you may run up to 1.5 kW PEP on them.

**FACT:** "No station may transmit with a transmitter power exceeding 1.5 kW PEP" — 1500 watts PEP output is the answer for a General on 12 meters, on 28 MHz, and on 1.8 MHz.

The legal yardstick is **PEP — peak envelope power** — "the average power supplied to the antenna transmission line by a transmitter during one RF cycle at the crest of the modulation envelope taken under normal operating conditions" (G1C11). The regulated quantity is PEP output from the transmitter — not RMS power, not power delivered to the antenna, and 2000 W is an invented distractor that appears nowhere in Part 97. The full PEP mathematics belongs to chapter 5; here you only need the definition and the companion rule that never stops applying: an amateur station must use the minimum transmitter power necessary to carry out the desired communications (§97.313(a)). The ceiling is permission, not a target.

Two specialized limits round out the group. Spread spectrum work is capped at 10 W PEP output (G1E08 — a group-G1E question riding along here because it is a power limit). And before you put a brand-new digital protocol on the air, its technical characteristics must have been documented publicly — the rule names CLOVER, G-TOR, and PacTOR as examples of publicly documented techniques (G1C07). The mirror-image rule: emissions using an unspecified digital code must never be transmitted for the purpose of obscuring the meaning of a communication. Encoding to hide meaning is prohibited; encoding to communicate efficiently is the whole point of the modes.

New words: PEP (peak envelope power), transmitter power output, spread spectrum, specified and unspecified digital codes. That finishes pool group G1C — 8 questions.

### Antennas, beacons, and what you may not transmit

Upgraded privileges usually mean a bigger antenna, and two rules govern the structure itself. The height threshold is 200 feet — 60.96 meters — above ground level: owners of antenna structures taller than that, and owners of structures near or at a public-use airport regardless of height, must notify the FAA and register with the FCC (G1B01). Below 200 feet and away from airports, no notification or registration is required. The second rule is the PRB-1 doctrine, written into §97.15(b): state and local regulation of a station antenna structure must not preclude amateur service communications — it must reasonably accommodate them, and it must be the minimum practicable regulation for the locality's legitimate purpose (G1B06). Your town may regulate your tower; it may not zone you off the air.

Beacons are the service's propagation instruments: a **beacon** is an amateur station transmitting communications for the observation of propagation and reception or other related experimental activities (G1B03), and it may transmit one-way communications. The limits are tight. A beacon must not transmit on more than one channel in the same band from the same location (G1B02) — note that the "National Beacon Organization" appearing in that question's distractors is fictional, and so is any internet-posting requirement. Beacon transmitter power must not exceed 100 W (G1B10), a far cry from your 1.5 kW ceiling. And on HF, automatically controlled beacons are permitted only in 28.20–28.30 MHz (G1B09). Beware that question's 21.08–21.09 MHz distractor: it is a real automatically controlled segment, but for digital stations, not beacons.

Hold that 28.20–28.30 MHz segment apart from a neighboring fact the pool tests separately. The five frequencies 14.100, 18.110, 21.150, 24.930, and 28.200 MHz carry the NCDXF/IARU international beacon network, and you should normally avoid transmitting on them (G1E10) — but that is good amateur practice under §97.101(a), not a Part 97 set-aside. The only automatic-beacon segment the rules themselves designate is 28.20–28.30 MHz. One is a rule; the other is courtesy. The exam wants you to know both, in their separate boxes.

Now the broad question of what may leave your antenna. One-way transmissions are generally prohibited — but §97.111(b) lists the exceptions: brief transmissions to adjust the station, brief transmissions to establish two-way contact, telecommand, emergency communications, information bulletins, telemetry, and "transmissions necessary to assisting persons learning, or improving proficiency in, the international Morse code" (G1B05). Two more one-way-flavored permissions: you may occasionally retransmit US-government weather and propagation forecast information intended for the general public — occasionally, not on a regular basis (G1B04) — and you may talk with amateurs in any country except those whose administrations have notified the ITU that they object to such communications (G1B08).

The prohibitions matter as much. An amateur station shall not engage in any form of broadcasting — transmissions intended for reception by the general public, either direct or relayed. Messages encoded for the purpose of obscuring their meaning are prohibited, but Q-signals and ordinary abbreviations are perfectly fine precisely because they do not obscure meaning (G1B07). And for everything the rules do not spell out, the catch-all: in all respects not specifically covered by FCC rules, each amateur station must be operated in accordance with good engineering and good amateur practice — with the FCC as the arbiter of that standard (G1B11).

Finally, three standing interference-avoidance duties travel together in one group-G1E question (G1E04). A station within 1600 m (1 mile) of an FCC monitoring facility must protect that facility from harmful interference. On secondary allocations you carry the don't-interfere, must-accept duty from earlier in this chapter. And spread-spectrum stations must not cause harmful interference to stations using other authorized emissions — and must accept all interference from them.

New words: beacon, PRB-1, reasonable accommodation, one-way transmission, broadcasting, good amateur practice. That is pool group G1B, 11 questions, with G1E04 and G1E10 taught alongside.

### Examiners, credit, and the upgrade mechanics

Book 2 walked you through exam day; here is the machinery behind it as the General pool tests it. Every exam is administered by a team of at least three Volunteer Examiners at a session coordinated by a Volunteer Examiner Coordinator (G1D04). Each VE must be accredited by the coordinating VEC — not by the FCC (G1D07) — must be at least 18 years old (G1D10), and must never have had an amateur license revoked or suspended. There is no US-citizenship requirement: a non-citizen holding an FCC General-class-or-higher license can serve as a VE (G1D08). And class matters: a General-class VE may administer Technician exams only — administering a General exam takes an Amateur Extra or Advanced VE (G1D02).

The pool also tests what happens to old licenses. A quick refresher from Book 2: an expired license has a 2-year renewal grace period during which you may not transmit but may renew. Beyond the grace period the grant is gone — but not forgotten. An expired General, Advanced, or pre-March-21-1987 Technician grant still earns examination credit under §97.505(a): to get back on the air at General class, you show proof of the old grant and pass the current Element 2 (G1D01, G1D11). Your old Element 3 credit survives even when the license does not.

Then comes the trio this book drills hardest, because it is the moment you have been working toward. A Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination — the CSCE your VE team hands you at the session — is valid for 365 days for the element credit it conveys (G1D09). And here is the immediacy rule, in one breath with both of its conditions and its identification requirement: once your Form 605 requesting the upgrade has been properly submitted to the administering VEs and you hold the CSCE showing you passed, §97.9(b) authorizes you to exercise General privileges immediately — on any General or Technician band segment (G1D03) — before the VEC files anything and before the FCC database changes, and whenever you operate using those new General privileges you must identify with the indicator AG appended to your call sign until the upgrade shows in the FCC database (G1D06). On phone the customary form is "temporary AG" after your call; on CW and digital you send your call followed by /AG. When your ULS record shows General — typically within days — you drop the suffix, and you keep your call sign throughout.

{{fig:ch01-ag-indicator}}

**FACT:** While operating under §97.9(b) upgrade authority the station must append an indicator to the call sign — "for a control operator who has requested a license modification from Novice or Technician to General Class: AG" — separated from the call sign by the slant mark (/) or any suitable word denoting it.

The group closes with a pair of remote-control questions whose asymmetry surprises everyone. Run your own US station by remote control from a hotel in another country, and nothing about your license obligation changes: the control operator of a US amateur station must hold an FCC operator/primary grant (or alien reciprocal authority), wherever they happen to sit (G1D05). But sit in the United States and remotely operate a station located in South America, and only that country's regulations apply (G1D12) — Part 97's band tables reach only places "outside any area where the amateur service is regulated by any authority other than the FCC," so a station on foreign soil answers to its own administration, not to yours. Direction one, your US license travels with you; direction two, the station's territory rules.

New words: VE, VEC, CSCE, element credit, grace period, control operator, control point, and the three control types — local, remote, and automatic — defined properly in the next section. That is pool group G1D, 12 questions.

### Third-party traffic, automatic digital, and special stations

**Third-party communications** are messages passed on behalf of someone who is not a control operator: you are the first party, the control operator at the other end is the second party, and the person whose message moves is the third party. The pool tests four rules. First, a third party may participate in stating the message only while the control operator is present at the control point, continuously supervising — and a person whose own amateur license was revoked (and not reinstated), whose license is under suspension, who surrendered for cancellation after an enforcement notice, or who is under an amateur-service cease-and-desist order is disqualified from third-party participation (G1E01). Second, within US jurisdiction third-party traffic is always permitted; to a foreign country it is permitted only for emergency or disaster-relief communications or where that country has made arrangements with the United States — the third-party agreements — and even then the content is limited to communications incidental to the purposes of the amateur service and remarks of a personal character (G1E05). Third, nothing about remote control changes any of this: third-party messages via a remotely controlled station are permitted whenever third-party traffic itself is permitted (G1E12). Fourth, no station may transmit third-party communications while automatically controlled, except a station transmitting a RTTY or data emission.

Those control types deserve their definitions, because the rest of the group leans on them. Under **local control** the control operator directly manipulates the station's operating adjustments. Under **remote control** the operator manipulates them indirectly, through a control link. **Automatic control** uses devices and procedures so that compliance with the rules is achieved without the control operator being present at a control point — and automatic control is a privilege granted station type by station type, never a default.

Automatically controlled digital stations are the pool's favorite case. An automatically controlled station may transmit RTTY or data anywhere on the 6 meter or shorter wavelength bands, and in limited segments of some HF bands — for example 10.140–10.150 MHz on 30 meters and 28.120–28.189 MHz on 10 meters (G1E11). Outside those segments, an automatically controlled RTTY/data station may operate only while responding to interrogation by a station under local or remote control, with no transmission exceeding 500 Hz of bandwidth (G1E03). Which means that when you call an unattended Winlink-style station outside the auto-control segments, the rule reaches back to you: the initiating station must be under local or remote control.

The special stations round out the subelement. An **auxiliary station** — the kind that provides a control link — may transmit only on the 2 meter and shorter wavelength bands, minus a list of excluded segments such as 144.0–144.5 MHz; it may be automatically controlled and may transmit one-way communications. A **repeater** may be automatically controlled, and its control operator is not accountable when the repeater inadvertently retransmits communications that violate the rules. Repeaters obey the same control-operator privilege chain as every other station, which produces the group's cleverest question: a Technician may talk through a 10 meter repeater only if the repeater's own control operator holds General class or higher (G1E02) — the repeater is the transmitting station on 10 meters, above 29.5 MHz where Technicians hold no privileges of their own, so its control operator's license is the one that counts. And one flat prohibition to close: amateur stations exchange messages only with other amateur stations (plus narrow exceptions like emergencies and RACES), so you may not communicate with non-licensed Wi-Fi stations on 2.4 GHz or anywhere else (G1E07) — sharing spectrum is not sharing a contact.

New words: third-party communications, third-party agreement, auxiliary station, repeater, automatic control, spread spectrum. That finishes pool group G1E — 11 questions — and with it all of subelement G1: 52 questions, 5 of them on your exam.

### Exam Focus

This chapter covers pool subelement G1 (Commission's Rules) — groups G1A through G1E, 52 of the pool's 423 questions, worth 5 of the 35 questions on your exam: one from each group. Here are ten representative questions, quoted exactly as the pool prints them.

> **G1A01** On which HF and/or MF amateur bands are there portions where General class licensees cannot transmit?
> A. 60 meters, 30 meters, 17 meters, and 12 meters
> B. 160 meters, 60 meters, 15 meters, and 12 meters
> C. 80 meters, 40 meters, 20 meters, and 15 meters
> D. 80 meters, 20 meters, 15 meters, and 10 meters
> **Answer: C** — the Extra-only slices sit on 80, 40, 20, and 15 meters; you have the other six HF bands end to end.

> **G1A05** On which of the following frequencies are General class licensees prohibited from operating as control operator?
> A. 7.125 MHz to 7.175 MHz
> B. 28.000 MHz to 28.025 MHz
> C. 21.275 MHz to 21.300 MHz
> D. All these choices are correct
> **Answer: A** — 7.125–7.175 MHz is Extra/Advanced territory on 40 meters; CW is legal on all of 10 meters, and 21.275–21.300 MHz is inside your 15 meter phone segment.

> **G1B01** What is the maximum height above ground for an antenna structure not near a public use airport without requiring notification to the FAA and registration with the FCC?
> A. 50 feet
> B. 100 feet
> C. 200 feet
> D. 250 feet
> **Answer: C** — 200 feet (60.96 meters) is the notification-and-registration threshold away from airports.

> **G1B09** On what HF frequencies are automatically controlled beacons permitted?
> A. On any frequency if power is less than 1 watt
> B. On any frequency if transmissions are in Morse code
> C. 21.08 MHz to 21.09 MHz
> D. 28.20 MHz to 28.30 MHz
> **Answer: D** — on HF the automatic-beacon segment is 28.20–28.30 MHz; the 21.08–21.09 MHz distractor is for automatically controlled digital stations.

> **G1C01** What is the maximum transmitter power an amateur station may use on 10.140 MHz?
> A. 200 watts PEP output
> B. 1000 watts PEP output
> C. 1500 watts PEP output
> D. 2000 watts PEP output
> **Answer: A** — 10.140 MHz is on 30 meters, the one HF band capped at 200 W PEP for everyone.

> **G1C03** What is the maximum bandwidth permitted by FCC rules for amateur radio stations transmitting on USB frequencies in the 60-meter band?
> A. 2.8 kHz
> B. 5.6 kHz
> C. 1.8 kHz
> D. 3 kHz
> **Answer: A** — 2.8 kHz maximum anywhere on 60 meters, per current §97.303(h)(3).

> **G1D03** On which of the following band segments may you operate if you are a Technician class operator and have an unexpired Certificate of Successful Completion of Examination (CSCE) for General class privileges?
> A. Only the Technician band segments until your upgrade is posted in the FCC database
> B. Only on the Technician band segments until you have a receipt for the FCC application fee payment
> C. On any General or Technician class band segment
> D. On any General or Technician class band segment except 30 meters and 60 meters
> **Answer: C** — Form 605 properly submitted and CSCE in hand, §97.9(b) opens every General and Technician segment immediately.

> **G1D06** Until an upgrade to General class is shown in the FCC database, when must a Technician licensee identify with “AG” after their call sign?
> A. Whenever they operate using General class frequency privileges
> B. Whenever they operate on any amateur frequency
> C. Whenever they operate using Technician frequency privileges
> D. A special identifier is not required if their General class license application has been filed with the FCC
> **Answer: A** — the /AG indicator rides only your General-privilege operation, and only until the database shows the upgrade.

> **G1E02** When may a 10-meter repeater retransmit the 2-meter signal from a station that has a Technician class control operator?
> A. Under no circumstances
> B. Only if the station on 10-meters is operating under a Special Temporary Authorization allowing such retransmission
> C. Only during an FCC-declared general state of communications emergency
> D. Only if the 10-meter repeater control operator holds at least a General class license
> **Answer: D** — the repeater is the transmitting station on 10 meters, so its control operator must hold General or higher.

> **G1E10** Why should an amateur operator normally avoid transmitting on 14.100, 18.110, 21.150, 24.930 and 28.200 MHz?
> A. A system of propagation beacon stations operates on those frequencies
> B. A system of automatic digital stations operates on those frequencies
> C. These frequencies are set aside for emergency operations
> D. These frequencies are set aside for bulletins from the FCC
> **Answer: A** — the NCDXF/IARU international beacon network operates there; avoiding it is good amateur practice, not a Part 97 set-aside.

### Key Takeaways

- The four bands with portions closed to Generals are 80, 40, 20, and 15 meters; you have all of 160, 60, 30, 17, 12, and 10 meters, and where your phone segment is partial it is the upper portion.

- Power: 1.5 kW PEP output nearly everywhere, 200 W PEP on 30 meters, 10 W PEP for spread spectrum — and always the minimum power necessary.

- 60 meters today is two parts: the contiguous 5351.5–5366.5 kHz segment at 9.15 W ERP plus four discrete channels at 100 W ERP, USB phone, 2.8 kHz maximum bandwidth — and non-dipole users keep antenna-gain records.

- Antenna structures over 200 feet (or near a public-use airport) need FAA notification and FCC registration; PRB-1 says local rules must reasonably accommodate amateur communications.

- Beacons: one channel per band per location, 100 W maximum, automatically controlled on HF only in 28.20–28.30 MHz; the NCDXF/IARU network frequencies are courtesy, not rule.

- The upgrade trio: CSCE valid 365 days; Form 605 properly submitted plus CSCE in hand means immediate General privileges; sign /AG whenever you use them until ULS shows the upgrade.

- VE mechanics: at least three VEs per session, accredited by the VEC, 18 or older, never revoked or suspended; a General-class VE administers Technician exams only.

- Third-party traffic needs a supervising control operator and (internationally) an agreement country; automatically controlled RTTY/data stations live on 6 meters and shorter plus listed HF segments.
