### Beat 1 — The ionosphere, properly (G3C's layer questions)
- The regions bottom-up: D (lowest), E, F1, F2 (G3C01); F2 is highest, so F2 skip reaches farthest (G3C03). Ionization comes from solar radiation; the regions strengthen by day and thin at night.
- Refraction, not a mirror bounce: signals are bent back to Earth when the frequency and angle are right — between the LUF and the MUF (preview of Beat 3).
- Day vs night: the D region is the **daytime absorber** — most absorbent below 10 MHz in daylight (G3C11), which is why 40/60/80/160 m are day-useless for long distance and open at night when the D region fades (G3C05).
- Figure: `{{fig:ch03-ionosphere-layers}}`.
- Vocabulary: ionosphere, D/E/F1/F2 region, refraction, absorption, skywave.

### Beat 2 — The Sun drives everything (G3A)
- Sunspots → ionization → higher frequencies propagate (G3A01). At low solar activity the highest HF bands (15, 12, 10 m) become least reliable (G3A04); 20 m supports worldwide daylight propagation at any point in the cycle (G3A07).
- The four timings, kept apart (the pool's favorite trap): a flare's UV/X-ray burst arrives in **about 8 minutes** (light-travel time) and causes a Sudden Ionospheric Disturbance that hits daytime lower HF hardest (G3A03, G3A02); a coronal mass ejection's particles take **15 hours to several days** (G3A11); conditions recur every **26–28 days** with the Sun's surface rotation (G3A10).
- Geomagnetic storms: temporary disturbance of Earth's magnetic field (G3A06); they degrade high-latitude (polar) HF paths (G3A08) while the accompanying aurora can reflect VHF signals with a raspy distortion (G3A09). Charged particles from coronal holes disturb HF propagation (G3A14).
- The three indices: solar flux index = 10.7 cm (2800 MHz) solar radio emission (G3A05 — not a sunspot count); K-index = short-term (3-hour) geomagnetic stability (G3A12); A-index = long-term (daily) (G3A13).
- Vocabulary: sunspot cycle, solar flux index, SID, CME, coronal hole, geomagnetic storm, auroral backscatter, K-index, A-index.

### Beat 3 — MUF, LUF, and choosing a band (G3B)
- MUF = Maximum Usable Frequency between two specific points (G3B08 — "Minimum Usable Frequency" is a planted expansion); LUF = Lowest Usable Frequency (G3B07). Between them the ionosphere refracts signals back to Earth (G3B05); below the LUF absorption kills the signal (G3B06); if the LUF rises above the MUF, no ordinary skywave path exists (G3B11).
- The best (least-attenuated) long-distance frequency is **just below the MUF** (G3B03); the MUF depends on path, time of day, season, solar radiation, and ionospheric disturbances — everything (G3B02).
- Critical frequency and critical angle (G3C02, G3C04 — the frequency-vs-angle axes, easy to swap): the critical frequency is the highest frequency refracted back at a given incidence angle; the critical angle is the highest takeoff angle that still returns — steeper punches through.
- Figure: `{{fig:ch03-muf-angles}}`.
- Vocabulary: MUF, LUF, critical frequency, critical angle, takeoff angle.

### Beat 4 — Hops, skip zones, and scatter (rest of G3B + G3C06–G3C09)
- One-hop distances: approximately 2,500 miles for F2 (G3B09), approximately 1,200 miles for E (G3B10). Long paths are multi-hop chains.
- The skip zone: the ring between ground-wave range and the first hop's landing. Scatter fills it: only a small fraction of the signal's energy scatters, so scatter signals are weak (G3C08), arrive over multiple paths, and sound distorted with a characteristic flutter (G3C06, G3C07); scatter is what lets you hear anything in the skip zone at all (G3C09).
- Path oddity: hearing your own signal return via the short path and the long path produces a slightly delayed echo (G3B01).
- Lower HF bands suffer high atmospheric static in summer (G3B12).
- Vocabulary: hop, skip distance, skip zone, scatter, multipath, fading, long path.

### Beat 5 — Special modes and checking conditions right now (G3C10 + G3B04)
- NVIS (near vertical incidence skywave): high-angle, short-distance MF/HF propagation — the emergency-communications workhorse for regional coverage (G3C10). Figure: `{{fig:ch03-nvis}}`. (The NVIS *antenna* heights live in ch09; here the propagation mode.)
- Gray line: Earth's day/night terminator — a window of enhanced low-band propagation around sunrise and sunset. Figure: `{{fig:ch03-grayline}}`. (Color from r5 §3: both ends of the path ride the terminator at once.)
- Checking conditions now: an internet network of automated receivers shows where your signal is being heard (G3B04 — reverse beacon network); the NCDXF/IARU beacons (14.100/18.110/21.150/24.930/28.200 MHz — cross-ref ch01) are the oldest "is the band open, and to where?" check; WSPR maps who heard whom (color only — exam content is ch08's).
- Vocabulary: NVIS, gray line, reverse beacon network, beacon (propagation).

## 5. Worked examples (at least one `> **Worked example:**` blockquote)

- Band choice from the MUF: "Today's MUF between your station and Europe is 21 MHz. The rule 'operate just below the MUF' picks 17 m (18.068–18.168 MHz) as the least-attenuated path; 15 m sits right at the edge and will flutter, while 20 m (14 MHz) works but absorbs more on every hop. λ(m) = 300 / f(MHz) — Book 2's shortcut — is how you sanity-check which band a frequency belongs to."
